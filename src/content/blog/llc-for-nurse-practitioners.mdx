## Do Nurse Practitioners Need an LLC?

No state forces a 1099 NP to form an entity, but most NPs running a **nurse practitioner private practice** or a meaningful contracting business should consider one. Here's what a properly formed and maintained entity does:

- **Separates business obligations from personal assets.** If your clinic signs a lease, finances an ultrasound machine, or gets sued over an unpaid vendor bill, creditors generally can't reach your home or retirement savings for those business debts.
- **Limits exposure to other people's mistakes.** In most states, a PLLC protects you from personal liability for a co-owner's or employee's malpractice. It does not protect you from your own.
- **Creates tax flexibility.** You can start as a pass-through entity and later elect S corporation status once profits justify the extra payroll and accounting work.
- **Makes contracting cleaner.** Many staffing agencies, telehealth platforms, and hospitals prefer (or require) contracting with an entity that has its own EIN and NPI.

Here's the caveat I give every clinician. An LLC is not malpractice insurance. If you personally misread a lab result or miss a drug interaction, you can be sued and held liable no matter what entity you use. I've seen too many new practice owners treat the LLC as a force field and then buy the cheapest claims-made policy they can find. The entity protects the *business* side of your risk; insurance protects the *clinical* side. You need both.

## PLLC vs. LLC: What Your State Allows

Nursing is a licensed profession, so your state's professional entity laws apply. States generally fall into three groups:

- **PLLC required or available.** Many states have a professional LLC statute. A **PLLC for nurse practitioners** typically requires every member to hold a license in the profession (or a related licensed profession the statute allows), and the name usually must include "PLLC," "P.L.L.C.," or "Professional Limited Liability Company." New York, Texas, Michigan, and North Carolina are common examples.
- **Standard LLC allowed.** Some states let licensed professionals use an ordinary LLC, sometimes with notice to the licensing board.
- **LLC not allowed for licensed professions.** California is the big one. California does not permit LLCs to provide professional services that require a state license. NPs there typically form a professional corporation (often called a nursing corporation) instead. Getting this wrong means your filing may be rejected or, worse, accepted but noncompliant.

Before filing, check two sources: your **Secretary of State** (for which entity types exist) and your **Board of Nursing** (for any registration, naming, or ownership rules). Some boards require a certificate of registration for the professional entity in addition to your individual license.

### Practice Authority Changes the Picture

Entity law is only half the analysis. The other half is **scope of practice**. According to the [American Association of Nurse Practitioners' state practice environment map](https://www.aanp.org/advocacy/state/state-practice-environment), roughly half the states plus D.C. grant full practice authority, while the rest impose reduced or restricted practice that requires a collaborative or supervisory agreement with a physician.

That matters for your business in practical ways:

- **Full practice authority states** (for example, Arizona, Colorado, Washington, and Maine) generally let you own and operate an **independent nurse practitioner business** without physician involvement.
- **Reduced or restricted practice states** may require a written collaborative agreement, chart review, or physician supervision. That physician is often a paid contractor, which adds anywhere from a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars a month to your overhead.
- **Corporate practice of medicine (CPOM) rules** in some states limit who can own a business that delivers medical services. Depending on how your state treats NP services, a nonlicensed partner (say, a spouse or investor) may not be able to own equity in your PLLC.

Practice-authority laws have been moving fast. Several states have expanded NP autonomy in recent legislative sessions, and California's AB 890 created new pathways for NPs to practice outside standardized procedures after meeting transition-to-practice requirements. Check your state's current rules in 2026 rather than relying on an article from even two years ago. ## How an LLC for Nurse Practitioners Is Taxed

By default, a single-member LLC is a "disregarded entity." Your practice income flows onto Schedule C of your personal return, and you pay both income tax and self-employment tax on the net profit. A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default.

### Self-Employment Tax

Self-employment tax is 15.3% (12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare) on net earnings, with the Social Security portion capped at the 2026 wage base of $184,500. For a successful NP, that's the line item that hurts most.

Example: an NP netting $150,000 from a 1099 contract owes roughly $21,000 in self-employment tax on top of income tax. That's before any S corp planning.

### The S Corporation Election

Once profit is consistently strong, many NPs elect to have the LLC taxed as an S corporation by filing Form 2553. You then pay yourself a reasonable W-2 salary and take the rest as distributions, which aren't subject to payroll taxes. The [IRS S corporation guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/s-corporations) explains the mechanics.

Here's a simplified illustration (not a projection for your situation):

| Scenario | Default LLC | LLC Taxed as S Corp |
|---|---|---|
| Net practice profit | $150,000 | $150,000 |
| W-2 salary | N/A | $95,000 |
| Distributions | N/A | $55,000 |
| SE / payroll tax (approx.) | ~$21,200 | ~$14,500 |
| Extra payroll + CPA costs | $0 | ~$2,000–$3,500 |
| Approximate net savings | | ~$3,000–$4,700 |

The key word is *reasonable*. The [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/nurse-anesthetists-nurse-midwives-and-nurse-practitioners.htm) reports median NP pay in the low-to-mid $100,000s, and the IRS can recharacterize distributions as wages if your salary is unrealistically low for your role and hours. Setting a $40,000 salary for full-time clinical work is an audit flag, not a strategy. See our guide to [reasonable salary for S corp LLC owners](/blog/reasonable-salary-for-s-corp-llc-owners/) and the [LLC vs S-Corp breakdown](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) before you file.

As a rough rule, the election starts making sense somewhere around $70,000–$90,000 in consistent net profit. Below that, payroll and bookkeeping costs often eat the savings.

### QBI Deduction

Health care is a "specified service trade or business" for the Section 199A qualified business income deduction. You can still claim the 20% deduction if your taxable income is below the threshold (roughly $200,000 single or $400,000 married filing jointly in 2026, with a phase-out above that). High-earning NP couples often phase out entirely. Talk to a CPA, because the interaction between S corp salary and QBI is easy to get wrong.

*Tax rules change and depend on your full financial picture. Consult a qualified tax professional before electing S corp status.*

## Step-by-Step: Setting Up Your NP Practice Entity

Here's the order I recommend, because doing these out of sequence creates credentialing delays that cost real money.

1. **Confirm entity type and ownership rules** with your Secretary of State and Board of Nursing. Decide between PLLC, LLC, or professional corporation.
2. **Choose a compliant name.** Include required designators ("PLLC"), and avoid words like "Medical" or "Physician" if your state restricts them for non-physician entities.
3. **Appoint a registered agent.** You can serve as your own if you have a physical address in the state and are available during business hours, but many NPs don't want their home address on public records. A service costs roughly $100–$300 a year. See [what a registered agent does](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/).
4. **File formation documents** (Articles of Organization or Certificate of Formation) and pay the state fee, which ranges from about $50 to $500.
5. **Draft an operating agreement.** Even single-member practices should have one. It should address what happens if you lose your license, become disabled, or want to bring in a partner. Our [LLC operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/) covers what to include.
6. **Get an EIN** from the IRS. It's free and takes about 10 minutes online.
7. **Apply for a Type 2 (organizational) NPI** through [NPPES](https://nppes.cms.hhs.gov/). Your individual Type 1 NPI stays with you; the Type 2 identifies the practice.
8. **Open a business bank account** and keep practice money completely separate.
9. **Credential with payers.** Update your CAQH profile, enroll the group in Medicare through PECOS, reassign your individual billing rights to the entity, and apply to commercial payers. Budget 90 to 180 days.
10. **Secure malpractice and general liability coverage** in the entity's name, plus a DEA registration and any state controlled-substance registration if you'll prescribe.

On the federal BOI report: under FinCEN's March 2025 interim final rule, U.S.-formed companies are currently exempt from beneficial ownership reporting. Rules have shifted several times, so check our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) for the latest.

## Common Mistakes NPs Make When Forming a Practice

- **Filing a standard LLC where a PLLC is required.** Some states accept the filing anyway, leaving you with an entity that can't legally provide professional services.
- **Forming the entity after credentialing starts.** Payers credential the entity by EIN and Type 2 NPI. Changing them midstream can restart the clock.
- **Bringing in an unlicensed co-owner.** In PLLC and CPOM states, a nonlicensed spouse or investor holding equity can invalidate the structure.
- **Ignoring the collaborative agreement.** In reduced-practice states, your practice can't operate legally without a compliant agreement. Get it in writing and review it with counsel.
- **Mixing personal and practice funds.** Commingling is the fastest way to let a creditor "pierce the veil" and reach personal assets.
- **Skipping tail coverage.** If you leave an employer with a claims-made malpractice policy, make sure you have tail or prior-acts coverage before you go independent.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a nurse practitioner own an LLC?

Yes, in most states. Depending on your state, you may need a PLLC or professional corporation instead of a standard LLC, and some reduced-practice states also require a physician collaborative agreement. California doesn't allow LLCs for licensed professional services, so NPs there generally form a nursing corporation.

### How much does it cost to form an LLC for nurse practitioners?

State filing fees range from about $50 to $500. Add $0–$349 for a formation service, $100–$300 a year for a registered agent, and possibly a Board of Nursing registration fee. Credentialing, malpractice insurance, and DEA registration ($888 for three years) are separate costs.

### Should a 1099 nurse practitioner form an LLC?

Often, yes. A 1099 NP working through staffing agencies or telehealth platforms gets cleaner contracting, liability separation for business debts, and the option to elect S corp status once income is high enough. It won't protect you from your own malpractice, so carry your own policy.

### Does an LLC protect a nurse practitioner from malpractice lawsuits?

Not from your own professional negligence. A PLLC generally protects you from a co-owner's or employee's malpractice and from general business debts, but you remain personally liable for care you provide. Malpractice insurance is essential.

### When should a nurse practitioner elect S corp status?

Many NPs consider it once net profit consistently exceeds roughly $70,000–$90,000. Below that, payroll and accounting costs usually cancel out the self-employment tax savings. Your salary must be reasonable for NP work, so consult a CPA before filing Form 2553.

### Do I need a separate NPI for my nurse practitioner LLC?

Yes, if you'll bill insurance under the practice. Apply for a Type 2 organizational NPI through NPPES. It's free, and you'll keep your individual Type 1 NPI as well.

### Can a nurse practitioner and a physician co-own a PLLC?

Some states allow multi-disciplinary professional entities that include both physicians and NPs; others restrict membership to one profession. Check your state's professional entity statute and corporate practice of medicine rules before drafting ownership terms.
