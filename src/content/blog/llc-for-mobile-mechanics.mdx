Mobile mechanics have one of the best business models in the trades: low overhead, loyal customers, and no garage lease. But working out of a van in someone's driveway also means you carry your tools, your inventory, and your liability everywhere you go. Setting up an LLC for mobile mechanics is one of the most practical steps you can take to separate your personal finances from the risks of working on other people's vehicles.

If you're ready to file, a formation service like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) starts at $0 plus state fees for its basic package, and most mobile mechanics can get an LLC filed in a few business days. [LegalZoom](/go/legalzoom/) is another well-known option if you prefer a more traditional, hand-holding process. We'll compare them below, but first, let's cover why an LLC matters in this trade and what it does (and doesn't) protect.

## Why Mobile Mechanics Need an LLC in 2026

When you fix a car in a shop, the shop's insurance and entity structure absorb a lot of risk. When you work independently as a sole proprietor, every claim lands on you personally: your savings, your truck, and in some cases your home.

Consider the realistic scenarios:

- **A brake job goes wrong.** A customer's car is involved in an accident and their attorney names you in the lawsuit. Without an entity, a judgment can reach personal assets.
- **Property damage on site.** A jack slips in a customer's garage, or fluid stains a driveway or HOA-managed property.
- **A vehicle gets damaged in your care.** Cars left with you for a part pickup or a day-long repair create bailment-style exposure.
- **Unpaid supplier or vendor bills.** If your parts account falls behind, an LLC can help keep the creditor focused on the business rather than your personal accounts.

An LLC (limited liability company) creates a legal separation between you and the business. For a broader primer, see our guide on [what an LLC is](/learn/what-is-an-llc/). If you're still weighing whether to bother at all, our breakdown of [LLC vs sole proprietorship](/learn/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/) covers the tradeoffs.

In my experience, the mechanics who regret not forming an LLC are rarely the ones who got sued on day one. They're the ones who grew to $150,000 or $200,000 in annual revenue, accumulated $25,000 in tools and equipment, and *then* realized they had a lot to lose with no structure in place. The LLC costs far less than the problem it prevents.

### What an LLC Does Not Do

Be honest with yourself about the limits:

- It does **not** protect you from your own negligence in a way that stops a claim from being filed. You can still be personally named if you personally caused the harm.
- It does **not** replace insurance. Courts and plaintiffs' attorneys look at coverage first. An LLC and insurance work together.
- Liability protection can be weakened if you mix personal and business money, which is called "piercing the corporate veil." Keep a separate business bank account and good records. Our guide on [opening an LLC bank account](/blog/llc-bank-account-how-to-open-one/) walks through that step.

## Licenses, Permits, and Registrations for a Mobile Mechanic

Rules differ widely by state and city, so treat this as a checklist of things to research rather than a universal list. A typical **mobile mechanic business license** setup might involve:

1. **State or local business license.** Many cities require a general business license even for home-based or vehicle-based operations.
2. **Automotive repair registration.** Some states require registration or licensing for auto repair businesses. California, for example, requires registration with the Bureau of Automotive Repair for most repair dealers. Verify your own state's rules.
3. **Sales tax permit.** If your state taxes parts (and many do), you'll need a seller's permit to collect and remit sales tax on parts you sell.
4. **EPA Section 609 certification.** Federal rules require technicians who service motor vehicle air conditioning to be certified. The [EPA's Section 609 information](https://www.epa.gov/section608) is a good starting point, and the test is inexpensive.
5. **Hazardous waste handling.** Used oil, antifreeze, and batteries need to be disposed of properly. Many states and local authorities regulate this, even for small operators.
6. **Zoning and parking.** Some municipalities restrict curbside repairs or working in residential driveways. Check local ordinances before you build a business around a specific service area.
7. **Employer Identification Number (EIN).** You'll need this to open a business bank account and hire help. It's free directly from the IRS, as explained in our guide to [getting an EIN for free](/blog/how-to-get-an-ein-for-your-llc-free-irs-method/).

Your state's Secretary of State website and local city or county office are the authoritative sources. Do not rely on a formation service to tell you which trade-specific licenses apply to you; they file your LLC, not your repair permits.

## Mobile Mechanic Insurance: What to Carry Alongside Your LLC

Insurance is where many new mobile mechanics get caught. Standard personal auto insurance generally won't cover a work van used for commercial purposes. Typical **mobile mechanic insurance** coverage includes:

| Coverage | What It Does | Typical Annual Cost Range* |
|---|---|---|
| General liability | Covers third-party injury and property damage | $600–$1,800 |
| Garagekeepers liability | Covers customer vehicles in your care or custody | $500–$2,000 |
| Commercial auto | Covers your work vehicle | $1,500–$4,500 |
| Tools and equipment (inland marine) | Covers theft or damage to tools | $200–$800 |
| Workers' compensation | Required in most states once you have employees | Varies by payroll and state |

*Ranges are rough estimates for illustration and vary widely by state, claims history, and carrier. Get actual quotes.

Garagekeepers coverage is the one most often overlooked. A standard general liability policy typically excludes damage to customer vehicles you're working on, which is the most common claim in your trade. Ask any agent directly whether the policy covers damage to the vehicle you are repairing.

## How an LLC Is Taxed for Mobile Mechanics

By default, a single-member LLC is a "disregarded entity" for federal tax purposes. You report profit and loss on Schedule C of your personal return, just like a sole proprietor. A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default. The IRS explains these classifications on its [LLC page at IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc).

### Self-Employment Tax

As a default-taxed LLC owner, your net profit is generally subject to self-employment tax of 15.3% (covering Social Security and Medicare) on the first portion of earnings, up to the annual Social Security wage base. This is the same as a sole proprietor. For detail, read our guide to [self-employment tax for LLC owners](/blog/self-employment-tax-for-llc-owners/).

### When an S-Corp Election Might Make Sense

Once your net profit is consistently strong, electing S-corporation tax treatment can reduce self-employment tax, because you pay yourself a "reasonable salary" and take remaining profit as distributions. Illustrative example, not a recommendation:

- Net profit: $110,000
- Reasonable salary (hypothetical): $65,000
- Distributions: $45,000
- Payroll tax is generally applied to the salary rather than the full $110,000

An S-corp election adds costs: payroll processing, a separate business tax return (Form 1120-S), and in some states additional fees or franchise taxes. Many advisors suggest it only becomes worthwhile at higher profit levels, but that threshold depends on your state and situation. Read our [LLC vs S-Corp comparison](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) and speak with a CPA before electing.

### Deductions Common to Mobile Mechanics

Mobile mechanics often have meaningful deductible expenses. Examples to discuss with your tax professional:

- Tools and equipment (possibly subject to depreciation rules or Section 179 expensing)
- Vehicle expenses, using either actual costs or the IRS standard mileage rate
- Parts and supplies
- Insurance premiums
- Licensing and certification fees
- Software, scheduling, and invoicing tools
- Phone and internet used for business
- Uniforms and protective equipment

Keep receipts and a mileage log. Mixed personal and business use of your van is a common audit trigger, so documentation matters.

### Quarterly Estimated Taxes

With no employer withholding, you generally must make quarterly estimated payments. Our [LLC quarterly tax payments guide](/blog/llc-quarterly-tax-payments-guide/) explains the schedule. A simple habit that works well: transfer 25 to 30 percent of each payment into a separate tax savings account, then adjust with your CPA.

For most mobile mechanics, the right state to form your LLC is the one where you actually operate. Forming in a different state, such as Delaware or Wyoming, usually adds cost without benefit because you'll still need to register as a foreign LLC where you do business. ## Step-by-Step: Forming Your Mobile Mechanic LLC

1. **Pick a name.** Check availability in your state's business database and make sure it isn't confusingly similar to another repair business. Include "LLC" if your state requires it.
2. **Choose a registered agent.** This is a person or company with a physical address in your state that accepts legal papers. You can serve as your own, but your address becomes public.
3. **File Articles of Organization.** This is the document that creates your LLC. See [what articles of organization are](/blog/what-are-articles-of-organization/).
4. **Create an operating agreement.** Even for a single-member LLC, this document supports your liability protection. Our [LLC operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/) explains what to include.
5. **Get an EIN.** Free from the IRS.
6. **Open a business bank account.** Keep every dollar of business income and expense separate.
7. **Obtain licenses and insurance.** Add the trade-specific items covered above.
8. **Check your BOI reporting status.** Beneficial ownership reporting rules have changed several times, so confirm the current requirements. See our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) for the latest.
9. **Calendar your annual obligations.** Many states require annual reports and fees. Missing them can lead to administrative dissolution.

## Common Mistakes Mobile Mechanics Make With Their LLC

- **Commingling funds.** Paying for groceries from the business account, or the reverse, undermines protection.
- **Skipping insurance.** An LLC does not cover a claim for faulty repairs by itself.
- **Not signing in the company name.** Sign invoices and contracts as "Your Name, Member/Owner of ABC Mobile Repair LLC." Personal guarantees on credit accounts and leases still create personal exposure.
- **Forgetting renewals.** Late annual reports can result in penalties or loss of good standing.
- **Ignoring waivers.** Written estimates, authorization forms, and clear disclaimers about pre-existing conditions protect you in disputes. Have a local attorney review your standard paperwork.
- **Misusing the S-corp election.** Choosing it too early can cost more than it saves.

I've seen too many small trade owners spend money on a fancy logo and van wrap while leaving the legal and insurance basics undone. Sequence it correctly: entity, insurance, bank account, then branding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do mobile mechanics need an LLC?

There is no universal legal requirement, but most advisors recommend one for mobile mechanics because the work involves safety-critical repairs, customer property, and on-site risk. An LLC combined with proper insurance is a common baseline.

### How much does it cost to start an LLC for a mobile mechanic business?

Expect your state filing fee (commonly $50 to $500), plus optional registered agent and formation service costs. Some services, including [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/), advertise $0 base packages plus state fees, though add-ons increase the total. Annual report or franchise tax costs vary by state.

### Do I need a business license to be a mobile mechanic?

In most places, yes. A **mobile mechanic business license** may be required at the city, county, or state level, and some states have separate auto repair registration. Check with your Secretary of State and local government.

### What insurance does a mobile mechanic need?

Commonly general liability, garagekeepers coverage, commercial auto, and tools coverage, plus workers' compensation if you have employees. Ask an independent insurance agent to confirm what fits your operation.

### Does an LLC protect me if I make a mistake on a repair?

It can limit the claim to business assets in many situations, but it does not shield you from liability for your own personal negligence in every case, and it does not replace insurance. Treat it as one layer of protection.

### Should I choose an LLC or stay a sole proprietor?

A sole proprietorship is simpler and cheaper to start, but offers no liability separation. Most mobile mechanics who work on customer vehicles benefit from the LLC. Compare the details in our [LLC vs sole proprietorship guide](/learn/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/).

### Do I need to file a BOI report for my mobile mechanic LLC?

Federal beneficial ownership reporting requirements have been revised multiple times, and your obligations may depend on current FinCEN rules and your entity's circumstances. Check [FinCEN's official site](https://www.fincen.gov/boi) and our [BOI report deadline guide](/blog/boi-report-deadline-2026/) for current status.

### Can I form my LLC in a different state than where I work?

You can, but you'd generally also need to register as a foreign LLC in your home state, which means paying two sets of fees. Most mobile mechanics are better off forming in their home state.
