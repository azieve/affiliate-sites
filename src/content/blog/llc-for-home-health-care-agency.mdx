## Home Health vs. Home Care: Why the Distinction Shapes Your LLC

"Home health care agency" covers two different businesses, and the difference drives your costs and structure.

- **Non-medical home care (personal care or companion care):** Bathing, meal prep, transportation, light housekeeping and companionship. Licensing varies a lot by state. Some states require a home care license and others require none. Clients usually pay privately or through long-term care insurance or Medicaid waiver programs.
- **Skilled home health:** Nursing, physical or occupational therapy, speech therapy and medical social work, ordered by a physician. To bill Medicare you need state licensure where required, Medicare certification, and typically accreditation or a state survey.

The LLC works for either. But a skilled agency aiming at Medicare faces far higher start-up capital and compliance than a private-pay companion care business. I've seen too many founders form the LLC, spend a few thousand dollars on branding, and only then learn the Medicare enrollment timeline runs many months and requires substantial reserve funds. Decide which business you're building before you spend money on anything else.

## Why an LLC Fits a Home Health Care Agency

A home health care LLC addresses three practical problems.

**1. Liability separation.** Your caregivers work unsupervised in clients' homes. Falls, medication mistakes, alleged theft and abuse claims are the realities of the industry. An LLC generally keeps a judgment against the business from reaching your house or personal savings. Two caveats:

- It doesn't protect you from your *own* negligence or from personal guarantees you sign on leases, loans or lines of credit.
- Courts can "pierce the veil" if you mix personal and business money or ignore formalities.

**2. A clean contracting entity.** Medicare and Medicaid enrollment, managed care contracts, referral-source agreements and employee payroll all need a legal entity with its own EIN. Forming the LLC before you apply keeps your enrollment records consistent.

**3. Flexibility on taxes and ownership.** Default LLC taxation is simple, and you can later elect S-corporation treatment once profits justify it (more below). You can also add investors or partners through the operating agreement.

An LLC is not a substitute for insurance. Lenders, referral partners and state regulators will expect you to carry coverage regardless. ## Licensing, Medicare and Capitalization: What Happens After You Form the LLC

Your LLC is a container. The licenses go inside it. A general sequence for a **skilled home health** agency looks like this:

1. **Form the LLC** in the state where you'll operate (see state notes below).
2. **Get an EIN** from the IRS, which is free through [IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/get-an-employer-identification-number).
3. **Obtain your NPI** (National Provider Identifier) for the entity.
4. **Apply for a state home health license**, where your state requires one. Some states also require a Certificate of Need or have moratoria on new agencies.
5. **Meet Medicare capitalization requirements.** Under [42 CFR 489.28](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-B/part-489), new home health agencies must show initial reserve operating funds. Plan on a six-figure cushion, and confirm the current formula with your Medicare Administrative Contractor.
6. **Enroll with Medicare** (CMS-855A) and complete the required survey or accreditation, such as from The Joint Commission, ACHC or CHAP. Start with [CMS.gov](https://www.cms.gov) for current enrollment rules.
7. **Obtain surety bonds and insurance** as required by the program and your state.

A few gotchas worth knowing:

- **The 36-month rule.** Medicare generally applies special rules when a home health agency's majority ownership changes within 36 months of enrollment or a prior change. Selling or restructuring early can force a new enrollment. Get your ownership structure right in the operating agreement from day one.
- **Ownership disclosure.** Medicare requires disclosure of owners and managing employees. Your LLC's member and manager details must match what you report to CMS and your state.
- **Exclusion screening.** Hiring or contracting with anyone excluded from federal health programs can trigger penalties. Check the HHS-OIG exclusion list before hiring, and monthly after that.

Requirements differ by state and change often. Treat this as a map, not a checklist, and confirm each step with your state health department and a healthcare regulatory attorney.

## Choosing Your State and Entity Details

Most agencies should form the LLC in the state where they'll provide care. Forming in Delaware or Wyoming and then registering as a foreign LLC in your operating state adds a second set of fees without a real benefit for a local, in-person business. State licensing agencies also tend to want the entity to be in good standing where care is delivered.

Typical annual costs vary widely. California's $800 minimum franchise tax is the best-known example, while Texas and Florida have different fee structures. See our state-specific guides for [Texas](/start-llc/texas/), [Florida](/start-llc/florida/) and [California](/start-llc/california/).

**Do you need a PLLC?** Professional LLCs are generally for licensed professionals such as physicians, dentists or therapists practicing together. A home health agency is typically a business entity that *employs* licensed clinicians, so a standard LLC is common. Rules vary by state, and if you plan to provide medical services directly through physician ownership, review our guide on an [LLC for a medical practice](/blog/llc-for-medical-practice/) and ask a healthcare attorney.

**Member-managed or manager-managed?** If you have outside investors or a clinical director with equity, manager-managed usually keeps governance cleaner. Whatever you choose, write a real [operating agreement](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/) that covers:

- Ownership percentages and capital contributions
- Decision authority, including who signs payer contracts
- What happens if a member is excluded from federal programs or loses a license
- Buy-sell terms, with the 36-month rule in mind

## Taxes for a Home Health Care LLC in 2026

A single-member LLC is taxed as a "disregarded entity" by default, and a multi-member LLC as a partnership. Per the [IRS guidance on LLCs](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc), you can also elect corporate or S-corp treatment.

**Self-employment tax.** As a default-taxed owner, you generally pay self-employment tax of about 15.3% on net earnings up to the Social Security wage base, then 2.9% above it. Check the IRS for the 2026 wage base.

**S-corp election.** Once your agency is solidly profitable, an S-corp election may reduce self-employment tax. You'd pay yourself a "reasonable salary" through payroll and take remaining profit as distributions. Illustrative example, not a projection: if your agency nets $120,000 and a reasonable salary for your role is $70,000, the payroll tax applies to the salary rather than the full amount. The savings must be weighed against payroll costs, state taxes and added compliance. Read our comparison of [LLC vs S-corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) and talk to a CPA before electing.

**Payroll is the big item.** Most agencies are payroll-heavy. Classify caregivers correctly. Misclassifying W-2 employees as 1099 contractors is a common and expensive error in home care, and the Department of Labor and state agencies scrutinize it. Home care workers also have specific wage and overtime rules, so confirm current requirements with your state labor department.

**Deductions.** Typical deductible costs include insurance premiums, payroll, mileage between client homes, software, licensing and accreditation fees, and training. For a longer list, see our [LLC tax deductions guide](/blog/llc-tax-deductions-list/).

**Reporting.** BOI reporting rules for domestic companies have shifted significantly since 2025. Check the current FinCEN position in our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) before assuming you must file.

## Insurance and Risk Management Beyond the LLC

I tell every new agency owner the same thing: the LLC is your second line of defense, and insurance is your first. At minimum, price these:

- **Professional liability (malpractice)** for clinical services
- **General liability** for property damage and bodily injury
- **Workers' compensation**, required in most states once you have employees
- **Commercial auto and hired/non-owned auto** if staff drive for work
- **Employee dishonesty or fidelity bond** (often requested by clients and payers)
- **Cyber liability** because you hold protected health information (HIPAA)
- **Abuse and molestation coverage**, which many carriers treat separately

Budgets vary dramatically by state, services and headcount, so get quotes from a broker who specializes in home care.

## FAQ: LLC for Home Health Care Agency

### Do I need an LLC to start a home health care agency?

No law requires you to use an LLC, but most owners choose one for liability protection and a clean contracting entity. Operating as a sole proprietor leaves your personal assets exposed. Some states also set requirements on how licensed agencies are organized, so check with your state health department.

### How much does it cost to form an LLC for a home health care agency?

State filing fees typically range from about $35 to $500, depending on the state. Formation services like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) start at $0 plus state fees, with paid tiers that add features. The larger costs come from licensing, insurance and capitalization.

### Can an LLC be Medicare-certified as a home health agency?

Yes, an LLC can enroll in Medicare as a home health agency if it meets CMS conditions of participation, capitalization requirements and your state's licensing rules. Ownership must be disclosed accurately on the enrollment application.

### Should I choose a PLLC or a regular LLC for a home health agency?

Most agencies use a regular LLC because they employ clinicians rather than being a professional practice owned by licensed individuals. State rules vary, so ask a healthcare attorney, especially if physicians or other licensed professionals will be owners.

### Does an LLC protect me from lawsuits over caregiver mistakes?

An LLC can shield your personal assets from the business's liabilities, but it doesn't protect you from your own negligence or from personal guarantees. Insurance is essential alongside the LLC.

### What taxes does a home health care LLC pay?

By default, profits pass through to your personal return, and you pay income tax plus self-employment tax. You may later elect S-corp status. The agency also owes payroll taxes for employees, plus any state taxes. Consult a CPA for your situation in 2026.

### Can I sell my home health agency soon after starting it?

Be careful. Medicare's ownership-change rules, including the 36-month provisions, can limit or complicate early sales and require new enrollment. Plan your exit terms with an attorney.
