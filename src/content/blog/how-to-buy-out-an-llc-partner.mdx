Partnerships end for ordinary reasons: one owner wants to retire, another wants to start something new, or the two of you simply stop agreeing on where the business should go. When that happens, the cleanest exit is usually a negotiated buyout, where one owner (or the company itself) purchases the departing owner's membership interest. Knowing **how to buy out an LLC partner** the right way protects you from surprise tax bills, lingering personal liability, and the kind of disputes that end up in court.

Here is the short version. A buyout has five moving parts: check what your operating agreement already says, value the departing partner's interest, choose a payment structure, sign a written LLC buyout agreement, and update every record that still lists the old owner. This guide walks through each one. ## Step 1: Start With Your Operating Agreement

Before you talk numbers, read your operating agreement. In my experience, this is the step people skip, and it is the one that most often changes the whole deal. I've seen too many business owners negotiate for weeks only to discover that their agreement already contained a buy-sell formula, a right of first refusal, or a required notice period that governed everything.

Look for these provisions:

- **Buy-sell clause:** Specifies triggers (death, disability, divorce, voluntary exit) and often a valuation formula.
- **Right of first refusal:** Gives the company or remaining members the first chance to buy before an outsider can.
- **Transfer restrictions:** Many agreements require unanimous or majority consent to change membership.
- **Valuation method:** Some agreements fix a formula (such as a multiple of trailing earnings); others require an independent appraiser.
- **Payment terms:** Some specify installment payments rather than a lump sum.

If your LLC never had a written agreement, state default rules apply, and those defaults may not match what you and your partner expect. ## Step 2: Decide on the Deal Structure

There are two basic ways to buy out an LLC partner, and the difference matters for taxes.

**Cross-purchase:** You, as the remaining member, personally buy the departing partner's interest. You pay the money, and you own a larger share afterward.

**Redemption:** The LLC itself buys the interest using company funds. The departing member's share is retired, and the remaining members' percentages increase proportionally.

For a multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership, a redemption is generally governed by [Section 736 of the Internal Revenue Code](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/736), which splits payments into those treated as a purchase of the interest and those treated as other partnership payments. A cross-purchase is typically treated as a straightforward sale of a partnership interest. The right structure depends on your cash position, your tax basis, and whether the LLC owns appreciated assets. Your CPA should run both scenarios before you sign anything.

## Step 3: Value the Departing Partner's Interest

Valuation is where most negotiations get tense. There is no single "correct" number, only a defensible range. The common approaches are:

**Book value.** Take total assets minus liabilities from the balance sheet and multiply by the partner's ownership percentage. This is simple but often undervalues profitable service businesses, because goodwill and earning power are not on the balance sheet.

**Earnings multiple.** Apply a multiple to annual earnings (often adjusted EBITDA or seller's discretionary earnings). Small private companies commonly sell for low single-digit multiples, though the range varies widely by industry and size.

**Asset-based valuation.** Appraise equipment, real estate, inventory, and intangible assets at fair market value. This suits real estate holding LLCs and asset-heavy businesses.

**Independent appraisal.** A certified business appraiser provides a documented opinion. It costs money, but it creates a neutral number both sides can accept, and it can help support your tax positions.

### A Worked Example

Say two partners own a landscaping LLC 50/50. The business earns $400,000 in adjusted annual earnings, and the partners agree on a 3x multiple, so the enterprise value is $1.2 million. The departing partner's 50% share works out to $600,000 before adjustments.

Now the adjustments begin. Does the business carry $150,000 of debt? Then the equity value falls. Is the departing partner owed unpaid distributions or a capital account balance? Those change the final figure. Some buyers argue for a discount because the departing partner holds a non-controlling interest; others argue against one because the buyer is the only logical purchaser. These are negotiation points, not rules, which is why written documentation matters so much.

## Step 4: Choose a Payment Structure

Few remaining owners can write a $600,000 check. Fortunately, a buyout does not have to be paid in cash on day one. Common structures include:

- **Lump sum:** Simple and clean, but requires financing or substantial cash reserves.
- **Installment note:** The buyer pays a down payment (say 20%) and the balance over three to seven years with interest, documented in a promissory note.
- **Earn-out:** Part of the price depends on future company performance.
- **Bank or SBA financing:** The buyer borrows to fund the purchase. Lenders will want to see valuation support and the signed agreement.
- **Redemption funded by the LLC:** The company pays from cash flow over time.

If you are the departing partner accepting an installment note, protect yourself. Ask for a security interest in the membership interest or company assets, a personal guaranty from the buyer, acceleration clauses if payments are missed, and restrictions on the company selling major assets while you are still owed money. If you are the buyer, negotiate prepayment rights so you can pay off the note early without penalty.

## Step 5: Sign a Written LLC Buyout Agreement

A handshake deal is not enough. A proper LLC buyout agreement should cover:

1. **Parties and interest being sold:** Names, ownership percentage, and a clear statement that the interest is free of liens.
2. **Purchase price and payment schedule:** Including interest rate, due dates, and default remedies.
3. **Representations and warranties:** The seller confirms they own the interest and disclose known liabilities; the buyer confirms ability to pay.
4. **Release of claims:** Mutual releases so neither side can reopen the deal later.
5. **Debt and guaranty handling:** How personal guaranties on loans, leases, and credit cards are released or replaced.
6. **Non-compete and non-solicitation:** Enforceability varies by state, so have counsel review these terms.
7. **Confidentiality and non-disparagement:** Protects relationships with customers and employees.
8. **Transition services:** Whether the departing partner stays on for a period to hand off clients and knowledge.
9. **Governing law and dispute resolution:** Mediation or arbitration clauses can save significant legal costs.

I strongly recommend having a business attorney draft or review this document. ## Step 6: Understand the Tax Consequences

Taxes can quietly eat a large share of a buyout if you do not plan ahead. Here are the main issues, explained in plain terms.

**For the seller:** Gain or loss on selling a partnership interest is generally capital in nature, but amounts attributable to "hot assets" such as unrealized receivables and inventory can be taxed as ordinary income. The IRS explains partnership interest sales in [Publication 541](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p541). If the sale involves hot assets, the partnership or parties may need to file Form 8308 as well.

**For the buyer:** In a cross-purchase, your basis in the acquired interest is generally your purchase price. In a redemption, remaining members may not receive the same basis step-up, which is one reason the structure choice matters. A Section 754 election can let the partnership adjust the basis of its assets after certain transfers, and your CPA can tell you whether it is worth making.

**If you end up as the only owner:** When a two-member LLC becomes a single-member LLC, the tax classification typically changes from a partnership to a disregarded entity unless it elects otherwise. IRS Revenue Ruling 99-6 addresses this situation, and the partnership's final tax year, final Form 1065, and final Schedules K-1 all need to be handled correctly. Ask your CPA whether the LLC keeps its existing EIN or needs a new one. For background on how these classifications work, see [how LLCs are taxed](/blog/llc-taxes-how-are-llcs-taxed/) and our comparison of [single-member vs multi-member LLCs](/blog/single-member-llc-vs-multi-member-llc/).

**State taxes:** Some states impose their own taxes or filing requirements on the sale or on changes in ownership. Check with a local professional.

Tax law changes frequently, and 2026 is no exception, so confirm current rules with a qualified tax advisor before closing.

## Step 7: Update Records and Close Out the Departing Partner

Signing the agreement is not the finish line. Cleanup is where liability tends to linger. Work through this checklist:

- **Amend the operating agreement** to reflect the new ownership percentages and remove the departing member.
- **File state paperwork** if your state lists members or managers in public filings. Many states require an amendment or an updated annual report.
- **Update bank accounts:** Remove the departing partner as signer and update beneficial ownership information with your bank.
- **Replace personal guaranties** on loans, leases, vendor accounts, and credit lines.
- **Update licenses, permits, and insurance** that list the departing member.
- **Notify your registered agent** and confirm your contact details are current. If you need a refresher, read [what a registered agent does](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/).
- **Update contracts, websites, and marketing materials** that name the former partner.
- **Check federal beneficial ownership reporting rules.** FinCEN's requirements have changed repeatedly in recent years, so verify what applies to your company at [FinCEN.gov](https://www.fincen.gov/boi) before assuming anything.

If the departing member is being removed rather than leaving voluntarily, there may be additional legal requirements. Read [how to remove a member from an LLC](/blog/how-to-remove-a-member-from-an-llc/) for that scenario.

## Common Buyout Mistakes to Avoid

- **Skipping valuation support.** A number pulled from thin air invites disputes and tax scrutiny.
- **Forgetting about debt.** If the departing partner remains on a personal guaranty, they stay exposed to the business's risks.
- **Ignoring tax structure.** The same headline price can produce very different after-tax outcomes.
- **Relying on verbal agreements.** Courts and the IRS both look to the documents.
- **Waiting too long.** The longer a disagreement sits, the more expensive the exit tends to be.

Market conditions in 2026 also matter. Higher borrowing costs than a few years ago mean financed buyouts can be more expensive, so seller-financed installment notes have remained a popular option for small businesses.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to buy out an LLC partner?

The price depends on the value of the partner's ownership interest, which comes from your valuation method, the company's debts, and any negotiated adjustments. On top of the purchase price, budget for attorney fees, possible appraisal costs, CPA fees, and state filing fees. Costs vary significantly by deal size and location.

### Can I buy out an LLC partner without their consent?

Generally not. A voluntary buyout requires agreement from the selling member. However, your operating agreement may include a forced buyout or expulsion provision under certain conditions, and state law may provide other remedies. Speak with an attorney before taking any step to push a member out.

### How do you value an LLC for a buyout?

Common approaches include book value, an earnings multiple, an asset-based valuation, or an independent appraisal. Many owners use more than one method to create a negotiating range. If your operating agreement specifies a formula, that formula usually controls.

### Do I need a lawyer to buy out an LLC member?

It is not legally required in most cases, but it is strongly advisable. An attorney can draft the LLC buyout agreement, confirm that you comply with your operating agreement and state law, and protect you from hidden liabilities.

### What taxes apply when you buy out an LLC partner?

The seller usually reports capital gain or loss on the sale, with ordinary income treatment for certain "hot assets." The buyer's basis and the LLC's tax classification can also change. Because the rules are technical, consult a CPA or tax attorney before closing.

### What happens if my LLC partner and I cannot agree on a buyout price?

Check your operating agreement for mediation, arbitration, or appraisal procedures. If none exist, you may consider a neutral appraiser, mediation, or ultimately a court-supervised process such as judicial dissolution, which is usually costly and slow.

### Can the LLC pay for the buyout instead of me?

Yes. In a redemption, the LLC buys back the departing member's interest with company funds. This can be convenient for cash flow, but it has different tax consequences than a personal purchase, and the company must remain able to pay its debts afterward.

### What happens to the LLC after a member leaves?

If at least one member remains, the LLC generally continues. If you become the sole owner, it typically becomes a single-member LLC. You will need to update your agreement, state records, and tax filings accordingly.

## Final Thoughts
