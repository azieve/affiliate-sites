Can a nonprofit own an LLC? Yes. A nonprofit corporation, including a 501(c)(3) public charity or private foundation, can be the sole member of a limited liability company or one of several members. It's a common structure in 2026. Charities use it to hold donated real estate, run a café or thrift store, isolate a risky program, or partner with a for-profit developer.

The structure only works if it's set up correctly. How the LLC is taxed, who controls it, and what it does can protect the parent's tax-exempt status or quietly put it at risk. ## Can a Nonprofit Own an LLC? The Short Legal Answer

Every U.S. state's LLC statute lets an LLC have members that are entities, not just individuals, and that includes nonprofit corporations. Nothing in the Internal Revenue Code stops a 501(c)(3) from holding an LLC interest either. The IRS has formally addressed the arrangement. In [IRS Notice 2012-52](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-12-52.pdf), it confirmed that donations to a single-member LLC wholly owned by a U.S. 501(c)(3) are treated as donations to the charity itself.

So the answer to "can a nonprofit own an LLC" is a clear yes. The real questions are:

1. **How many members will the LLC have?** One (the nonprofit alone) or several (other charities, individuals, or for-profit companies)?
2. **How will the LLC be taxed?** As a disregarded entity, a partnership, or a corporation?
3. **What will the LLC actually do?** Is the activity related to the charity's exempt purpose, or is it a business?

Your answers decide the tax filings, the risk to exempt status, and what the operating agreement needs to say. If LLC basics are new to you, start with our [What Is an LLC?](/learn/what-is-an-llc/) primer.

## Why Nonprofits Form LLCs: 6 Common Reasons

In my experience, nonprofit boards rarely set out to create an LLC. They usually back into it when a specific asset or project feels too risky to keep on the main balance sheet. These are the patterns I see most often:

- **Holding donated real estate.** A donor gives the charity a rental duplex or a parcel of land. Putting it in an LLC keeps a slip-and-fall claim or an environmental problem on that property away from the charity's endowment and other programs.
- **Isolating a higher-risk program.** Youth sports leagues, adventure camps, food service, and medical clinics carry more liability than a grant-making office. A separate LLC with its own insurance limits the damage one lawsuit can do.
- **Running an earned-income venture.** Social enterprises such as a job-training bakery, a museum gift shop, or a consulting arm often sit in an LLC so their revenue, staff, and contracts stay separate.
- **Joint ventures with for-profits.** Affordable-housing deals, health system partnerships, and solar projects often pair a nonprofit with private investors in a multi-member LLC.
- **Accepting unusual gifts.** Closely held business interests, intellectual property, or cryptocurrency can be easier to receive, manage, and sell through a dedicated entity.
- **Branding or geographic expansion.** A national nonprofit might use separate LLCs for regional operations so local contracts and leases don't expose the whole organization.

None of these reasons changes the charity's mission. The LLC is a tool for containing risk and keeping the books clean.

## How the IRS Taxes a Nonprofit-Owned LLC

This is where most of the confusion sits. An LLC is a state-law entity, and the IRS taxes it under the "check-the-box" rules. A nonprofit-owned LLC falls into one of three tax categories.

### Option 1: Single-member LLC as a disregarded entity (the default)

If the nonprofit is the only member and the LLC doesn't elect otherwise, the IRS treats it as a **disregarded entity**. For federal tax purposes it doesn't exist separately. Its income, expenses, and assets are reported on the parent nonprofit's Form 990, and the parent lists it on Schedule R.

What that means in practice:

- The LLC does **not** file its own Form 1023 or need its own determination letter. It shares the parent's exemption.
- Donors can deduct gifts made to the LLC, consistent with Notice 2012-52.
- The LLC can still get its own EIN, and it needs one to run payroll or open a bank account. See our guide to [getting an EIN for free from the IRS](/blog/how-to-get-an-ein-for-your-llc-free-irs-method/).
- Any taxable business income the LLC earns is treated as the parent's income.

This is the most common structure. It works well for related activities and passive holdings like a property the charity itself uses.

### Option 2: Multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership

If the LLC has two or more members, such as two nonprofits or a nonprofit and an investor, it's taxed as a partnership by default. It files Form 1065, and each member receives a Schedule K-1. The nonprofit's share of income keeps its character. Related income stays exempt, while unrelated business income flows through to the nonprofit as taxable.

### Option 3: LLC that elects corporate taxation

An LLC can file Form 8832 to be taxed as a C corporation. The nonprofit then owns a **taxable subsidiary**. The subsidiary pays the 21% federal corporate rate on its own profits, and only dividends move up to the parent, which are generally excluded from UBIT.

Charities pick this route when a commercial venture is big enough that its income could make people question whether the nonprofit is still mainly charitable. A taxable subsidiary keeps that activity at arm's length.

| Structure | Federal tax filing | Shares parent's exemption? | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-member, disregarded | Included on parent's Form 990 | Yes | Related programs, property holding, liability isolation |
| Multi-member partnership | Form 1065 + K-1s | No, but income character flows through | Joint ventures, co-investments |
| LLC taxed as C corp | Form 1120 | No | Large commercial or unrelated businesses |

For a deeper look at how pass-through and corporate taxation differ, see [how LLCs are taxed](/blog/llc-taxes-how-are-llcs-taxed/).

## The UBIT Trap: Why "Disregarded" Doesn't Mean Tax-Free

The most expensive mistake I see is the belief that putting a business inside an LLC shields the nonprofit from tax. It doesn't. With a disregarded entity, the IRS looks straight through the LLC.

Under the [unrelated business income tax (UBIT) rules](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/unrelated-business-income-tax), income from a trade or business that is regularly carried on and not substantially related to the exempt purpose is taxable. If gross unrelated income reaches $1,000 or more, the nonprofit must file Form 990-T and pay tax at corporate rates.

Here's a worked example. A literacy nonprofit forms a single-member LLC to own a downtown building. It uses one floor for tutoring and leases two floors to a law firm.

- The tutoring floor is a related use, so there's no tax issue.
- Rent from the law firm is generally **excluded** from UBIT as rent from real property, *unless* the building is debt-financed. If the LLC took out a mortgage to buy it, part of that rent can become taxable "debt-financed income."
- If the LLC also runs a parking lot open to the public on weekdays, that revenue is likely unrelated business income.

The LLC changes none of this math. What it does is keep a tenant's lawsuit from reaching the charity's other assets. Separating liability and taxes isn't a loophole; it's how the structure is meant to work.

There's also a bigger concern than the tax bill. If unrelated activity becomes a substantial part of the overall operation, the IRS can question whether the organization still operates exclusively for exempt purposes. There's no bright-line percentage, so boards with growing commercial revenue should consider moving it into a taxable subsidiary before it becomes a problem. Talk to a tax professional who works with exempt organizations.

## Joint Ventures: When a Nonprofit Shares the LLC With Others

A multi-member LLC with a for-profit partner is the highest-risk version of this structure. The IRS has published guidance on it. In Revenue Ruling 98-15 (the "whole hospital" ruling) and Revenue Ruling 2004-51 (the "ancillary joint venture" ruling), the key test is **control**. The nonprofit must have enough authority over the venture to make sure it pursues charitable purposes ahead of private profit.

Practical guardrails for a nonprofit in a joint-venture LLC:

- **Charitable purpose clause.** The operating agreement should say that the charitable purpose overrides any duty to maximize profit, at least for the activities the nonprofit contributes.
- **Governance rights.** Board seats, veto power over key decisions, or reserved powers over the charitable function.
- **Arm's-length deals.** Management fees, leases, and loans to the for-profit partner at fair market value. Anything else risks **private benefit** or **private inurement**, both of which can cost a charity its exemption.
- **Exit rights.** A clear way out if the venture drifts from its purpose.

I've watched a small housing nonprofit sign a developer's standard LLC agreement almost unchanged. It gave the developer full management control and the nonprofit a token 1% interest. Untangling it two years later cost more in legal fees than the nonprofit had earned from the deal. ## Can an LLC Own a Nonprofit? (And What "Sole Member Nonprofit" Means)

Many people search the reverse question: can an LLC own a nonprofit? The short answer is **not in the ownership sense**. A nonprofit corporation has no shareholders and no equity. Nobody "owns" it the way you'd own shares in a company, and its assets are permanently dedicated to its purpose.

What an LLC *can* do, depending on state law:

- **Act as an incorporator** that files the nonprofit's articles of incorporation.
- **Serve as a member** of a membership nonprofit, if the state statute and the nonprofit's bylaws allow entity members. Membership gives voting rights, such as electing directors, but no claim on profits or assets.
- **Fund the nonprofit** through donations, which a business can generally deduct within IRS limits.

This leads to the term **sole member nonprofit**. A sole member nonprofit is a nonprofit corporation with exactly one member, usually another nonprofit. Health systems, universities, and religious bodies use this to control subsidiaries. The parent, as sole member, elects the subsidiary's board and approves major decisions. If the sole member were an LLC owned by individuals, the IRS would look very closely at private benefit, and any 501(c)(3) application would face hard questions. For a business owner who wants to support a cause, a donor-advised fund or a separately governed private foundation is usually the cleaner route.

## How to Set Up a Nonprofit-Owned LLC in 2026: Step by Step

Once the board has decided on the structure and purpose, the mechanics look a lot like forming any LLC.

1. **Get board approval.** Pass a resolution authorizing the LLC, naming who signs for the nonprofit, and stating the LLC's purpose. Some nonprofit bylaws also require member approval.
2. **Choose the state.** Usually the state where the property or activity is located. Forming in Delaware and then registering as a foreign LLC at home doubles your fees for little benefit. See our [Delaware LLC guide](/start-llc/delaware/) if you have a genuine multi-state reason.
3. **File the articles of organization.** List the nonprofit as the member (or manager). State fees in 2026 range from about $35 to $500. Massachusetts is at the top at $500, and Texas charges $300.
4. **Appoint a registered agent.** Use a professional agent if the LLC's address shouldn't be public or no one is reliably in the office during business hours. Our [registered agent explainer](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/) covers the options.
5. **Draft the operating agreement.** Include a purpose clause tied to the parent's exempt purposes, a ban on distributions to anyone other than the nonprofit member, and a dissolution clause sending assets back to the parent or another 501(c)(3).
6. **Get an EIN** and open a separate bank account. Never mix the LLC's funds with the parent's.
7. **Apply for state exemptions separately.** This is the step people miss. Federal disregarded-entity status does **not** automatically carry over to state property tax, sales tax, or charitable solicitation registration. Many states require the LLC to file its own application.
8. **Update insurance and reporting.** Add the LLC to the nonprofit's insurance program or buy separate coverage, and list it on Schedule R of the next Form 990. Beneficial ownership reporting rules have changed several times, so check our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) for the current position.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a 501(c)(3) be the sole member of an LLC?
Yes. A 501(c)(3) can be the only member of an LLC. By default, the IRS treats that LLC as a disregarded entity, so its activities are reported on the parent's Form 990 and it shares the parent's tax exemption.

### Does a nonprofit-owned LLC need its own 501(c)(3) status?
Not if it's a single-member LLC treated as a disregarded entity. It's covered by the parent's exemption. A multi-member LLC or one taxed as a corporation is a separate taxpayer and doesn't automatically share the parent's exemption.

### Are donations to a nonprofit-owned LLC tax-deductible?
Generally yes, if the LLC is wholly owned by a U.S. 501(c)(3) and treated as a disregarded entity. Under IRS Notice 2012-52, the gift is treated as made to the charity, and the acknowledgment letter should name the parent nonprofit.

### Does the LLC protect the nonprofit from lawsuits?
It can, if you respect the separation. Keep separate bank accounts, sign contracts in the LLC's name, carry adequate insurance, and keep the LLC adequately funded. Courts can disregard an LLC that is just a shell or whose funds are mixed with the parent's.

### Will the LLC's business income be taxed?
Income related to the charity's mission generally isn't taxed. Unrelated business income, such as running a commercial parking lot, is taxable, and the nonprofit must file Form 990-T if gross unrelated income reaches $1,000. Electing corporate taxation for the LLC is an option when that income is significant.

### Can an LLC own a nonprofit?
Not in the ownership sense. Nonprofits have no owners or shareholders. Depending on state law and the bylaws, an LLC may be able to act as an incorporator or a voting member of a membership nonprofit, but it can't hold equity or receive profits.

### What is a sole member nonprofit?
It's a nonprofit corporation with a single member, usually a parent nonprofit, that elects the board and approves major decisions. Hospital systems and universities often use this structure to control subsidiary nonprofits.

### How much does it cost to form an LLC owned by a nonprofit?
The state filing fee is usually $35 to $500, plus any annual report fees and registered agent costs. Formation services start at $0 plus state fees. The larger cost is usually attorney review of the operating agreement and tax structure, which can run from a few hundred to several thousand dollars.
