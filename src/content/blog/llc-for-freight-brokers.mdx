Freight brokers sit in one of the most exposed positions in the supply chain. You never touch the cargo, yet you sign the contract with the shipper, you select the carrier, and you move six or seven figures of other people's money through your accounts every year. When a load goes missing, a carrier gets double-brokered, or a shipper refuses to pay, the first name on the lawsuit is usually the broker's. That is why an LLC for freight brokers is not a formality you get around to later. It is the foundation that your FMCSA broker authority, your $75,000 BMC-84 bond, your carrier contracts, and your bank accounts are all built on.

The good news is that the entity itself is the easy part. A formation service like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) will file your Articles of Organization for $0 plus your state's filing fee, and most states approve a new LLC in a few business days. The hard part is sequencing: getting the LLC, the EIN, the USDOT number, the broker authority application, the bond, the BOC-3 process agents, and the Unified Carrier Registration done in the right order so that every federal record points at the company rather than at you personally.

This guide walks through exactly that sequence. It covers why brokers in particular need the liability shield, how the LLC interacts with FMCSA registration and the surety bond, how broker revenue is taxed, what it all costs in 2026, and which formation service makes sense for a one-person brokerage versus a growing office of agents.

## Why Freight Brokers Carry More Liability Than They Think

Most new brokers assume the carrier bears the risk because the carrier has the truck, the cargo insurance, and the driver. In practice, brokers get pulled into disputes constantly, for three reasons.

**Negligent selection claims.** If a carrier you hired causes a serious accident, the plaintiff's attorney will argue that you should have known the carrier was unsafe. Courts in several circuits have allowed negligent-hiring claims against brokers to proceed despite federal preemption arguments, and the question has been litigated all the way up to petitions at the U.S. Supreme Court in recent years. Whether or not those claims ultimately succeed, defending one costs tens of thousands of dollars.

**Cargo and payment disputes.** When a load is damaged, the shipper often pursues the broker first because the broker is the party they have a contract with. Separately, when a shipper goes bankrupt and does not pay, carriers may demand payment from the broker anyway, and in some states can pursue the shipper directly, which creates a three-way fight over the same invoice.

**Fraud exposure.** Double brokering and carrier identity theft have become the defining problem of the 2024 to 2026 freight market. If a fraudulent "carrier" books your load and disappears with the freight, the shipper looks to you. Industry groups like the [Transportation Intermediaries Association](https://www.tianet.org/) have estimated that freight fraud costs the industry hundreds of millions of dollars annually, and the FMCSA has responded with tighter registration and identity-verification requirements.

An LLC does not stop any of these claims from being filed. What it does is draw a legal line between the brokerage's assets and your house, your personal savings, and your retirement accounts. If the brokerage is sued and the claim exceeds your insurance, the plaintiff is generally limited to what the LLC owns. Without the entity, you are a sole proprietor, and every judgment against the business is a judgment against you personally. Our overview of [what an LLC is](/learn/what-is-an-llc/) covers the general mechanics, but in brokerage the stakes are unusually high because gross dollar volume is so large relative to the owner's actual margin.

In my experience, the brokers who regret skipping the LLC are not the ones who get sued over an accident. They are the ones whose shipper went under owing $140,000 across twenty loads, who then had carriers lined up demanding payment, and who discovered that every one of those carrier agreements had been signed in their own name.

## How the LLC Fits With FMCSA Broker Authority

Here is the sequencing most first-time brokers get wrong. FMCSA registration is issued to a legal entity. If you apply for broker authority before forming your LLC, that authority is issued to you as an individual, and transferring it later means filing a change-of-name or a new application, re-filing your bond, and updating every carrier packet. Form the LLC first.

The federal side of a freight brokerage, as laid out on the [FMCSA registration pages](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/registration/get-mc-number-authority-operate), has four pieces:

1. **USDOT number and broker operating authority (MC number).** Brokers of property apply through the Unified Registration System. The federal filing fee is $300 per authority type. The application is filed in the LLC's legal name with its EIN, so you need both before you start.

2. **BMC-84 surety bond or BMC-85 trust fund.** Federal law requires every property broker to maintain $75,000 in financial security. This has been the minimum since MAP-21 raised it from $10,000 in 2013. Nearly all small brokers use a BMC-84 bond, where a surety company guarantees the $75,000 and you pay an annual premium. The bond is issued to the LLC and filed electronically by the surety with FMCSA.

3. **BOC-3 process agent designation.** You must designate a process agent in every state where you do business. Almost every broker uses a blanket process-agent company, which files the BOC-3 for a one-time fee of roughly $20 to $50. This is a federal requirement and is completely separate from your state-level registered agent, which we cover below.

4. **Unified Carrier Registration (UCR).** Brokers register annually under the [UCR program](https://www.ucr.gov/). Brokers pay the lowest fee bracket, which has been under $50 per year recently, but the registration must be renewed every year or your authority can be revoked.

FMCSA will not grant your authority until the bond and BOC-3 are on file, and there is a mandatory 10-business-day protest period before the authority becomes active. In practice, plan on three to five weeks from forming the LLC to legally brokering your first load, assuming you have the bond lined up early.

### The 2026 bond rule changes every broker should know

Brokers forming an LLC in 2026 are operating under a stricter financial responsibility regime than brokers who started a few years ago. FMCSA's broker and freight forwarder financial responsibility final rule, published in late 2023 with phased compliance dates running into January 2026, tightened the $75,000 requirement in several practical ways:

- If a broker's available financial security falls below $75,000, for example because the surety has paid out carrier claims, the surety or trustee must notify FMCSA, and the broker's authority is subject to suspension if the shortfall is not cured within seven business days.
- BMC-85 trust funds are now limited to assets that are readily available, which closed a loophole where some trusts were backed by illiquid or questionable collateral.
- Sureties and trust providers that fail to pay valid claims face suspension from the program for three years.

The practical lesson for a new broker is that the bond is not a one-time checkbox. It is an ongoing solvency test. Pay carriers on time, because every claim against your bond chips away at the $75,000 and puts your authority at risk.

## Registered Agent vs. BOC-3 Process Agent: You Need Both

This is the most common point of confusion I see among brokers, and it is worth stating plainly: your LLC's registered agent and your FMCSA process agents are two different legal requirements that happen to sound alike.

Your **registered agent** is a state-law requirement for the LLC itself. Every state requires an LLC to designate a person or company with a physical address in that state to receive lawsuits and official state mail. If you miss a lawsuit because nobody was at the address, you can lose by default judgment. Our guide to [what a registered agent is](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/) covers the role in detail.

Your **BOC-3 process agents** are a federal requirement for the brokerage's operating authority. They cover service of process in every state, not just your home state, because brokers arrange shipments nationwide.

Formation services typically include a year of registered-agent service with their paid plans. ZenBusiness bundles it into its Pro tier and bills roughly $199 per year afterward, while [Northwest Registered Agent](/go/northwest/) is known for its registered-agent service at $125 per year with mail scanning included. Neither one files your BOC-3; that comes from a dedicated process-agent company, and your bond agent or FMCSA compliance service will usually point you to one.

## How Freight Broker Income Is Taxed Through an LLC

Brokerage is a high-gross, thin-margin business, and that shape has real tax consequences for how you structure the LLC.

**Gross versus net.** A one-person brokerage moving 15 loads a week at $2,200 average can gross well over $1.5 million a year while netting $180,000 to $250,000 after carrier payments and overhead. For tax purposes, your carrier payments are cost of goods sold, and only the net margin is income to you. But your state may still look at the gross number for certain purposes. Texas, for example, has no personal income tax but imposes a franchise tax on gross revenue above a no-tax-due threshold that has sat around $2.47 million in recent years. A brokerage that grows past that threshold on gross revenue will owe franchise tax even if its net profit is modest. If you are forming in Texas, our [Texas LLC guide](/start-llc/texas/) explains the filing and the franchise tax report.

**Default tax treatment.** A single-member LLC is a disregarded entity by default. All net profit flows onto your personal Schedule C and is subject to self-employment tax of 15.3 percent on the first roughly $176,000 of net earnings in 2026, plus income tax. A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default and files Form 1065.

**The S corporation election.** Once a brokerage is reliably netting around $80,000 or more, electing S corporation status often saves real money. You pay yourself a reasonable salary through payroll, and the remaining profit comes out as a distribution that is not subject to self-employment tax. On a $200,000 net profit with a $100,000 salary, the self-employment tax savings can approach $10,000 a year, offset by payroll service costs and a slightly more complex return. The election is made on IRS Form 2553, and we have a step-by-step guide to [filing Form 2553 for an LLC](/blog/how-to-file-form-2553-for-an-llc/). Our broader comparison of [LLC vs. S-Corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) covers the tradeoffs. Talk to a CPA before electing; the "reasonable salary" question is one the IRS scrutinizes, and a broker who pays themselves $30,000 while distributing $170,000 is inviting an audit.

**Carrier payments and 1099s.** Here is a nuance that surprises many new brokers. Payments for freight are generally exempt from Form 1099 reporting under Treasury regulations, so you typically do not issue 1099-NEC forms to your motor carriers for hauling loads. You do still issue 1099-NEC forms to independent sales agents, dispatchers, and other contractors you pay $600 or more. The [IRS small business pages on LLCs](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc) are the starting point for federal filing obligations, but given the dollar volumes involved, a CPA who understands transportation intermediaries is worth the fee.

**Factoring and cash flow.** Many brokers use factoring or quick-pay arrangements to pay carriers in days while waiting 30 to 60 days for shipper payment. Factoring fees are deductible business expenses, but they can eat two to four percent of gross, which on a thin margin business is significant. Keep those contracts and the bond in the LLC's name so the paper trail stays clean.

## Step-by-Step: Forming an LLC for a Freight Brokerage in 2026

Here is the order of operations that avoids re-filing anything.

**1. Choose your state.** For most brokers, that means your home state. Brokers are not required to be domiciled in any particular state, and forming in Delaware or Wyoming while operating from Georgia just means registering as a foreign LLC in Georgia and paying two sets of fees. The exception is a brokerage with partners in different states or outside investors, where a Delaware entity can simplify governance. Our [Delaware LLC guide](/start-llc/delaware/) covers when that makes sense.

**2. Pick a name and check it twice.** Check your state's business registry and also search the FMCSA registration database. Having an LLC name that is confusingly similar to an existing broker or carrier creates exactly the kind of identity confusion fraudsters exploit. Many brokers also want "Logistics" rather than "Trucking" in the name to make clear to shippers and carriers that they are an intermediary, not a motor carrier.

**3. File the Articles of Organization.** You can file directly with your Secretary of State, or use a formation service. State fees range from about $35 in Montana to $500 in Massachusetts, with Texas at $300 and Florida at $125. Our guide to [how much an LLC costs](/learn/how-much-does-an-llc-cost/) has the state-by-state table.

**4. Get your EIN.** Apply free at IRS.gov as soon as the LLC is approved. You will need it for the FMCSA application, the bond, and the bank account.

**5. Sign an operating agreement.** Even a single-member brokerage should have one. It is the document a bank, surety, or court looks at to confirm the LLC is a real, separately governed entity, and if you ever bring on an agent as a partner, you will want the ownership and profit-split terms already written down. See our [operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/).

**6. Open a dedicated business bank account.** This is non-negotiable in brokerage. Shipper payments and carrier payments must flow through an account in the LLC's name. Commingling brokerage funds with personal funds is the single fastest way to lose your liability protection in court, and it also makes bond claims and audits a nightmare.

**7. Secure the BMC-84 bond.** Apply with a surety agency that specializes in freight brokers. Annual premiums for a $75,000 BMC-84 run from roughly $900 to $2,000 for applicants with strong personal credit and can exceed $5,000 for applicants with weak credit, since the surety is underwriting your likelihood of not paying carriers. Some sureties require a personal guarantee from the owner, which is one place where the LLC shield does not help you.

**8. Apply for broker authority and file the BOC-3.** Apply through FMCSA's Unified Registration System in the LLC's name, pay the $300 fee, have your surety file the BMC-84, have your process agent file the BOC-3, and register with UCR.

**9. Get contingent cargo and general liability insurance.** Not federally required for brokers, but most shippers will not work with a broker who lacks contingent cargo coverage, typically $100,000 per load, and general liability of $1 million. Have policies issued to the LLC.

**10. Handle ongoing compliance.** Annual reports with your state, UCR renewal each year, bond renewal, and your state's franchise or gross receipts taxes. Note that the federal Beneficial Ownership Information reporting requirement was substantially narrowed in 2025 so that most domestic LLCs are no longer required to file; our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) tracks the current status.

## If You Also Run Trucks

Some brokers hold both broker authority and motor carrier authority, either because they started as owner-operators or because they want the flexibility to haul their own freight. FMCSA permits a single entity to hold both, but it requires strict separation: a broker cannot broker a load to its own trucks without disclosing that to the shipper, and the two activities carry different insurance and bond requirements. Many compliance attorneys recommend holding the two authorities in separate LLCs so that a cargo claim against the carrier side cannot reach the brokerage's receivables, and vice versa. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do freight brokers need an LLC to get FMCSA broker authority?

No. FMCSA will issue broker authority to a sole proprietor. But because the authority, the bond, and every carrier agreement are issued in the applicant's legal name, applying as an individual means all of those documents are tied to you personally, and converting to an LLC later requires re-filing. Forming the LLC first is strongly recommended.

### How much is the BMC-84 bond for a freight broker?

The bond amount is $75,000, but you do not pay that amount. You pay an annual premium to a surety company, typically $900 to $2,000 for applicants with good credit and considerably more for applicants with poor credit or prior bond claims. The surety pays valid carrier claims up to $75,000 and then seeks reimbursement from you.

### What is the difference between a registered agent and a BOC-3 process agent?

A registered agent is required by your state for the LLC itself and receives lawsuits and state mail at an address in your formation state. A BOC-3 process agent is required by FMCSA for your broker authority and covers service of process in all states where you arrange freight. Freight brokers need both, and they are usually provided by different companies.

### Should a freight broker LLC elect S corporation status?

Often yes, once net profit is consistently around $80,000 or higher. The S corporation election lets you split income between salary and distributions, reducing self-employment tax. Below that level, payroll costs and added complexity usually outweigh the savings. Have a CPA model your specific numbers before filing Form 2553.

### Can I run a freight brokerage from home as an LLC?

Yes, and many do. Your LLC still needs a registered agent with a physical address in your state, and your FMCSA registration will list a principal place of business. If you want to keep your home address off public records, use a registered agent service that allows its address on filings and consider a virtual business address for FMCSA.

### What happens to my LLC if a carrier files a claim against my bond?

The surety investigates and, if the claim is valid, pays the carrier from the $75,000 bond. The surety then demands reimbursement from your LLC, and from you personally if you signed a personal indemnity agreement, which most sureties require. Under the current FMCSA rules, if your available bond amount falls below $75,000 and is not restored within seven business days, your broker authority can be suspended.

### Do I need to send 1099s to the carriers I pay?

Generally no. Payments for freight are exempt from Form 1099 reporting under federal regulations. You do need to issue 1099-NEC forms to independent agents, dispatchers, and other non-employee service providers you pay $600 or more during the year. Confirm with your CPA, as state rules and your specific arrangements may differ.

### Is it better to have separate LLCs for brokerage and trucking?

If you hold both broker and motor carrier authority, most compliance professionals recommend separate LLCs. It keeps cargo and accident liability on the carrier side from reaching brokerage receivables, keeps the insurance and bond obligations distinct, and makes the required disclosure to shippers cleaner when you move a brokered load on your own trucks.

A freight brokerage is a business that moves enormous dollar volume on thin margins while standing contractually between two parties who may each try to hold you responsible when something goes wrong. An LLC for freight brokers puts a legal wall between that exposure and your personal finances, and it gives FMCSA, your surety, your bank, and your shippers a single clean entity to deal with. Form it first, run every dollar through it, keep the bond healthy, and revisit your tax election once the margin justifies it.
