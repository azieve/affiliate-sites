Most owners form an LLC to protect their personal assets and simplify taxes. Very few plan for the day an owner dies. Yet **what happens to an LLC when a member dies** is one of the most expensive questions to answer after the fact. The answer depends on three things: your operating agreement, your state's LLC statute, and whether the LLC has one owner or several.

The short version is that the LLC does not automatically disappear in most states. The deceased member's *economic* interest usually passes to their estate or heirs. ## The Short Answer: It Depends on Your Operating Agreement and State Law

An LLC is a legal entity separate from its owners. That separation is why the business can often survive an owner's death. But the details come from two sources, in this order:

1. **Your operating agreement.** If it addresses death, incapacity, and succession, courts generally follow it.
2. **Your state's default rules.** If the agreement is silent, or you never had one, the state statute fills the gap.

Many states have adopted some version of the Revised Uniform Limited Liability Company Act (RULLCA), published by the [Uniform Law Commission](https://www.uniformlaws.org/). Others, including Delaware, have their own statutes. Under the common default framework:

- Death causes the member to be **dissociated**. They are no longer a member with voting and management rights.
- The deceased member's **economic rights** (their share of profits and distributions) pass to their estate or heirs as a "transferee."
- The estate generally **does not** become a full member with a vote unless the remaining members admit them, or the operating agreement says so.
- In a multi-member LLC, the business typically **continues** with the surviving members.

Older statutes were harsher and could dissolve the LLC on a member's death unless the others voted to continue. A few states still lean that way, so check your own state's statute or ask a local attorney.

In my experience, the biggest surprise for families is that inheriting an LLC interest does not mean inheriting a seat at the table. I've seen heirs hold a 40% economic stake in a profitable company and have no vote on whether distributions get paid.

## What Happens When a Member of a Multi-Member LLC Dies

Take a three-member LLC where each member owns one-third. One member, Dana, dies.

**Default rules (no operating agreement provisions):**

- Dana is dissociated as of the date of death.
- Dana's estate holds her one-third economic interest. That means her share of distributions, but not her vote.
- The two surviving members keep managing the company.
- The estate usually has limited information rights, not the full management access Dana had.
- The surviving members have no automatic right to buy out the estate, and the estate has no automatic right to be bought out. This is the gap that causes disputes.

**With a good operating agreement:**

- A **buy-sell clause** may require or allow the surviving members (or the LLC) to purchase Dana's interest at a formula price or an appraised value.
- Funding may come from **life insurance**, paid out to the LLC or the other members, so the buyout does not drain operating cash.
- The agreement may let a named heir **step in as a member**, subject to conditions.

### A Worked Example

Suppose a two-member LLC valued at $800,000 has no buy-sell terms. Member A (60%) dies. Member B (40%) now has a company where 60% of the profits belong to Member A's estate, which is run by an executor and perhaps a spouse who knows nothing about the business. Member B cannot force a buyout, and the estate cannot easily force a sale. Distributions, salaries, and reinvestment all become negotiations with someone who may be grieving, inexperienced, or in conflict with Member B.

With a buy-sell agreement funded by a $480,000 life insurance policy on Member A, the same scenario is a clean transaction. The estate gets paid, and Member B owns 100%.

## What Happens When a Single-Member LLC Owner Dies

A **single-member LLC owner dies** is the scenario with the most legal risk, because there is no one left to run the company.

Under RULLCA-style statutes, an LLC with no members is generally dissolved unless, within a set period (commonly 90 days), the deceased member's legal representative or heirs agree to continue the LLC and are admitted as members. Delaware's statute works in a similar way, and you can read its framework in the [Delaware LLC Act](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title6/c018/). Other states have their own timelines and rules.

In practice, this means:

- **The business can freeze.** Bank accounts, contracts, and licenses are tied to the owner. Without someone with authority, bills go unpaid.
- **The personal representative (executor)** typically gets limited authority to wind up the LLC or step into the owner's shoes. That authority may require court letters, which can take weeks or months.
- **Probate is possible.** If the LLC interest is not in a living trust or covered by a transfer-on-death arrangement, it may go through probate, which is public and slow.
- **The liability shield is not automatically lost**, but a company that sits unmanaged, misses annual reports, or is not wound up properly can drift into administrative dissolution.

## Managing Member vs Successor: Who Actually Runs the LLC?

This is where many owners get tripped up, so it deserves its own section. **Managing member vs successor** is really a question about two different roles.

- **A managing member** (or manager) is the person with authority to run the business day to day: sign contracts, open bank accounts, hire, and bind the LLC. In a manager-managed LLC, the manager may not even be an owner. See our guide to [member-managed vs manager-managed LLCs](/blog/llc-member-vs-manager-managed/) for background.
- **A successor** is the person or entity named to take over a role or interest when someone dies or becomes incapacitated. A successor manager steps into management. A successor member (or transferee) steps into ownership or economic rights.

Here is how the two interact when an owner dies:

| Situation | Who controls the LLC | Who holds the economics |
|---|---|---|
| Member-managed, multi-member, no agreement | Surviving members | Deceased member's estate (as transferee) |
| Manager-managed, deceased was a non-manager member | Existing manager continues | Estate or heirs |
| Manager-managed, deceased was the manager | Depends on agreement; members usually vote on a new manager | Estate or heirs |
| Single-member, no successor named | Nobody, until the personal representative acts | Estate |
| Single-member, successor manager named in agreement | The named successor, immediately | Estate or heirs |

The practical lesson is that **naming a successor manager and a successor member are two separate decisions.** You might want your spouse to inherit the profits but your business partner or a trusted employee to run operations. That split is perfectly legal, but only if it is written down.

Without that language, a court or the default statute decides, and the outcome may be neither what you nor your family would have chosen.

## Tax Consequences When an LLC Member Dies

Please treat this section as a general overview rather than tax advice. The details depend on how your LLC is taxed.

**Single-member LLCs (disregarded entities).** For income tax purposes, the IRS treats the business as part of the owner. After death, the estate generally becomes the owner for tax purposes, and the estate may need its own EIN. The [IRS LLC guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc) explains classification options.

**Multi-member LLCs (partnerships by default).** The partnership's tax year typically closes for the deceased partner on the date of death, so income is split between the final personal return and the estate. The partnership itself continues if the business does.

**Basis step-up.** Under Internal Revenue Code Section 1014, assets inherited at death generally receive a basis equal to fair market value at the date of death. For an LLC interest, this can significantly reduce capital gains tax if heirs later sell. A multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership may also benefit from a Section 754 election, which adjusts the *inside* basis of partnership assets. Your accountant should evaluate this promptly, since timing matters.

**S-corp elections.** If the LLC elected S-corp status, the estate can hold the shares for a time, but not every kind of heir or trust qualifies as an eligible shareholder. A mistake can terminate the election. See our guide on [LLC vs S-Corp](/blog/llc-vs-s-corp-which-is-better-for-taxes/) for the basics.

**Estate tax.** For 2026, the federal estate tax exemption is $15 million per person under current law, so most small business owners will not owe federal estate tax. Some states have their own estate or inheritance taxes with much lower thresholds. Confirm the current figures with a professional, since the law has changed repeatedly in recent years.

## Practical Steps to Take Before a Member Dies

I've seen too many business owners put off these steps because the topic feels morbid. Spending an afternoon on them now can save your family months of litigation and cost.

1. **Put a death and succession clause in your operating agreement.** Spell out who becomes a member, who manages, how the interest is valued, and how the estate is paid.
2. **Add a buy-sell agreement.** Decide whether it is a cross-purchase (members buy each other out) or a redemption (the LLC buys the interest). Use a valuation formula or a named appraiser.
3. **Fund it with life insurance.** Term coverage is often the cheapest way to guarantee the cash is there.
4. **Name a successor manager** and give them signing authority that takes effect on death or incapacity.
5. **For solo owners, consider a revocable living trust** that holds the LLC interest, so it can pass without probate.
6. **Keep records accessible.** Store the operating agreement, EIN letter, bank details, and state filings where a trusted person can find them.
7. **Review the plan every year or after a major event.** Divorce, a new member, or a big change in company value can make an old agreement obsolete.

## What the Surviving Members or Heirs Should Do Right Away

If a member has just died, here is a sensible order of operations:

- **Read the operating agreement first.** It controls most decisions.
- **Notify the bank, key customers, and insurers** as appropriate, and confirm who has signing authority.
- **Contact the estate's executor or personal representative** and get legal advice on their authority.
- **Check your state's deadlines** for continuing the LLC if it was a single-member company.
- **Talk to a CPA** about the final-year tax filings, the basis step-up, and any Section 754 election.
- **Keep the LLC in good standing.** Annual reports and fees are still due, and a missed filing can lead to administrative dissolution. If that happens, our guide on [how to reinstate a dissolved LLC](/blog/how-to-reinstate-a-dissolved-llc/) explains the recovery process.
- **If winding down is the right call,** follow a proper process. Our guide on [how to dissolve an LLC](/blog/how-to-dissolve-an-llc/) covers the steps.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What happens to an LLC when a member dies?
In most states with a multi-member LLC, the business continues. The deceased member is dissociated, and their estate or heirs typically keep the economic rights (profits and distributions) but not voting or management rights. The operating agreement can change this.

### What happens if the only member of an LLC dies?
The LLC may dissolve unless the member's legal representative or heirs agree to continue it within the period set by state law, often around 90 days. Until someone is authorized to act, the business may be unable to operate, so naming a successor in advance is important.

### Does an LLC dissolve when a member dies?
Not automatically in most modern statutes. Older state laws, or an operating agreement that says so, can make death a dissolution event. Check your state's statute and your operating agreement.

### Can a spouse or child inherit an LLC membership?
They can usually inherit the economic interest. Becoming a full member with voting rights typically requires the consent of the remaining members or permission under the operating agreement.

### Who runs the LLC after the managing member dies?
It depends on your operating agreement. In a manager-managed LLC, members usually vote on a new manager unless a successor is named. In a single-member LLC with no named successor, no one has clear authority until the personal representative is appointed.

### Does the LLC's liability protection end when the owner dies?
No, the liability shield does not end simply because of death. But failing to maintain the LLC, such as missing filings or mixing estate and company funds, can create risk.

### Do heirs get a stepped-up basis in an LLC interest?
Generally yes. Under IRC Section 1014, inherited assets usually get a basis equal to fair market value at death. How this applies depends on your LLC's tax classification, so confirm it with a CPA.

### Should I have a buy-sell agreement for my LLC?
If you have co-owners, almost always. It sets the price and terms for a buyout, gives the estate liquidity, and lets the survivors keep control of the business.
