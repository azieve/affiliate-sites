If you move packages, legal documents, lab specimens, pharmacy orders, or restaurant catering across town for a living, you are running a business with real exposure, whether or not you have ever filed a piece of paperwork. Every pickup involves someone else's property, a vehicle on public roads, and a deadline that someone is paying you to hit. An LLC for courier and delivery business owners is the simplest way to put a legal boundary between that exposure and your personal finances. A formation service like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) will file one for $0 plus your state's filing fee, and most couriers can have their paperwork submitted in under 20 minutes.

This guide is written for local and regional couriers: same-day delivery operators, medical and specimen couriers, document runners, pharmacy and lab route drivers, and small delivery services with a few vans. It is not about long-haul operations, which face a completely different regulatory world. Here we cover why a courier business LLC matters, where the real risks hide, how courier income is taxed in 2026, what insurance the LLC does not replace, and how to set everything up without overspending.

## Why Couriers Face More Liability Than They Think

Most people start a delivery service with a reliable car and a handful of clients. The legal structure is an afterthought. That works fine until something goes wrong, and in this line of work, the things that go wrong tend to involve other people's property and other people's health.

Here is what you actually take on every working day:

- **Custody of goods you do not own.** A damaged $4,000 architectural model, a lost signed contract, or a spoiled temperature-sensitive medication is a claim against you, not against the client.
- **Vehicle operation for commercial purposes.** You spend more hours on the road than almost any other small business owner. Fender-benders are a statistical certainty over a long enough career.
- **Access to sensitive information.** Medical couriers handle protected health information. Legal couriers handle privileged documents. Even a restaurant delivery service sees names, addresses, and payment details.
- **Contractual promises.** Same-day and time-critical deliveries come with service-level commitments. Miss a courthouse filing deadline and your client's case may suffer, and they will look to you for recovery.
- **People you bring on.** The moment you hire a second driver or subcontract overflow routes, you absorb their mistakes too.

If you operate as a sole proprietor, there is no legal distinction between you and the business. A judgment for a lost specimen shipment or an at-fault collision can reach your savings, your personal vehicle, and the equity in your home. A properly maintained delivery service LLC generally limits a creditor's recovery to what the LLC owns.

In my experience, the courier operators who get hurt are rarely the ones who caused a dramatic accident. They are the ones who had a routine dispute, a damaged parcel claim or a client who refused to pay a $3,200 invoice, and discovered they had no entity, no written terms, and no separation between business and personal money. The LLC does not stop disputes from happening. It changes what is on the table when they do.

Two important caveats. First, an LLC will not protect you from your own negligence behind the wheel. If you personally cause a crash, you can be sued personally regardless of entity. That is what insurance is for, which we cover below. Second, the protection only holds if you actually run the LLC as a separate business: separate bank account, contracts in the LLC's name, and no mixing of funds. Courts can disregard an LLC that exists on paper only.

## LLC vs. Sole Proprietorship for an Independent Courier

The honest comparison for most couriers is between doing nothing and forming an LLC. Our detailed [LLC vs. sole proprietorship](/learn/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/) breakdown covers the general trade-offs, but here is how it plays out specifically for an independent courier.

**Liability.** Sole proprietorship: unlimited personal liability. LLC: liability generally limited to business assets, with the exceptions noted above.

**Taxes.** Identical by default. A single-member LLC is a "disregarded entity" for federal tax purposes, so you report income and expenses on Schedule C exactly as a sole proprietor would. The LLC does not raise or lower your tax bill on its own. It does give you the option to elect S-corp treatment later, which can matter once your profit grows.

**Credibility.** This one is underrated. Hospitals, laboratories, law firms, and pharmacies increasingly require vendors to be registered business entities with their own insurance certificates. Several medical courier contracts I have reviewed explicitly state that the vendor must be a corporation or LLC. A sole proprietor cannot even get to the bidding stage.

**Cost.** Sole proprietorship costs nothing to form. An LLC costs your state's filing fee, which ranges from about $35 in Montana to $500 in Massachusetts, plus annual report or franchise tax obligations that vary widely. Our guide on [how much an LLC costs](/learn/how-much-does-an-llc-cost/) has a state-by-state table.

**Administration.** An LLC requires a registered agent, an annual report in most states, and basic record-keeping. For a one-person courier operation, this adds perhaps two to four hours a year.

For a courier earning under $15,000 a year as a side gig with a single low-risk client, a sole proprietorship with good insurance may be defensible. For anyone running regular routes, handling medical or legal materials, hiring drivers, or earning a full-time income, a courier business LLC is the standard answer.

## Special Considerations for a Medical Courier LLC

Medical and specimen couriers deserve their own section because the regulatory and liability stakes are higher than for general delivery work, and because the LLC interacts with those rules in specific ways.

### HIPAA and business associate agreements

If you transport lab specimens, pharmacy orders, medical records, or anything else that contains protected health information, your clients are "covered entities" and you are almost certainly a "business associate" under HIPAA. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services explains the obligations in its [business associate guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/business-associates/index.html). Practically, this means:

- Clients will require you to sign a Business Associate Agreement (BAA).
- You must implement reasonable safeguards (locked containers, chain-of-custody logs, no photos of labels).
- You can be directly liable for breaches, with civil penalties that are adjusted annually for inflation and can run into six figures for serious violations.

A BAA signed by your medical courier LLC, rather than by you personally, keeps that contractual liability inside the entity. That is one of the clearest arguments for forming the LLC before you pursue healthcare clients rather than after.

### Biological specimens and hazardous materials rules

Diagnostic specimens are typically shipped as "Biological Substance, Category B" (UN3373) under federal hazardous materials regulations administered by the Department of Transportation's Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration. Ground couriers carrying these materials need to follow packaging and labeling rules and may need documented training. Your LLC should hold the training records and written procedures, both because clients will ask for them and because they demonstrate the kind of operational discipline that supports your liability shield.

### Temperature control and spoilage claims

Refrigerated medications and specimens that go out of range are a frequent source of claims. A $900 biologic that spoils on a hot afternoon is a real loss, and a client's lab may bill you for recollection costs on top. Your contracts should define temperature responsibilities, your insurance should include cargo or inland marine coverage for spoilage, and your LLC should be the party on both documents.

## How Courier and Delivery Income Is Taxed in 2026

Courier work is unusually deduction-rich, and getting the tax side right often saves more than the LLC costs. Here is how the pieces fit together for the 2026 tax year.

### Self-employment tax

Whether you are a sole proprietor or a single-member LLC taxed as a disregarded entity, your net profit is subject to self-employment tax of 15.3% (12.4% Social Security up to the annual wage base plus 2.9% Medicare), in addition to ordinary income tax. On $60,000 of net profit, that is roughly $8,500 in self-employment tax alone before any income tax. This is the number that makes the S-corp election interesting later.

### Vehicle expenses: the biggest deduction

For a courier, the vehicle is the business. You have two methods:

**Standard mileage rate.** You multiply business miles by the IRS rate (70 cents per mile for 2025; confirm the 2026 figure on IRS.gov). A courier driving 30,000 business miles a year deducts roughly $21,000 under the 2025 rate. You must keep a contemporaneous mileage log. IRS [Publication 463](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p463) lays out the substantiation rules, and the agency does audit this deduction.

**Actual expenses.** You deduct the business percentage of fuel, maintenance, insurance, registration, depreciation, and lease payments. If you bought a dedicated cargo van, actual expenses often win, especially in the first year. Federal legislation enacted in 2025 restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualifying business property, which can allow a large first-year write-off on a vehicle used more than 50% for business, subject to limits on passenger automobiles. Confirm the details with a CPA before you buy; the rules are technical and the decision is hard to reverse.

Key rule: once you use actual expenses on a vehicle, you generally cannot switch that vehicle back to the standard mileage method. Choose deliberately.

### Other deductible courier expenses

- Phone and data plan (business percentage)
- Route optimization and dispatch software
- Insulated bags, coolers, dollies, hand trucks, and packaging supplies
- Tolls and parking (but not traffic tickets)
- Commercial auto, general liability, and cargo insurance premiums
- Uniforms with your logo, vehicle magnets, and wraps
- Background checks and drug screening required by clients
- Health insurance premiums (above-the-line deduction for the self-employed)
- Retirement contributions to a Solo 401(k) or SEP IRA
- The qualified business income deduction, which can shelter up to 20% of qualified business income for eligible owners

### When the S-corp election starts to make sense

Once your delivery service LLC consistently nets around $60,000 to $80,000 or more, ask a CPA to model an S-corp election. You pay yourself a reasonable salary (subject to payroll taxes) and take the remaining profit as distributions that avoid self-employment tax. The savings must outweigh payroll service fees, a separate tax return, and the administrative burden. Our [LLC vs. S-corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) guide walks through the math. Formation services like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) and [LegalZoom](/go/legalzoom/) can file Form 2553 for you, though you can also submit it yourself at no cost.

## Insurance: What the LLC Does Not Cover

I want to be direct about this because it is the most common and most expensive misunderstanding I see among new couriers. **Your personal auto policy almost certainly excludes commercial delivery use.** If you are in a collision while making a paid delivery and the insurer discovers the business use, they can deny the claim entirely. The LLC does not fix that. Insurance does.

A properly protected courier operation in 2026 typically carries:

- **Commercial auto insurance.** Covers vehicles used for business, including liability and physical damage. For a single car or small van, expect roughly $1,800 to $4,500 per year depending on location, driving record, and coverage limits. Expect clients to require at least $1,000,000 in combined single limits.
- **Hired and non-owned auto coverage.** Essential once you have subcontracted drivers using their own vehicles.
- **General liability.** Covers injury or property damage that is not vehicle-related, such as a dropped package that damages a client's lobby floor. Often $400 to $900 per year for a small operation.
- **Cargo or inland marine coverage.** Covers the goods in your custody. Medical couriers should confirm spoilage and refrigeration breakdown are included.
- **Workers' compensation.** Required in most states once you have employees. Some states require it even for a single employee.
- **Occupational accident coverage.** A common alternative for owner-operators and 1099 drivers where workers' comp is not mandated.

Insurance protects against the loss itself. The LLC protects your personal assets if the loss exceeds coverage or falls into a gap. You need both, and your LLC should be the named insured on every policy.

## Hiring Drivers: The Contractor Classification Trap

Growth in courier work almost always means adding drivers, and nearly everyone wants to treat them as independent contractors. Be careful. Worker classification is one of the most actively enforced areas of employment law, and the gig-economy delivery platforms have spent years in litigation over exactly this question. State tests vary significantly: California's ABC test is strict, while many other states use a more flexible economic-realities analysis.

Factors that push toward employee status include setting drivers' schedules, requiring uniforms, dictating routes, prohibiting them from working for competitors, and paying hourly rather than per delivery. Misclassification can mean back payroll taxes, penalties, unpaid overtime, and workers' comp exposure. Your LLC absorbs the business-level consequences, but owners can be held personally responsible for unpaid payroll taxes, so this is not something the entity shields you from.

If you plan to scale, budget for a consultation with an employment attorney in your state before you onboard your first driver. The $500 you spend will look cheap next to a misclassification audit.

## How to Set Up Your Courier LLC Step by Step

Forming an LLC for courier and delivery business work is straightforward. Most owners can complete it in an afternoon.

### 1. Choose your state

Form in the state where you actually operate. A courier running routes in Phoenix should form an Arizona LLC, not a Delaware or Wyoming one. Out-of-state formation for a local service business just means paying to register as a foreign LLC in your home state anyway, doubling your fees. If you deliver across a metro area that straddles state lines, you may need to register in both; talk to a professional about where your "nexus" lies.

### 2. Pick a name and check availability

Your name must be distinguishable from existing entities in your state and include a designator such as "LLC." Search your Secretary of State's database, and check the USPTO trademark database before you print vehicle wraps. "Rapid Courier LLC" sounds generic, but a trademark conflict can force an expensive rebrand.

### 3. Appoint a registered agent

Every LLC needs a registered agent with a physical address in the state to receive legal notices. You can serve as your own, but couriers are rarely at a fixed address during business hours, which is exactly when process servers arrive. A missed lawsuit notice can lead to a default judgment. Using a commercial registered agent (typically $100 to $300 per year, and included free for the first year with several formation packages) is a sensible expense for this profession. See our explainer on [what a registered agent does](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/).

### 4. File the Articles of Organization

This is the document that creates the LLC. You can file directly with your state or let a formation service handle it. The state fee is the same either way.

### 5. Draft an operating agreement

Even for a single-member LLC, an operating agreement documents that the business is a separate entity with its own rules. Banks and some clients will ask for it. If you bring on a partner or investor later, it becomes essential.

### 6. Get an EIN

Free from the IRS and takes about ten minutes online. You need it to open a business bank account and to hire anyone.

### 7. Open a dedicated business bank account

Non-negotiable. Every client payment goes in, every business expense comes out. This single habit does more to preserve your liability protection than anything else on this list.

### 8. Handle licenses, permits, and compliance filings

Local business licenses vary by city and county. Medical couriers may need additional registrations. Check our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) for the current status of federal beneficial ownership reporting, which has changed significantly since the requirement was introduced. Set calendar reminders for your state's annual report.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need an LLC to start a courier business?

No. You can legally operate as a sole proprietor in every state. But many commercial, medical, and legal clients will only contract with a registered business entity, and the liability exposure of transporting other people's property makes an LLC for courier and delivery business operators the sensible default for anyone doing this full time.

### How much does it cost to form an LLC for a courier and delivery business?

State filing fees range from roughly $35 to $500, with most states between $50 and $200. Formation services such as ZenBusiness charge $0 for the basic filing on top of the state fee. Budget another $100 to $300 per year for a registered agent and whatever your state charges for annual reports or franchise taxes.

### Does an LLC protect me if I cause a car accident while delivering?

Only partially. The LLC can shield your personal assets from claims against the business, but if you were personally driving and at fault, you can also be sued individually. Commercial auto insurance with adequate limits is your primary protection for collisions. The LLC is the backstop.

### Can I use my personal car for my courier LLC?

Yes, but tell your insurer. Personal auto policies typically exclude commercial delivery use, and a claim can be denied if the insurer learns you were working. You will need a commercial policy or a business-use endorsement. For taxes, you can deduct the business-use percentage via mileage or actual expenses.

### Do medical couriers need special licenses?

Requirements vary by state and client. Most medical couriers need to sign HIPAA business associate agreements, follow federal rules for transporting Category B biological substances, and complete client-mandated training such as bloodborne pathogen awareness. Some states have additional registration requirements for transporting pharmaceuticals.

### Should I form my courier LLC in Delaware or Wyoming?

Almost never for a local delivery business. You would still have to register as a foreign LLC in the state where you operate, paying two sets of fees for no practical benefit. Form in your home state.

### When should a courier LLC elect S-corp status?

Typically when net profit is consistently in the $60,000 to $80,000 range or higher, so that self-employment tax savings exceed the added payroll and accounting costs. Have a CPA run the numbers for your specific situation.

### Can I pay my delivery drivers as 1099 contractors?

Sometimes, but the test depends on how much control you exercise and on your state's rules. Setting schedules, requiring uniforms, and dictating routes all point toward employee status. Misclassification penalties can be severe, so get state-specific advice before hiring.
