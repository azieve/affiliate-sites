Moving is one of the few businesses where your crew carries a customer's entire household out the front door. That includes the piano, the antique dresser and the 65-inch TV. It's also a business where a single bad day can cost more than a year of profit: a dropped sofa through a window, a truck accident on the interstate, a claim over a missing box of jewelry. Forming an LLC for a moving company is one of the most cost-effective steps you can take to keep those events from reaching your house, your savings and your personal credit.

The formation itself is cheap and quick. A service like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) can file your paperwork starting at $0 plus your state's filing fee, which typically runs from about $50 to $500 depending on the state. The harder part is everything surrounding the filing: federal and state moving licenses, the right insurance, a payroll structure that fits movers, and tax decisions that change as you grow. This guide walks through all of it for 2026.

## Why a Moving Company Needs an LLC

A limited liability company separates your business from you personally. If the company is sued or owes a debt, creditors generally go after company assets, not your home or personal bank accounts. For an overview of the structure, see our guide on [what is an LLC](/learn/what-is-an-llc/).

Movers face risks that most small businesses don't:

- **Cargo damage and loss claims.** Customers hand you high-value, sentimental property.
- **Worker injuries.** Lifting, stairs and tight spaces cause strains, falls and crush injuries.
- **Vehicle accidents.** A loaded box truck is a serious liability on the road.
- **Property damage.** Scratched hardwood floors, dented walls and broken railings are routine claims.
- **Disputes over estimates.** Pricing disagreements are a leading source of consumer complaints in the industry.

Here is a realistic scenario. Say your two-truck operation is sued after a driver causes a collision and the damages exceed your policy limits. As a sole proprietor, the plaintiff can pursue your personal assets. With a properly maintained LLC, they are generally limited to the business's assets. In my experience, the movers who get hurt worst are rarely the ones with bad luck. They're the ones who "meant to" form an entity and never did, or who formed one and then ran it carelessly.

That second point matters. An LLC is not a magic shield. Courts can "pierce the veil" if you mix personal and business funds, skip required filings, or personally commit wrongdoing. The LLC protects you from business debts and most claims against the company. It does not protect you from your own negligence, such as personally driving recklessly. Insurance does that work.

## LLC vs. Sole Proprietorship for Movers

Plenty of owner-operators start with "man and a van" and no entity at all. That works until the first serious claim. Compare the two:

| Factor | Sole Proprietorship | LLC |
|---|---|---|
| Personal liability protection | None | Yes, for business debts and most claims |
| Formation cost | $0 (plus any DBA fee) | State filing fee, roughly $50 to $500 |
| Ongoing cost | None | Annual report or franchise tax in many states |
| Credibility with customers and brokers | Lower | Higher |
| Tax treatment | Schedule C, self-employment tax | Default same as sole proprietor; can elect S-corp |

For a deeper breakdown, read our [LLC vs sole proprietorship](/learn/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/) guide. In short, if you have employees, own a truck, or handle customers' belongings, an LLC is worth the modest cost.

## Licensing and Registration for a Moving Company LLC

This is where moving differs most from other service businesses, and where new owners get tripped up. Forming the LLC does not authorize you to move anyone's belongings. Licensing depends on where you operate.

**Interstate moves (across state lines).** Household goods movers must register with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration. According to the [FMCSA](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/), this generally means:

- Obtaining a USDOT number
- Applying for motor carrier (MC) operating authority for household goods
- Designating a process agent (the BOC-3 filing) in each state where you operate
- Carrying the required minimum insurance and filing proof with FMCSA
- Registering under the Unified Carrier Registration (UCR) program annually

The FMCSA has also published consumer-protection requirements for household goods carriers, such as providing the "Ready to Move?" and "Your Rights and Responsibilities When You Move" booklets to customers. Check the current fee schedule on FMCSA's site, because the operating authority application fee has been several hundred dollars and agency fees change.

**Intrastate moves (within one state).** Rules vary widely. Some states, such as California and Texas, require a state-level moving permit or registration and specific insurance. Others have light requirements, and some leave it to local jurisdictions. Check your state's transportation, public utilities or consumer-affairs agency before you advertise.

**Gotcha: name consistency.** Your legal LLC name, USDOT registration, insurance policies, vehicle titles and bank account should match exactly. A mismatch can delay authority approval and cause trouble with insurance claims. Form the LLC first, get your EIN, and then apply for DOT and state permits under that name.

## Moving Business Insurance: What to Carry Alongside Your LLC

Insurance is the real backbone of your protection. The LLC covers what insurance doesn't. Most moving companies need a stack of policies:

- **General liability.** Covers property damage and injury claims at customer sites.
- **Commercial auto.** Covers your trucks. Personal auto policies typically do not cover commercial moving use.
- **Motor truck cargo insurance.** Covers customers' goods in your custody. Federal rules for household goods carriers include minimum cargo coverage, and many states require their own minimums.
- **Workers' compensation.** Required in nearly every state once you have employees. Moving is a high-risk class, so premiums are significant.
- **Umbrella or excess liability.** Adds limits above your underlying policies.

Pricing varies enormously by state, fleet size, driving records and claims history. A single-truck operation might pay several thousand dollars a year for core coverage, while multi-truck companies with employees can pay far more, mostly driven by workers' comp and commercial auto. Get quotes from brokers who specialize in moving and trucking.

**Valuation coverage trap.** Federal rules for interstate household goods moves require carriers to offer customers different levels of liability coverage. The default "released value" protection is only 60 cents per pound per article. That means a 20-pound TV that cost $1,200 might be valued at $12. Customers who don't understand this file angry complaints and chargebacks. Explain it clearly in writing before the move, and offer full-value protection options. It protects your reputation as well as your customers.

## How to Start a Moving Company LLC: Step by Step

Here is an efficient order of operations for a moving company LLC:

1. **Choose your state.** Almost always, form the LLC in the state where you physically operate. Forming in Delaware or Wyoming and then registering as a foreign LLC at home usually just doubles your fees. Our [best state to form an LLC](/blog/best-state-to-form-an-llc/) guide explains when out-of-state formation actually makes sense.
2. **Pick a compliant name.** Include "LLC" and check availability on your state's business search. Make sure the name is not confusingly similar to an existing carrier.
3. **Appoint a registered agent.** This is the person or company that receives legal papers. See [what is a registered agent](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/) for details. Movers are often on the road, so using a professional agent avoids missed lawsuit notices.
4. **File Articles of Organization** with your Secretary of State.
5. **Create an operating agreement.** Even a single-member LLC should have one. Our [LLC operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/) covers what to include, such as ownership percentages, decision-making and what happens if a partner leaves.
6. **Get an EIN** from the IRS. It's free at [IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/apply-for-an-employer-identification-number-ein-online). Beware of sites that charge for it.
7. **Open a business bank account.** Keep all revenue, tips collected on behalf of crews and expenses in this account.
8. **Apply for USDOT, MC authority and state permits** as needed.
9. **Bind your insurance** before you move your first customer.
10. **Set up payroll and bookkeeping.** Moving companies often hire seasonal labor, so build systems before the summer rush.

## Taxes for an LLC for Movers

By default, the IRS treats a single-member LLC as a "disregarded entity." You report income on Schedule C of your personal return and pay self-employment tax (15.3% on net earnings up to the Social Security wage base, then 2.9% Medicare above that). A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default. Our [LLC taxes guide](/blog/llc-taxes-how-are-llcs-taxed/) covers each option in detail.

**S-corp election.** Once your net profit is steady and substantial, electing S-corp taxation may reduce self-employment tax. You pay yourself a "reasonable salary" through payroll, and the remaining profit passes through without self-employment tax. As a rough illustration only: if your moving company nets $120,000 and a reasonable salary for your role is $70,000, the remaining $50,000 would avoid self-employment tax, less the added costs of payroll processing, state requirements and a separate business tax return. The savings can be real, but so can the compliance burden and IRS scrutiny of salary levels. Compare the options in our [LLC vs S-corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) guide and talk to a CPA before electing.

**Deductions movers commonly claim** (subject to IRS rules and documentation):

- Truck depreciation or lease payments, fuel, tolls and maintenance
- Insurance premiums
- Moving equipment: dollies, pads, straps, hand trucks, ramps
- Packing supplies resold or used on jobs
- Uniforms and safety gear
- Marketing, software and lead-generation costs
- Payroll and payroll taxes

Review [IRS Publication 463](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p463) for the rules on vehicle and travel expenses, and keep mileage logs and receipts. Heavy trucks may qualify for accelerated depreciation, but eligibility depends on weight rating, use and current tax law, so confirm with a professional.

**Worker classification gotcha.** Many movers hire day laborers as 1099 contractors to save on payroll taxes. This is one of the most audited areas. If your crew works under your direction, uses your equipment and works set schedules, they are likely employees. Misclassification can bring back taxes, penalties and workers' comp exposure. I've seen too many owners treat "helpers" as contractors for years and then face a large bill after one injury claim.

## Ongoing Compliance and Protecting Your Liability Shield

To keep your liability protection intact, treat the LLC as a real, separate business:

- **Keep finances separate.** Never pay personal expenses from the business account.
- **File annual reports and pay state fees on time.** Requirements vary. For example, [California](/start-llc/california/) charges an $800 minimum annual franchise tax, while [Texas](/start-llc/texas/) uses a franchise tax with a no-tax-due threshold. Failing to file can lead to administrative dissolution.
- **Maintain your DOT and state registrations.** Biennial updates are required for USDOT registration, and lapses can result in deactivation.
- **Watch beneficial ownership reporting rules.** FinCEN's rules for domestic companies have changed repeatedly in recent years. Check our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) for the latest status in 2026 before assuming you do or don't need to file.
- **Sign contracts as the LLC.** Sign "Smith Moving LLC, by John Smith, Owner," not just your name, and put the LLC name on estimates, invoices and truck lettering.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need an LLC to start a moving company?

No law requires one, but it is strongly advisable. Without an LLC, claims from property damage, injuries or accidents can reach your personal assets. You'll still need the right licenses and insurance either way.

### How much does it cost to start a moving company LLC?

Formation typically costs a state filing fee of about $50 to $500, plus optional service fees if you use a provider like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/). Licensing, permits and insurance are separate and usually far larger costs.

### Do I need a USDOT number for my moving company?

If you move household goods across state lines, you generally need a USDOT number and operating authority from the FMCSA. Some states also require a USDOT number for intrastate commercial vehicles above certain weight thresholds. Check the FMCSA and your state agency.

### Can I operate as a moving company without insurance because I have an LLC?

No. An LLC does not replace insurance, and many licenses require proof of coverage. The LLC limits your personal exposure, while insurance pays claims. You need both.

### Should my moving company LLC elect S-corp status?

It depends on your profit level and how much of it you can reasonably take as salary. Many owners consider it once net profit is consistently well above a reasonable salary for their role. Talk to a CPA before filing the election, because payroll and compliance costs offset some savings.

### What happens if I don't keep my LLC in good standing?

Your state can administratively dissolve the LLC, and you may lose liability protection and the ability to sue or enter certain contracts. Reinstatement usually involves back fees and penalties. Our guide on [what happens if you don't renew your LLC](/blog/what-happens-if-you-don-t-renew-your-llc/) explains the consequences.

### Which state should I form my moving company LLC in?

Usually your home state, where your trucks, crews and customers are. Forming elsewhere typically adds a second registration and fees without reducing your obligations where you operate.
