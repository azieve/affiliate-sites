If you run a private nutrition practice, you are probably handling client health information, giving individualized advice, and collecting payments, often from a kitchen table or a laptop. Forming an LLC for nutritionists and dietitians is one of the most practical steps you can take to separate your personal assets from your business. It also makes your practice look more professional to clients, insurers, and referral partners.

Formation is not expensive. Most state filing fees run $50 to $500, and a service such as [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) starts at $0 plus state fees for its basic package. [LegalZoom](/go/legalzoom/) is a well-known alternative with higher service fees. This guide explains how an LLC works for dietitians and nutritionists in 2026, including a licensing wrinkle that catches many people off guard: in some states you may need a professional LLC (PLLC) rather than a standard one.

## Do Nutritionists and Dietitians Really Need an LLC?

Not legally, in most cases. You can practice as a sole proprietor the day you start seeing clients. The question is whether you should.

Consider what can go wrong in a nutrition practice:

- A client with diabetes or a kidney condition says your meal plan worsened their health.
- A client claims you shared their information improperly.
- A supplement you recommended is linked to a bad reaction.
- A group program participant is injured at an in-person event, or you are sued over a contract dispute with a landlord or vendor.

As a sole proprietor, a judgment in any of these cases can reach your savings, car, and possibly your home. An LLC creates a legal wall between the business and your personal assets for business debts and many claims.

**An important limit:** an LLC does not protect you from your own professional negligence. If you personally give harmful advice, you can still be sued. That is why professional liability (malpractice) insurance matters regardless of entity type. Think of the LLC as protection from business debts, contract claims, and the mistakes of employees or contractors, and insurance as protection from your own professional errors.

In my experience, the practitioners who benefit most are those who have moved past "a few clients on the side." If you have recurring revenue, a lease, contractors, or a growing online program, the LLC is cheap insurance. If you are still testing the market with two clients a month, the case is weaker, though many people form early to start with clean books. For the broader fundamentals, see our guide on [what an LLC is](/learn/what-is-an-llc/) and our comparison of [LLC vs sole proprietorship](/learn/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/).

## LLC vs. PLLC: The Licensing Question

This is the part most generic LLC guides skip. Many states restrict how licensed professionals can organize. Some require a **professional LLC (PLLC)** or a professional corporation for licensed health professions. Whether dietitians fall under those rules depends entirely on your state.

Key points:

- **Registered Dietitians (RD/RDN)** are credentialed through the Commission on Dietetic Registration, and many states also license or certify them. States with licensure often have separate professional-entity rules.
- **Nutritionists** face the widest variation. Some states license or restrict the title, others regulate almost nothing, and a few limit who can provide individualized medical nutrition therapy.
- **Professional entity rules** may require that owners hold the relevant license, that the name include "PLLC," or that you obtain approval from a licensing board before filing.
- Some states allow a standard LLC for your profession, while others route you toward a PLLC. Your state's business filing office and your licensing board are the authorities here.

Before you file, confirm with both your state licensing board and the secretary of state (or equivalent) which entity type applies to you. Filing the wrong type can mean a rejected filing or a later amendment. For a closely related example, our guide on [LLC for therapists and counselors](/blog/llc-for-therapists-and-counselors/) and [LLC for physical therapists](/blog/llc-for-physical-therapists/) walk through how PLLC rules play out in other health professions.

If your state treats your work as unregulated coaching rather than a licensed profession, a standard LLC is typically fine. This is common for health coaches and wellness-focused nutrition consultants, which is the segment a nutrition coaching business LLC usually serves.

## How an LLC Is Taxed for Dietitians and Nutritionists

By default, a single-member LLC is a "disregarded entity" for federal income tax. You report income and expenses on Schedule C, exactly as a sole proprietor does. A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default. The IRS explains these classifications on its [LLC page at IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc).

**Self-employment tax** is the part that surprises new private practice dietitians. Net business profit is generally subject to self-employment tax of 15.3% (12.4% Social Security up to the annual wage base, plus 2.9% Medicare). Our deeper explainer on [self-employment tax for LLC owners](/blog/self-employment-tax-for-llc-owners/) covers the math.

### When an S corporation election can help

Once profit grows, you can elect to have your LLC taxed as an S corporation by filing Form 2553. You then pay yourself a "reasonable salary" subject to payroll taxes, and remaining profit passes through without self-employment tax.

A simplified illustration, not tax advice:

| Scenario | Net profit | Approx. SE tax as sole prop/default LLC | Potential effect with S corp |
|---|---|---|---|
| Part-time practice | $45,000 | about $6,360 | Savings often outweighed by added payroll and filing costs |
| Established practice | $110,000 | about $15,540 | Possible savings if a reasonable salary is, say, $70,000 |

The S election adds costs: payroll processing, a separate business tax return (Form 1120-S), and in some states additional fees. Many advisors suggest it begins to make sense at roughly $80,000 or more in net profit, but your numbers and state matter. See [LLC vs S-Corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) and our guide to [reasonable salary for S corp LLC owners](/blog/reasonable-salary-for-s-corp-llc-owners/) before deciding.

## Privacy, HIPAA, and Client Data

If you bill insurance electronically or otherwise qualify as a HIPAA "covered entity," you must meet federal privacy and security requirements. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services explains who is covered at [HHS.gov](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/covered-entities/index.html). Even if you are not covered, state privacy laws and client expectations still apply.

Practical steps:

- Use HIPAA-conscious tools for notes, messaging, and telehealth, and sign business associate agreements where needed.
- Keep business and personal communication channels separate.
- Put privacy language into your client agreements.

An LLC does not make you HIPAA compliant, but separating your business identity helps you keep records organized and defensible.

Another practical point: your LLC's public record will list a registered agent address. If you work from home and do not want your address public, a registered agent service helps. Our guide to [what a registered agent is](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/) explains options, and [Northwest Registered Agent](/go/northwest/) is a strong choice when privacy is the priority (see its [review](/reviews/northwest-registered-agent/)).

## How to Set Up Your Nutrition Practice LLC: Step by Step

**1. Confirm your entity type.** Contact your licensing board and state business filing office to determine whether you need an LLC or PLLC.

**2. Choose your state.** Almost always, form in the state where you live and practice. Forming elsewhere, such as Delaware, rarely helps a local practice and usually adds a second set of fees because you must register as a foreign entity at home. Our [best state to form an LLC](/blog/best-state-to-form-an-llc/) guide explains why.

**3. Pick a compliant name.** Check availability with the state, make sure the name does not imply credentials you do not hold (for example, using "Registered Dietitian" if you are not one), and add "LLC" or "PLLC" as required.

**4. Appoint a registered agent.** You can serve as your own, or hire a service.

**5. File your Articles of Organization.** See [what articles of organization are](/blog/what-are-articles-of-organization/).

**6. Draft an operating agreement.** Even single-member LLCs should have one. Our [operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/) covers what to include.

**7. Get an EIN.** The IRS issues EINs free; see our [free EIN walkthrough](/blog/how-to-get-an-ein-for-your-llc-free-irs-method/). Avoid sites that charge for it.

**8. Open a business bank account.** Keeping funds separate is the single most important habit for preserving liability protection. See [how to open an LLC bank account](/blog/llc-bank-account-how-to-open-one/).

**9. Update licenses, insurance, and payer enrollments.** Notify your licensing board, insurers, and any payer networks of your new entity, and apply for local business licenses where required.

**10. Calendar your ongoing filings.** Many states require annual or biennial reports.

On BOI reporting: rules for beneficial ownership information have changed significantly in recent years, so check [FinCEN's current guidance](https://www.fincen.gov/boi) and our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) to see whether anything applies to your new LLC in 2026.

Your costs break into state fees and optional service fees.

**State filing fees (examples, check current figures):** Many states fall between $50 and $200, while some are higher. **Ongoing costs:** annual or biennial reports, registered agent renewal if you use one, and franchise or minimum taxes in some states.

## Paying Yourself and Keeping Your Protection Intact

With a default-taxed LLC, you take owner's draws rather than a paycheck. Our guide on [how to pay yourself from an LLC](/blog/how-to-pay-yourself-from-an-llc/) explains the mechanics.

To keep liability protection:

- Never mix personal and business funds.
- Sign contracts as "Jane Doe, Owner, Jane Doe Nutrition LLC," not personally.
- Keep your operating agreement and state filings current.
- Carry professional liability insurance and general liability insurance.
- Use written client agreements with scope of services and disclaimers.

If you ever hire staff or subcontractors, see [can an LLC have employees](/blog/can-an-llc-have-employees/) and be careful about worker classification.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do dietitians need an LLC?

Not legally in most cases, but an LLC separates personal and business assets and can strengthen your professional credibility. It does not replace malpractice insurance.

### Should a registered dietitian form an LLC or a PLLC?

It depends on your state. Some states require licensed professionals to use PLLCs or professional corporations, while others allow standard LLCs. Confirm with your licensing board and secretary of state before filing.

### How much does it cost to form an LLC for a nutrition business?

State filing fees typically range from $50 to $500, plus any optional service fees. Ongoing annual reports and franchise taxes vary by state.

### Does an LLC protect me from malpractice claims?

Generally no, not for your own professional negligence. An LLC can protect you from business debts and from the actions of others, but you remain responsible for your own professional conduct. Carry professional liability insurance.

### How are LLC dietitians taxed?

By default, single-member LLC profits flow through to your personal return and are subject to income tax and self-employment tax. You may later elect S corporation treatment if it makes financial sense. Talk to a CPA.

### Can a health coach or nutrition coach form a regular LLC?

Often yes, particularly where coaching is unregulated. If you provide individualized medical nutrition therapy, licensing rules may apply, so check your state's requirements.

### Can I use a formation service for a PLLC?

Some services support PLLC filings, but not all. Confirm before purchasing, and consider attorney-backed options if board approval is required.

### Do I need to file a BOI report in 2026?

Requirements have shifted through litigation and FinCEN rulemaking. Check [FinCEN's website](https://www.fincen.gov/boi) and our [BOI changes and updates guide](/blog/boi-report-changes-and-updates-2026/) for the current status.
