Picking up a 1099 locum assignment means you stop being an employee and start being a business, whether or not you've formed anything. The agency sends you a 1099, nobody withholds tax, and you are on the hook for self-employment tax, quarterly estimates, and your own retirement plan. The open question is whether wrapping that income in a legal entity is worth the cost and trouble.

This guide covers the **LLC for independent contractor physicians and locum tenens** from three angles: whether your state lets you form one, what the entity does and doesn't protect, and how the tax math changes in 2026. The short answer is that it depends heavily on your state's professional-entity rules. Where an LLC or PLLC is allowed, it is usually inexpensive to set up. ## Do Locum Tenens Physicians Actually Need an LLC?

Not always. A sole proprietor locum physician is legal and common. Many doctors start with a handful of assignments and keep operating as individuals, which is the simplest setup. If you're weighing the basics, our guide to [LLC vs sole proprietorship](/learn/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/) covers the general tradeoffs.

An entity starts to make sense when one or more of these apply:

- **Your 1099 income is consistent and substantial.** A locum physician who works 30 or more weeks a year can easily clear $250,000–$400,000 in gross income, depending on specialty. At that level, entity-level tax planning can be worth thousands per year.
- **You want to use an S corp election.** This is the main financial reason physicians form an entity (more below).
- **You have non-clinical exposure.** Contracts, office space, vehicles, equipment, or employees (a scribe, a virtual assistant) create liabilities an entity can help contain.
- **You want cleaner books and banking.** Separating business and personal money makes tax filing, mortgage underwriting, and audits far easier.

I've seen too many physicians form an LLC in a hurry after one tax-season panic, then run personal expenses through the account anyway. The paperwork alone doesn't help you. The entity only works if you treat it as a separate business.

## What an LLC Will and Won't Protect

This is the section I'd ask every physician to read twice. An LLC or PLLC does **not** shield you from your own professional negligence. If you personally commit malpractice, you are personally liable, regardless of the entity. State professional-entity statutes are explicit about this.

What the entity can protect you from:

- A business partner's or colleague's malpractice (relevant if you have co-owners)
- Contract disputes and commercial debts
- Lease, equipment, and vendor obligations
- Claims tied to non-clinical staff you hire

Your real malpractice protection is insurance. Most locum agencies supply coverage, but you need to read the details:

- **Claims-made vs. occurrence:** Claims-made policies need "tail" coverage after an assignment ends, which can run into five figures. Ask who pays for it.
- **Limits:** Common limits are $1M per claim / $3M aggregate. Confirm what applies to each assignment.
- **State coverage:** If you accept assignments across multiple states, confirm each is covered.

For a deeper look at how the entity side of a clinical business works, see our guide to [LLC for medical practice](/blog/llc-for-medical-practice/). That page is aimed at practice owners; this one is aimed at independent contractors who don't own a practice.

## Can a Physician Form an LLC? State Rules Matter

Here is where physicians differ from nearly every other contractor. Many states restrict how licensed physicians can organize because of professional-practice and corporate-practice-of-medicine rules. The result generally falls into three buckets:

1. **PLLC required or available.** Many states let physicians form a professional LLC (PLLC), with ownership limited to licensed professionals. Texas, Florida, and New York, for example, have professional-entity frameworks, though each has its own rules.
2. **Regular LLC permitted.** Some states allow a regular LLC for professional services, sometimes with conditions.
3. **LLC not available for medical practice.** California is the notable example. California generally does not permit physicians to practice through an LLC, and requires a professional corporation instead. If you live in or intend to be licensed in a restrictive state, an LLC may simply not be an option. Check with the state medical board or an attorney.

Two points trip up locum physicians in particular:

- **Home state vs. work state.** Your entity usually forms where you live or where you'll be based. You can be licensed and work in many states without forming an entity in each, but some states have foreign-registration or professional-entity filing requirements. A multi-state practice raises this question quickly. Our general guide to [foreign LLC registration](/blog/foreign-llc-registration-doing-business-in-another-state/) explains the mechanics.
- **Medical board notification.** Some boards require you to register or report a professional entity. Check before you file.

State-specific guides can help you gauge costs and filing steps, such as [starting an LLC in Texas](/start-llc/texas/), [Florida](/start-llc/florida/), or [Delaware](/start-llc/delaware/). Remember that these are general-business guides and your professional entity may carry extra requirements.

## Locum Tenens Taxes: Where the Real Money Is

The tax side is where most physicians feel the pain, and where an entity can help.

### Self-employment tax as a 1099 physician

As a 1099 contractor, you pay both halves of Social Security and Medicare. For 2026, the self-employment tax is 15.3% on net earnings: 12.4% for Social Security up to the annual wage base (**$184,500 in 2026**) and 2.9% for Medicare with no cap. An Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% applies to high earners above certain thresholds. The IRS explains the rules on its [self-employment tax page](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc554), and our own [self-employment tax guide for LLC owners](/blog/self-employment-tax-for-llc-owners/) breaks down the math.

Here's a simplified example. Say your net locum income is $300,000:

- Social Security portion: 12.4% × $184,500 ≈ $22,878
- Medicare portion: 2.9% × ~$277,000 (net earnings after the 92.35% adjustment) ≈ $8,000
- Total: roughly $31,000, before the additional 0.9% Medicare on the high end

Those figures are illustrative; your actual numbers depend on deductions and filing status.

### The S corp election

An LLC (or PLLC) can elect to be taxed as an S corporation by filing IRS Form 2553. Under an S corp, you pay yourself a reasonable W-2 salary and take the remaining profit as distributions that aren't subject to self-employment tax. The IRS describes the framework on its [S corporations page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/s-corporations).

Using the same $300,000 profile, if you set a reasonable salary of $180,000 and take $120,000 as distributions, you might avoid roughly $15,000–$18,000 in combined payroll taxes that year, minus the added costs of payroll, a corporate tax return, and possible state fees. These estimates are illustrative, not guaranteed.

The catch is "reasonable compensation." The IRS expects physician salaries to reflect what the work is worth. Setting a salary of $40,000 on $400,000 of clinical income is an audit invitation. Our guide to [reasonable salary for S corp LLC owners](/blog/reasonable-salary-for-s-corp-llc-owners/) goes through how to set it, and our [LLC vs S-Corp comparison](/blog/llc-vs-s-corp-which-is-better-for-taxes/) lays out the tradeoffs. For the paperwork, see [how to file Form 2553](/blog/how-to-file-form-2553-for-an-llc/).

An S corp usually starts to pencil out for physicians once net income is comfortably above their reasonable salary, and many advisors look at it once profit passes roughly $100,000–$150,000. A CPA should run your numbers.

### Retirement and the QBI deduction

- **Solo 401(k) or SEP-IRA:** Contractors can shelter a large amount of income. For 2026, the employee deferral limit for a 401(k) is $24,500, and total contributions can reach $72,000 (excluding catch-up amounts). With an S corp, contributions are based on your W-2 salary, which is one reason you shouldn't set it artificially low.
- **Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction:** Physicians are in a "specified service trade or business," so the 20% deduction phases out above certain income thresholds (roughly $200,000 single and $400,000 married filing jointly in 2026). High-earning locum doctors often get a reduced benefit or none, so don't count on it.

### State tax and multistate work

Locum tenens taxes get complicated fast when you work in multiple states. You may owe nonresident income tax in each work state and need to claim credits at home. Some states also tax pass-through entities directly. Track your days worked per state from the start, because reconstructing them later is miserable.

## How to Set Up an LLC or PLLC as a Locum Physician

Here's an efficient sequence, in the order I'd recommend.

1. **Confirm your state allows it.** Read your state's professional-entity statute and medical board guidance first. This step prevents most wasted money.
2. **Choose a name and state.** Most physicians form in their state of residence. Forming in Delaware or Wyoming rarely makes sense for a clinician whose work is tied to a state license; see our [best state to form an LLC](/blog/best-state-to-form-an-llc/) guide for the general case.
3. **Appoint a registered agent.** You can serve as your own, but as a traveling physician, you'll want someone reliable at a fixed address. Our explainer on [what a registered agent is](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/) covers this.
4. **File articles of organization (or the PLLC equivalent).** Some states require a license verification letter from your board.
5. **Write an operating agreement.** Even single-member LLCs should have one. See our [operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/).
6. **Get an EIN.** It's free through the IRS; see [how to get an EIN for your LLC for free](/blog/how-to-get-an-ein-for-your-llc-free-irs-method/).
7. **Open a business bank account.** Route all assignment payments through it. Our [LLC bank account guide](/blog/llc-bank-account-how-to-open-one/) walks through it.
8. **Ask your agency to contract with your entity.** This matters: if you sign agency contracts personally but your LLC collects the money, you've undermined the separation. Get the contract and payment details in the entity's name, and update your W-9.
9. **Consider the S election and payroll** once your CPA confirms the numbers.

On the BOI question: under FinCEN's 2025 interim final rule, domestic U.S. companies are currently exempt from beneficial ownership reporting. This area has changed repeatedly, so verify the current status before you file. Our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) tracks it.

## Common Mistakes Locum Physicians Make

- **Forming the entity but contracting personally.** The LLC should be the party to the agreement and the payee.
- **Skipping the professional-entity check.** A plain LLC may be invalid for physicians in your state.
- **Assuming the LLC covers malpractice.** It doesn't. Review insurance and tail coverage.
- **Ignoring quarterly estimated taxes.** Without withholding, you owe estimates each quarter. See our [LLC quarterly tax payments guide](/blog/llc-quarterly-tax-payments-guide/).
- **Underpaying yourself in an S corp.** Unreasonably low salaries draw scrutiny.
- **Missing annual filings.** Calendar every state report and renewal.
- **Not tracking deductions.** Licensing fees, travel, CME, board certification, malpractice premiums, and home-office costs may be deductible. Our [LLC tax deductions list](/blog/llc-tax-deductions-list/) and [home office deduction guide](/blog/home-office-deduction-for-llc-owners/) help. Travel deductions depend on your "tax home," which is a tricky area, so ask a CPA.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do locum tenens doctors need an LLC?

No, it's not required. Many locum physicians work as sole proprietors. An LLC or PLLC helps most when income is substantial and consistent, you want an S corp election, or you have business liabilities beyond clinical care.

### Can an independent contractor physician form an LLC in every state?

No. Some states require a PLLC or professional corporation for physicians, and some, such as California, generally don't allow physicians to practice through an LLC. Check your state's rules and your medical board's guidance first.

### Does an LLC protect a physician from malpractice lawsuits?

Not from your own negligence. It can protect you from other people's professional errors (if you have partners) and from business debts and contract claims. Malpractice insurance remains your core protection.

### How much does it cost to form an LLC or PLLC as a physician?

State filing fees typically range from under $100 to a few hundred dollars, and formation services add their own fees. PLLCs may carry extra costs, like a license verification or board filing. See our [LLC cost guide](/learn/how-much-does-an-llc-cost/) for ranges.

### Should a locum physician elect S corp status?

Often worth evaluating once net income is well above a reasonable salary. The savings come from reducing self-employment tax on distributions, offset by payroll and compliance costs. A CPA should model it for your situation.

### Do I pay self-employment tax on locum income?

Yes, if you're a 1099 contractor and not operating through an S corp. The rate is 15.3% on net earnings up to the Social Security wage base and 2.9% above it, plus the possible Additional Medicare Tax.

### Which state should a locum physician form their entity in?

Usually the state where you live and hold your primary license. Forming elsewhere seldom provides an advantage for a licensed clinician and can create extra filings.

### Do I need a separate LLC for each state I work in?

Generally no. One entity can often serve multiple states, but some states require foreign registration or have professional-entity rules. Confirm with a local attorney before assuming.
