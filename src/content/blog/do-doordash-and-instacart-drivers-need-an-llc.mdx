If you deliver for DoorDash, Instacart, or both, you have probably wondered whether you should be running your gig work through a company. The short answer: **no, an LLC is not legally required to deliver for either app**, and for many part-time drivers it adds cost without much benefit. But for full-time delivery drivers, multi-app couriers, and anyone building a bigger operation, the picture changes. This guide explains, in plain terms, whether and when an LLC makes sense in 2026.

Here is the practical version. Forming an LLC through a service like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) can cost $0 in service fees on its entry plan, but you will still pay your state's filing fee (roughly $35 to $500 depending on where you live) plus any annual report or franchise tax. That is real money for someone netting a few hundred dollars a week, so it pays to understand exactly what you are buying before you file.

## Do DoorDash and Instacart Drivers Need an LLC? The Short Answer

Neither DoorDash nor Instacart requires you to have a business entity. You sign up as an independent contractor, usually as an individual, and you are automatically treated as a sole proprietor by the IRS. You can start earning with just your Social Security number.

So when people ask whether DoorDash and Instacart drivers need an LLC, the honest answer is "it depends on three things":

1. **How much you earn.** The more net profit you have, the more tax planning options matter.
2. **How much risk you carry.** Driving a car for hours a day is the main exposure, and an LLC does not fix all of it (more on that below).
3. **How you plan to grow.** Hiring helpers, running multiple vehicles, or building a delivery business changes everything.

In my experience, the drivers who benefit most from an entity are not the ones who sign up expecting a magic tax break. They are the ones who treat deliveries like a real business: tracking mileage, separating finances, carrying proper insurance, and planning for growth.

If you are weighing the general question for all gig work, you may also want to read our companion piece on whether [Uber and Lyft drivers need an LLC](/blog/do-uber-and-lyft-drivers-need-an-llc/). This article focuses only on delivery apps, which have different risk and income patterns.

## How DoorDash and Instacart Treat You: Independent Contractor Status

Both platforms classify drivers and shoppers as independent contractors, not employees. That has several consequences:

- **No tax withholding.** Nothing is taken out for federal income tax, Social Security, or Medicare. You are responsible for all of it.
- **1099 reporting.** Platforms generally issue a Form 1099-NEC (or sometimes a 1099-K, depending on how payments are routed) when you cross reporting thresholds. Reporting thresholds have been adjusted by recent federal legislation, and you owe tax on all income whether or not you receive a form.
- **Your own expenses.** You pay for gas, maintenance, phone data, insulated bags, parking, and insurance, though many are deductible.
- **No unemployment or workers' compensation** in most cases, though rules vary by state and are evolving in places like California, Massachusetts, and New York.

An independent contractor LLC does not change this classification. The LLC would simply become the entity that contracts with the app (or, more commonly, you remain the individual who signed up and the LLC sits behind you). Be careful here: the platform's terms of service matter, and some apps tie your account to your personal identity and background check. Read your agreement and ask the platform's support whether business accounts are supported before assuming you can switch.

## What an LLC Protects (and What It Doesn't) for Delivery Drivers

This is the section I wish every new driver read before paying a formation service.

An LLC is a separate legal entity that can shield your personal assets from business debts and certain business liabilities. You can learn the basics in our guide on [what an LLC is](/learn/what-is-an-llc/).

**What an LLC can help with:**

- Business contract debts, such as a lease for a delivery vehicle or equipment financing, if structured correctly and not personally guaranteed.
- Claims arising from the actions of employees or subcontractors you hire, when you are not personally at fault.
- Separating your personal bank accounts and records from your delivery income, which makes taxes and audits simpler.

**What an LLC does not do:**

- **It does not protect you from your own negligence.** If you cause a car accident while delivering, you can still be personally liable. Courts do not treat "I was driving for my LLC" as a shield for your own negligent driving.
- **It does not replace auto insurance.** Insurance is your first and most important protection on the road.
- **It does not lower your taxes by default.** A single-member LLC is "disregarded" for tax purposes, so you still report income on Schedule C and pay self-employment tax.

### The insurance gap is the real risk

This is where I have seen too many gig workers get hurt. A standard personal auto policy often excludes commercial use or delivery for pay. DoorDash states that it provides certain excess auto liability coverage while a Dasher is actively on a delivery, but that coverage has conditions, and it typically does not cover you while you are waiting for orders. Instacart publishes its own insurance terms for shoppers, which also have limits and conditions. Review the current terms on each platform's official site.

Before spending money on an LLC, call your insurance agent and ask about a rideshare/delivery endorsement or a commercial policy. If you have to choose between spending $300 on an LLC and spending that on closing a coverage gap, close the coverage gap first. An LLC will not pay a medical bill after an accident your policy refuses to cover.

## Delivery Driver Taxes in 2026: What an LLC Does and Doesn't Change

Taxes are where most drivers expect the LLC to help. In practice, the default LLC does not change your federal tax bill.

### Self-employment tax

As a contractor, you owe self-employment tax of 15.3% (12.4% Social Security up to the annual wage base, plus 2.9% Medicare) on 92.35% of your net earnings. That applies whether you are a sole proprietor or a single-member LLC. For a deeper walk-through, see our guide to [self-employment tax for LLC owners](/blog/self-employment-tax-for-llc-owners/).

### Deductions are the same either way

You can deduct ordinary and necessary business expenses with or without an LLC. For delivery drivers, the big ones are:

- **Vehicle expenses.** You can choose the IRS standard mileage rate or actual expenses (gas, repairs, insurance, depreciation). The IRS publishes the rate each year, and it is worth checking the current figure for 2026. See [IRS Publication 463](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p463) for the rules. Choosing the wrong method in the first year can limit your options later, so ask a tax professional.
- **Phone and data plan** (business-use percentage).
- **Insulated bags, hot bags, and supplies.**
- **Parking and tolls** while on deliveries.
- **Accounting software and tax preparation fees.**

The single most valuable habit is a **mileage log**. Mileage while on the clock is often the largest deduction a driver has. Apps and spreadsheets both work, but the IRS expects contemporaneous records.

### A worked example

Say you net $42,000 in delivery earnings after vehicle and other expenses. As a sole proprietor or single-member LLC, you would pay self-employment tax on roughly $38,787 (92.35% of $42,000), which is about $5,935, plus income tax on your net profit after the standard deduction and any qualified business income deduction you are eligible for. The LLC itself does not change either number. These figures are illustrations only; your actual tax depends on your filing status, other income, and state.

### When an S-corp election might matter

At higher profit levels, some owners elect S-corporation tax treatment to split income into a "reasonable salary" and distributions, which can reduce self-employment tax. This generally becomes worth considering only when net profit is comfortably above what you would pay a reasonable employee, and it adds payroll, filing, and compliance costs. For most delivery drivers earning less than about $60,000 to $80,000 in net profit, the extra cost eats the savings. Read our breakdown of [LLC vs S-Corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) and the guide on [reasonable salary for S-Corp LLC owners](/blog/reasonable-salary-for-s-corp-llc-owners/) before making a move, and talk to a CPA first.

### Estimated quarterly payments

With no withholding, you generally need to make quarterly estimated tax payments to the IRS. The [IRS Self-Employed Individuals Tax Center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employed-individuals-tax-center) explains the process. Our [LLC quarterly tax payments guide](/blog/llc-quarterly-tax-payments-guide/) covers the due dates and safe-harbor rules.

## When Forming an LLC Makes Sense for Delivery Work

Here is my honest framework after years of advising small operators.

**An LLC probably makes sense if you:**

- Deliver full-time (30+ hours a week) and net more than roughly $40,000 a year.
- Hire others (for example, a spouse, relative, or contractor) to deliver or help with fulfillment.
- Own or lease more than one vehicle for your delivery operation.
- Are expanding into direct local delivery contracts with restaurants, grocers, or small businesses outside the apps.
- Want a clean separation of finances and a professional footing with banks and clients.

**An LLC probably does not make sense if you:**

- Deliver casually for a few hours a week or as a side income under a few thousand dollars.
- Do not yet have adequate auto insurance.
- Live in a high-cost-entity state (such as California, with its annual minimum franchise tax) and your profits are modest.

## Steps to Set Up an LLC If You Decide to Do It

If the numbers work for you, the process is straightforward:

1. **Pick your state.** For most local delivery drivers, this is the state where you live and drive. Forming elsewhere (for example, Delaware) usually adds cost without benefit for local gig work. See our [best state to form an LLC](/blog/best-state-to-form-an-llc/) guide.
2. **Choose a name** and check availability with your state.
3. **Appoint a registered agent.** Read [what a registered agent is](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/) if this is new to you.
4. **File Articles of Organization** with your state, directly or through a service.
5. **Get an EIN** from the IRS. It is free; see our guide on [getting an EIN for your LLC using the free IRS method](/blog/how-to-get-an-ein-for-your-llc-free-irs-method/).
6. **Draft an operating agreement.** Even for single-member LLCs, it helps support your liability protection. See the [operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/).
7. **Open a business bank account** and run all delivery income and expenses through it.
8. **Update your insurance** and check whether your delivery apps allow business payout accounts.

Keep your finances strictly separate. Courts can "pierce the corporate veil" when owners mix personal and business money, which can erase the liability protection you paid for.

## Common Mistakes Delivery Drivers Make

- **Forming an LLC to "save on taxes" without a plan.** The default LLC does not reduce your federal taxes.
- **Skipping insurance.** As covered above, this is the largest unprotected risk.
- **Mixing personal and business money.** It undermines the LLC and makes tax prep painful.
- **Ignoring state and local rules.** Some cities require business licenses, and your state may have its own registration and tax requirements.
- **Not saving for taxes.** A common rule of thumb is to set aside a meaningful share of every payout (many drivers use 20% to 30%), then adjust with a tax professional based on your situation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need an LLC to deliver for DoorDash or Instacart?

No. Both platforms let you sign up as an individual independent contractor, and you are treated as a sole proprietor for tax purposes. An LLC is optional.

### How much does it cost to start an LLC as a delivery driver?

You will pay a state filing fee, typically between about $35 and $500, plus any annual report or franchise tax. Formation services such as [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) and [LegalZoom](/go/legalzoom/) offer entry plans with $0 in service fees, though paid tiers and add-ons cost more. Check current pricing before you buy.

### Will an LLC lower my taxes as a DoorDash or Instacart driver?

Not by default. A single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietorship, so you still pay self-employment tax on your net earnings. Possible savings from an S-corp election generally only appear at higher profit levels. Speak with a CPA before electing.

### Does an LLC protect me if I get in an accident while delivering?

Not from your own negligence. You can still be personally responsible for harm you cause while driving. Adequate auto insurance, including coverage for delivery use, is your primary protection.

### What happens if I deliver without an LLC?

Nothing unusual. You are a sole proprietor, you report income on Schedule C, and you pay self-employment and income tax. The main difference is that you have no entity separating your personal assets from business claims.

### Can I use my personal car for delivery if I have an LLC?

Yes, but check your auto policy first. Many personal policies exclude delivery for pay, and the LLC does not change that. Ask your insurer about a delivery endorsement or commercial coverage.

### Can I deduct my mileage if I don't have an LLC?

Yes. Eligible business mileage is deductible for sole proprietors and LLC owners alike. See [IRS Publication 463](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p463) for recordkeeping requirements.

### When should I convert from a sole proprietor to an LLC?

Consider it when your net profit rises, you start hiring help, you add vehicles, or you want cleaner separation of finances. Our guide on [converting a sole proprietorship to an LLC](/blog/how-to-convert-sole-proprietorship-to-llc/) explains the steps.
