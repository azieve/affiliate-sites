Opening a dental practice usually means signing for equipment financing, a lease, and a build-out before you've seen a single patient. Most new owners take on several hundred thousand dollars of obligations in their first year. That is why the entity you choose matters, and why an LLC for dentists (PLLC) is one of the first decisions to get right.

Here is the catch most generic LLC guides skip: **dentistry is a licensed profession, and many states don't let you use a standard LLC to practice it.** Some require a professional LLC (PLLC) or professional corporation (PC). A few restrict dental practice entities even further. This guide covers what a dental PLLC does and doesn't protect, how the rules differ by state, how the tax treatment works in 2026, and how to file without overpaying. For the filing itself, a service such as [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) can handle standard paperwork. Some states add steps that only your dental board or an attorney can resolve.

## What a PLLC Is and Why Dentists Often Need One

A PLLC is a limited liability company reserved for licensed professionals: dentists, physicians, attorneys, accountants, and similar occupations. It looks like a regular LLC in structure, with members, an operating agreement, and pass-through taxation. It differs in who can own it and what the state requires before it will register it.

Typical PLLC requirements for a dental practice include:

- **Licensed owners only.** Members must hold an active dental license in the state. A spouse, investor, or office manager without a license generally cannot own an interest.
- **Board approval or a license certificate.** Many states ask for a certificate of good standing or a letter from the state dental board before the Secretary of State will accept your articles.
- **Restricted name.** Names often must include "PLLC" or "Professional Limited Liability Company" and may need to reflect the licensed profession.
- **Limited purpose clause.** The articles typically state that the company exists only to render dental services.

If you want the broader background first, our guide on [what an LLC is](/learn/what-is-an-llc/) covers the basics, and our article on [LLCs for medical practices](/blog/llc-for-medical-practice/) covers the parallel rules for physicians.

In my experience, the most expensive mistake new dentists make is filing a plain LLC with an online service, discovering months later that their state's dental practice act required a professional entity, and then having to re-form, re-bank, and re-credential with insurers. Check your state's rules before you file anything.

## Does Your State Allow a Dental PLLC? Check Before You File

Rules vary widely, so treat the examples below as orientation, not advice for your state.

- **Texas, Florida, and New York** have statutes allowing licensed professionals, including dentists, to organize as professional LLCs, with licensing and naming conditions attached.
- **California** is a notable exception. California generally does not allow dentists to practice through an LLC. Dentists there typically use a dental professional corporation. See our [California LLC guide](/start-llc/california/) for the general rules, but confirm dental specifics with the Dental Board of California or an attorney.
- **Other states** may allow either a PLLC or a professional corporation, and a few permit a regular LLC with a professional-services rider.

Before filing, do three things:

1. Read your state's dental practice act and entity statute, or ask your dental board which entity types are permitted.
2. Confirm whether you need a pre-approval or license verification from the board before filing.
3. Ask your malpractice carrier whether they have a preference. Some write policies differently for PLLCs than for sole practitioners.

The American Dental Association ([ADA.org](https://www.ada.org)) publishes practice-management resources and state-level guidance that can help you orient yourself before talking to an attorney.

## What a Dental PLLC Protects You From (and What It Doesn't)

This is the point many dentists get wrong, so I'll be blunt about it.

**A PLLC protects your personal assets from business debts and from the acts of your partners and employees.** That covers things like:

- Your office lease and equipment loans (unless you signed personal guarantees, which most lenders require)
- Vendor and lab bills
- Claims arising from a partner's or associate's malpractice, in many states

**A PLLC does not shield you from your own professional negligence.** If you personally commit malpractice, your license obligations and personal liability still apply. Professional entity statutes consistently preserve this. That is why malpractice insurance is non-negotiable, not optional.

Two practical consequences follow:

- **Personal guarantees survive.** Banks and equipment lenders usually require them from the owner-dentist, so your PLLC may not insulate you from the largest debts you carry.
- **Keep entity formalities tight.** Separate bank accounts, no commingling, and a written operating agreement protect the liability shield. Our [LLC operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/) walks through what to include. For a dental PLLC, add buy-sell provisions covering death, disability, license loss, and departure of a partner.

## Taxes for a Dental PLLC in 2026

By default, a single-member PLLC is a "disregarded entity" taxed like a sole proprietor, and a multi-member PLLC is taxed as a partnership. In both cases profit passes through to your personal return. Our overview of [how LLCs are taxed](/blog/llc-taxes-how-are-llcs-taxed/) explains the mechanics.

The default has a cost: you pay self-employment tax (15.3% up to the Social Security wage base, then 2.9% Medicare above it) on net earnings. For a dentist netting $250,000, that adds up quickly.

### The S-corp election

Many dentists elect S-corporation tax treatment by filing IRS Form 2553, while still being legally a PLLC. Under an S-corp election you pay yourself a reasonable W-2 salary, subject to payroll taxes, and take remaining profit as distributions that avoid self-employment tax. The IRS explains the rules on its [S corporation page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/s-corporations).

An illustrative example: a solo dentist with $240,000 in net practice income pays herself a $140,000 salary and takes $100,000 as distributions. The payroll-tax savings on that $100,000 can be meaningful, but they are offset by payroll processing costs, state-level treatment, and added compliance. The numbers differ in every case.

Two cautions:

- **"Reasonable salary" is scrutinized.** The IRS expects compensation consistent with what a dentist doing your work would earn. Setting it too low invites trouble. Our guide on [reasonable salary for S-corp owners](/blog/reasonable-salary-for-s-corp-llc-owners/) goes into the numbers.
- **Not every state treats S-corps the same.** Some states tax them differently or add entity-level fees.

For a full comparison, read [LLC vs S-corp: which is better for taxes](/blog/llc-vs-s-corp-which-is-better-for-taxes/), and see [how to file Form 2553](/blog/how-to-file-form-2553-for-an-llc/). Because the right salary and election timing depend on your numbers, work with a CPA who knows dental practices. This is general education, not tax advice.

**Board and licensing costs.** Certificate of good standing or license verification fees are usually modest, often $10 to $50 or so, but check your board.

## Step-by-Step: Setting Up Your Dental PLLC

1. **Confirm the permitted entity type** with your state dental board or an attorney.
2. **Choose a compliant name**, including the required PLLC designator, and check availability.
3. **Obtain any required board approval** or license certificate.
4. **File articles of organization** (often called a certificate of formation) with the Secretary of State, directly or through a service like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/).
5. **Appoint a registered agent.** Our guide on [what a registered agent is](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/) explains why you need one.
6. **Draft an operating agreement** with buy-sell and license-loss provisions.
7. **Get an EIN** from the IRS (free at IRS.gov).
8. **Open a dedicated business bank account.**
9. **Update your licenses, DEA registration, NPI, and payer contracts** to the new entity.
10. **Decide on S-corp treatment** with your CPA, and file Form 2553 within the deadline if you elect it.

On beneficial ownership reporting: federal BOI rules for domestic companies changed significantly in 2025, and requirements may continue to evolve in 2026. Check our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) and FinCEN's site for the current status before assuming anything either way.

## Ongoing Compliance and Common Gotchas

- **Annual reports and fees.** Most states require annual or biennial filings, and professional entities sometimes have additional renewal steps.
- **License renewals.** If a member's license lapses, the PLLC may lose its eligibility to operate. Build this into your calendar.
- **Insurance.** Carry professional liability, general liability, cyber coverage (patient records are a target), and workers' compensation as required.
- **Ownership transfers.** Selling to a non-dentist, including many private-equity-backed dental service organizations, often requires restructuring. State rules on the corporate practice of dentistry differ, so consult counsel before any sale or DSO deal.
- **Switching later.** If you started as a sole proprietor, see our article on [converting a sole proprietorship to an LLC](/blog/how-to-convert-sole-proprietorship-to-llc/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a dentist form a regular LLC instead of a PLLC?

It depends on the state. Some states require licensed professionals to use a PLLC or professional corporation, while others allow a standard LLC with restrictions. Some, like California, don't permit dental practice through an LLC at all. Confirm with your dental board before filing.

### How much does it cost to form a PLLC for a dental practice?

State fees commonly range from about $50 to a few hundred dollars, plus any board verification fee and optional formation-service fees. Expect more if your state requires publication. Verify current figures with your state and your chosen provider.

### Does a PLLC protect me from malpractice lawsuits?

Not from your own malpractice. A PLLC generally shields you from business debts and from other members' or employees' wrongdoing, but you remain personally responsible for your own professional negligence. Malpractice insurance is essential.

### Can a non-dentist own part of my dental PLLC?

Generally no. Most states limit ownership of dental professional entities to licensed dentists, though details vary by state and sometimes allow limited exceptions. Consult an attorney before bringing in investors.

### Should my dental PLLC elect S-corp status?

Often worth evaluating once net income is high enough to justify payroll costs. The election can reduce self-employment tax, but you must pay a reasonable salary and handle added compliance. A CPA can model it for your income.

### Do I need a separate EIN for my dental PLLC?

Yes, in most cases. A PLLC with employees, or one electing S-corp status, needs its own EIN, and having one keeps business and personal finances separate. You can apply for free through the IRS.

### What happens if my dental license lapses?

In many states, the PLLC can no longer render dental services through that member, and the entity's good standing may be affected. Review your operating agreement's license-loss provisions and notify your board and insurer promptly.

### Can I use an online service to form a dental PLLC?

Often yes, for the filing itself, but confirm the service supports professional entities in your state and the board-certificate step. For complex situations, an attorney-backed option such as [LLC Attorney](/go/llc-attorney/) may be a better fit.
