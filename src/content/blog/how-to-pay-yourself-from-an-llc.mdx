## The Short Answer: Your Tax Classification Decides How You Get Paid

An LLC is a legal structure, not a tax classification. The IRS treats it differently depending on its membership and elections. The [IRS LLC guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc) explains the defaults:

| LLC type | Default tax treatment | How you take money out |
|---|---|---|
| Single-member LLC | Disregarded entity (Schedule C) | Owner's draw |
| Multi-member LLC | Partnership (Form 1065, K-1s) | Draws, distributions, guaranteed payments |
| LLC taxed as S-corp | S-corporation (Form 1120-S) | W-2 salary plus distributions |
| LLC taxed as C-corp | C-corporation | W-2 salary and dividends |

If you aren't sure which one you are, our [LLC taxes explainer](/blog/llc-taxes-how-are-llcs-taxed/) walks through each classification.

## Method 1: Owner's Draw (Single-Member LLCs and Partnerships)

An owner's draw is the simplest way to pay yourself from an LLC. You transfer money from the business bank account to your personal account. There is no payroll, no W-2, and no withholding.

**How it works in practice**

Say your single-member LLC earns $90,000 in net profit in 2026. You might draw $5,000 a month for personal expenses. The draws themselves aren't taxed. You owe tax on the *profit*, whether or not you withdraw it. If the business earns $90,000 and you draw only $40,000, you still owe tax on the full $90,000.

**The tax bill on profit**

- **Income tax** at your ordinary rate on net profit.
- **Self-employment tax** of 15.3% on net earnings (12.4% Social Security up to the annual wage base, plus 2.9% Medicare). The Social Security wage base is $184,500 for 2026, according to the [Social Security Administration](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/cola/cbb.html).
- A possible **qualified business income (QBI) deduction** of up to 20% for eligible pass-through income, subject to income limits and other rules.

On $90,000 of profit, self-employment tax alone is roughly $12,700 before the deduction for half of it. Our [self-employment tax guide](/blog/self-employment-tax-for-llc-owners/) shows how to estimate this.

**Pros and cons of an owner's draw**

- *Pros:* Flexible, no payroll costs, simple bookkeeping.
- *Cons:* No tax is withheld, so you must pay estimated taxes. All profit is subject to self-employment tax.

## Method 2: Distributions and Guaranteed Payments (Multi-Member LLCs)

Partnership-taxed LLCs have two distinct ways to pay members, and mixing them up causes real trouble.

**LLC distributions** are payments of profit to members, usually in proportion to ownership percentages as set out in your operating agreement. Distributions aren't deductions for the business. Each member is taxed on their allocated share of profit through a Schedule K-1, regardless of how much cash they actually receive.

**Guaranteed payments** are payments to a member for services or for use of capital, made without regard to the LLC's profit. They work a bit like a salary, but with differences:

- The LLC deducts guaranteed payments as a business expense.
- The member reports them as ordinary income and pays self-employment tax on them.
- No payroll taxes are withheld. Partners generally can't be W-2 employees of their own partnership.

**Example:** Maya and Jordan own a 50/50 design studio LLC. Maya works full time in the business and Jordan is a passive investor. The operating agreement gives Maya a $4,000 monthly guaranteed payment for her work. Remaining profit is split 50/50 as distributions. Without the guaranteed payment, Jordan would receive half of the profit for doing none of the work, which is why the structure matters.

## Method 3: Salary Plus Distributions (S-Corp Election)

Once profit climbs, many owners elect to have their LLC taxed as an S-corp by filing IRS Form 2553. Then you become an employee of your own company and must pay yourself a W-2 salary. Profit above that salary can be taken as distributions, which aren't subject to self-employment tax.

**The "reasonable compensation" rule**

The IRS requires S-corp owners who work in the business to receive reasonable pay for the services they perform. The [IRS reasonable compensation guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/wage-compensation-for-s-corporation-officers) says salary should reflect what comparable businesses pay for similar work. There is no fixed percentage. The popular "60/40" rule of thumb is not an IRS standard.

**Illustration with simplified numbers**

Your LLC nets $150,000 before owner pay.

| | Default (sole proprietor-style) | S-corp election |
|---|---|---|
| Owner salary | n/a | $75,000 |
| Distribution | n/a | $75,000 |
| Earnings subject to SE/payroll tax | ~$150,000 | $75,000 |
| Approx. payroll/SE tax (15.3%) | ~$21,200 (after 92.35% adjustment) | ~$11,500 |
| Estimated difference | | Roughly $9,000+ potential savings |

These figures are illustrative only. Actual savings depend on your state, your reasonable salary, and added costs.

**Costs that offset the savings**

- Payroll software or service: commonly $400 to $1,000+ per year.
- A separate business tax return (Form 1120-S), often $800 to $2,500 from a CPA.
- State unemployment tax and, in some states, additional franchise or entity-level taxes.

For most owners, the S-corp election tends to start making sense once net profit is consistently above roughly $60,000 to $80,000, but that's a guideline and not a rule. ## Taxes You Must Plan For When Paying Yourself

Because LLC owners usually don't have tax withheld, you need a system.

**1. Quarterly estimated taxes.** Sole proprietors and partners generally pay estimated tax on 1040-ES due dates: April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15. Missing them can trigger underpayment penalties. Our [LLC quarterly tax payments guide](/blog/llc-quarterly-tax-payments-guide/) covers safe-harbor rules.

**2. The "tax holdback" habit.** I've seen too many business owners spend their entire draw and then face a surprise April bill. A common approach is moving 25% to 30% of every deposit into a separate tax savings account. Adjust the percentage with your accountant.

**3. State taxes.** Some states charge an annual LLC fee or franchise tax regardless of profit, such as California's $800 minimum. Others tax pass-through income at the entity level. Check your state's rules.

**4. Health insurance and retirement.** Self-employed owners can often deduct health insurance premiums. A SEP IRA or Solo 401(k) may also let you shelter part of your income. These interact with your pay method, so review them with a tax professional.

## Mistakes That Can Hurt You (Including Your Liability Protection)

**Commingling funds.** Paying personal bills from the business account, or the reverse, is the classic error. Courts can "pierce the corporate veil" when an owner treats the LLC as a personal wallet, which could expose personal assets to business debts. Open a dedicated account; see our [LLC bank account guide](/blog/llc-bank-account-how-to-open-one/).

**Paying yourself a W-2 salary without an S-corp election.** A single-member LLC taxed as a disregarded entity can't put its owner on payroll. Doing it anyway creates tax headaches.

**Setting an unreasonably low S-corp salary.** The IRS audits this. A $20,000 salary for a $200,000 consultant is a red flag.

**Withdrawing more than the business can afford.** Large draws that leave the LLC unable to pay bills or taxes can cause trouble, and distributions that leave a company insolvent can create legal exposure under some states' laws.

**No records.** Document every draw, distribution, and payment with a clear memo or accounting entry. Bookkeeping software makes this easy.

**Not forming the LLC properly in the first place.** Skipping an EIN, operating agreement, or business bank account makes every payment harder to track. ## A Practical Step-by-Step Process

1. **Confirm your tax classification.** Single-member, partnership, or S-corp?
2. **Open a separate business bank account** and route all revenue through it.
3. **Set your pay method.** Draws for disregarded entities, guaranteed payments and distributions for partnerships, salary plus distributions for S-corps.
4. **Pay yourself on a regular schedule.** A set amount monthly or biweekly makes personal budgeting easier.
5. **Hold back tax money** from each deposit.
6. **Make quarterly estimated payments** (or run payroll with withholding in an S-corp).
7. **Review annually** with a CPA to adjust pay as profit changes.

Many owners start with simple draws in year one, then reassess once profit stabilizes. That's a sensible approach, and one that a lot of new owners in 2026 are using as economic conditions keep margins unpredictable for small businesses.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I pay myself from a single-member LLC?

Use an owner's draw: transfer money from your business account to your personal account. You report all business profit on Schedule C and pay income and self-employment taxes on it, not on the draw itself. Make quarterly estimated tax payments.

### Can I put myself on payroll in my LLC?

Only if your LLC has elected to be taxed as an S-corp or C-corp. A default single-member LLC or partnership generally can't pay its owners W-2 wages. Partners receive guaranteed payments instead.

### How often should I pay myself from my LLC?

There's no legal requirement. Many owners pay themselves monthly or twice a month for consistency, leaving enough in the account for expenses and taxes. S-corp owners on payroll follow a regular payroll schedule.

### Do I pay taxes on money I leave in the LLC?

Yes, for pass-through LLCs. You're taxed on profit as it's earned, whether or not you withdraw it. This is why leaving cash in the business doesn't avoid a tax bill.

### What's the difference between an owner's draw and a distribution?

"Owner's draw" typically refers to single-member LLCs, while "distribution" is used for multi-member LLCs and S-corps. Both are withdrawals of profit that aren't payroll. Tax is based on profit, not on the amount withdrawn.

### How much salary should I pay myself in an S-corp?

The IRS expects reasonable compensation for the work you do, based on factors such as your duties, training, experience, and what similar businesses pay. Consult a CPA to document a defensible number.

### Can I pay myself if my LLC isn't profitable?

You can take draws as long as the business has cash and can still pay its debts, but draws don't change your tax liability. Taking money out during a loss year usually reduces your capital in the business, and aggressive withdrawals can create legal and cash flow problems.

### Do I have to pay myself at all?

No law forces a pass-through LLC owner to take money out. But in an S-corp where you work in the business, you generally must take a reasonable salary.
