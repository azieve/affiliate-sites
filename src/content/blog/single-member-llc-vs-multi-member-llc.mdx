## What Is a Single Member LLC?

A **single member LLC (SMLLC)** is a limited liability company with exactly one owner — referred to as the "member." It blends the personal liability protection of a corporation with the simplicity of a sole proprietorship.

From the IRS's perspective, a single member LLC is treated as a **"disregarded entity"** by default. The business doesn't file a separate federal income tax return. Instead, profits and losses flow directly onto the owner's personal return via **Schedule C** — the same form used by freelancers and independent contractors. Despite the word "disregarded," the LLC's legal separation from its owner is entirely real. Your personal assets — home, car, savings accounts — are shielded from business debts and lawsuits, provided you maintain proper separation between personal and business finances.

**Who typically forms a single member LLC:**
- Freelancers and independent contractors
- Solo consultants and coaches
- Real estate investors holding a single property
- Online business owners and content creators
- Side hustlers transitioning away from sole proprietorship status

According to the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/single-member-limited-liability-companies), the majority of LLCs in the United States are single-member entities — a direct reflection of how many Americans are building businesses on their own.

## What Is a Multi-Member LLC?

A **multi-member LLC** has two or more members. It can be owned by individuals, other LLCs, corporations, trusts, or foreign entities. There is no statutory cap on the number of members (though S-Corp elections impose their own restrictions — more on that below).

By default, the IRS treats a multi-member LLC as a **partnership** for tax purposes. Unlike a single member LLC, a multi-member LLC *must* file a separate federal tax return — **Form 1065** (U.S. Return of Partnership Income) — and issue a **Schedule K-1** to each member reflecting their share of income, deductions, and credits. Members then report their K-1 figures on their individual returns.

This structure creates more administrative work than a single member LLC, but it also enables more sophisticated profit-sharing arrangements and clearly documented ownership stakes.

**Who typically forms a multi-member LLC:**
- Business partners starting a company together
- Married couples co-owning a business
- Real estate investors pooling capital
- Family businesses with shared ownership
- Friends launching a restaurant, studio, or startup

The [U.S. Small Business Administration](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/choose-business-structure) consistently ranks LLCs among the most popular business structures for new ventures — and the multi-member variation is particularly attractive when shared investment and decision-making are involved.

## How Each Structure Is Taxed

Tax treatment is where the single member LLC vs multi-member LLC distinction matters most in practice — and where most business owners get blindsided.

### Single Member LLC Taxes

As a disregarded entity, single member LLC taxes flow entirely through to the owner's personal return. You report income and deductible expenses on Schedule C, and your net profit is subject to:

- **Federal income tax** at your marginal rate (10%–37% in 2026, depending on total income)
- **Self-employment (SE) tax** at 15.3% on net earnings up to the Social Security wage base (which adjusts annually; for 2025 it was $176,100), then 2.9% on earnings above that threshold

On $100,000 of net profit, a solo LLC owner would owe roughly $14,130 in SE taxes (after the allowable above-the-line deduction for half of SE tax paid), plus federal income tax on top. That's a meaningful tax burden that many first-time LLC owners don't fully anticipate.

**S-Corp election option:** A single member LLC can elect to be taxed as an S-Corporation by filing IRS Form 2553. This structure lets the owner pay themselves a "reasonable salary" (subject to payroll taxes) while taking remaining profits as distributions — which are *not* subject to SE tax. On $100,000 in annual profit, this strategy can realistically save $8,000–$12,000 per year once the business is earning consistently enough to justify the added payroll administration. See our [LLC vs S-Corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) guide for a full breakdown.

### Multi-Member LLC Taxes

Multi-member LLC taxes involve a layer of complexity that single member structures don't. The LLC files Form 1065 annually, and each member receives a Schedule K-1 showing their distributive share of income, deductions, and credits — regardless of whether any cash was actually distributed.

This last point catches many new multi-member LLC owners completely off guard. If the LLC earns $200,000 in net profit but retains $100,000 for reinvestment and distributes only $100,000, each member still pays income tax on their full allocated share of the $200,000. This **"phantom income" problem** is one of the most important things to address in your operating agreement — typically with a provision requiring the LLC to distribute at minimum enough cash to cover each member's estimated tax liability.

Under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, eligible pass-through business owners (including both single and multi-member LLC owners) can potentially deduct up to 20% of qualified business income (QBI) under IRC Section 199A. The rules are complex and phase out at higher income levels, but this **pass-through taxation** deduction represents meaningful savings for eligible businesses.

**Multi-member S-Corp election:** Multi-member LLCs can also elect S-Corp status, subject to strict IRS requirements: no more than 100 shareholders, all must be U.S. citizens or permanent residents, only one class of stock, and no non-resident alien members.

## Operating Agreements: Non-Negotiable for Multi-Member LLCs

An [LLC operating agreement](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/) is the internal governance document that defines how your LLC runs — ownership percentages, profit distribution, voting rights, management structure, and what happens when a member wants to exit or dies.

**For multi-member LLCs, the operating agreement isn't just recommended — it's essential.** Without one, your LLC defaults to state statutory rules, which often don't reflect your actual intentions. Some states default to equal ownership splits regardless of capital contributed. Others require unanimous member consent for major decisions, which can paralyze a functioning business.

In my experience advising business owners over the years, I've seen far too many partnerships — between close friends, siblings, spouses — implode not because the business failed, but because they never formalized how decisions get made, how profits get split, or what happens when one person wants out. A solid operating agreement won't prevent all disputes, but it gives you a framework to resolve them without expensive litigation.

**Key provisions every multi-member operating agreement should address:**

1. **Ownership percentages** — exact membership interest for each member
2. **Capital contributions** — initial investment from each member, and rules for future contributions
3. **Profit and loss allocation** — can differ from ownership percentage
4. **Tax distribution policy** — cash distributions to cover members' tax obligations (addresses phantom income)
5. **Management structure** — member-managed vs. manager-managed (see our [LLC Member vs Manager Managed](/blog/llc-member-vs-manager-managed/) guide)
6. **Voting rights** — what requires unanimous consent vs. simple majority
7. **Transfer restrictions** — can a member sell their interest, and to whom?
8. **Buyout provisions** — how a departing member's interest is valued and purchased
9. **Dissolution terms** — what triggers dissolution and how assets are distributed

**For single member LLCs:** Most states don't require a written operating agreement for a solo LLC, but having one matters anyway. It reinforces the business-personal separation that protects your liability shield, documents your authority to act on behalf of the LLC, and is routinely required by banks when opening a business checking account. ## Liability Protection: Are Both Structures Equally Protected?

Both single member and multi-member LLCs provide liability protection — your personal assets are shielded from business debts and judgments against the company. But there's a practical nuance worth understanding.

In some states, courts have shown a greater willingness to "pierce the corporate veil" of single member LLCs — essentially disregarding the LLC structure and holding the owner personally liable. The reasoning is that with only one person, the line between business and personal activity can blur more easily. This is not a reason to avoid a single member LLC, but it is a reason to be scrupulous about maintaining proper records and separation.

**Steps to protect your liability shield in either structure:**
- Maintain separate bank accounts (no personal transactions through the business account, ever)
- Sign all contracts in the LLC's name, not your personal name
- File all required annual reports and pay state fees on time
- Avoid undercapitalizing the LLC (don't drain it of assets immediately after formation)

States like Wyoming and Delaware have strong statutory protections for single member LLCs, including robust **charging order** protection — a legal mechanism that prevents a creditor of an individual member from seizing the LLC's assets directly. If asset protection is a priority, these jurisdictions are worth examining. For a full cost comparison by state, see [How Much Does an LLC Cost?](/learn/how-much-does-an-llc-cost/)

It's also worth noting that in 2026, the **Beneficial Ownership Information (BOI) reporting requirements** under the Corporate Transparency Act apply to most LLCs — both single member and multi-member. Staying current on these compliance obligations is a core part of maintaining your LLC in good standing.

## Which Structure Should You Choose?

Here's a practical framework for making this decision:

**Choose a single member LLC if:**
- You're launching and operating the business entirely on your own
- You want the lowest possible tax filing complexity
- You're a freelancer, independent consultant, or solo operator
- You don't need to split equity or profits with anyone else
- You want to minimize annual accounting and compliance costs

**Choose a multi-member LLC if:**
- Two or more people are co-owning the business from the start
- You're pooling capital from multiple investors or co-founders
- You're a married couple running a business together (check your state's qualified joint venture rules)
- You want formally documented ownership percentages and profit-sharing arrangements
- You're building something that may eventually bring in outside investors or additional equity holders

**What about changing later?** It's possible to convert a single member LLC to a multi-member LLC by adding a member, or to remove a member and revert to single member status. But these transitions have real tax consequences — [adding a member to an LLC](/blog/how-to-add-a-member-to-an-llc/) changes your default federal tax classification from disregarded entity to partnership, which can require mid-year accounting adjustments, a new EIN, and the obligation to file Form 1065 going forward. Plan structural changes in advance with a CPA.

When evaluating single member LLC vs multi-member LLC options, the right answer almost always comes down to one question: **are you actually starting this business with other people?** If yes, a multi-member structure is the appropriate choice. If no, the single member LLC gives you all the protection you need with considerably less administrative overhead.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a single member LLC be converted to a multi-member LLC?

Yes. You add a member to your existing LLC by amending your Articles of Organization, updating or drafting a formal operating agreement, and issuing a membership interest to the new member. The significant consequence is that adding a member changes your LLC's default federal tax classification from a disregarded entity to a partnership — meaning you'll need to file Form 1065 going forward and obtain an EIN if you don't already have one. Coordinate this transition with a CPA to avoid mid-year accounting complications.

### Does a multi-member LLC need an EIN?

Yes, always. A multi-member LLC is treated as a partnership for federal tax purposes and must have an Employer Identification Number to file Form 1065 and issue Schedule K-1s to members. Single member LLCs only require an EIN if they have employees or elect corporate tax treatment — though obtaining one is still a good idea for any LLC, since most banks require it to open a business checking account.

### How are multi-member LLC profits taxed if cash isn't distributed?

This is one of the most common surprises in multi-member LLC taxes. Members pay income tax on their *allocable share* of profits — not just on actual distributions received. If the LLC earns $200,000 but retains $100,000 and distributes only $100,000 in cash, each member still owes tax on their full allocated portion of the $200,000. This "phantom income" issue should be preemptively addressed in your operating agreement, typically with a mandatory tax distribution clause requiring the LLC to distribute enough cash to cover each member's estimated tax obligation on allocated income.

### Do I need an operating agreement for a single member LLC?

Most states don't require it, but you should have one anyway. A single member LLC operating agreement establishes your management authority in writing, reinforces your liability shield, and is typically required by banks when opening a business account. It also documents your LLC's ownership structure and internal rules — useful if your business is ever audited or drawn into litigation. Formation services like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) include an operating agreement template as part of the formation package.

### Can a husband and wife form a single member LLC together?

In most states, a jointly owned LLC is a multi-member LLC by definition and defaults to partnership tax treatment. However, in community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin), married couples can elect to treat their jointly-owned LLC as a "qualified joint venture" — splitting the business on two Schedule C forms and bypassing the Form 1065 partnership return entirely. This election is only available in community property states and only for spouses who are the LLC's sole owners.

### What are the ongoing annual compliance requirements for each structure?

Both types share the same foundational requirements: paying annual state fees, filing annual reports (where required by state law), maintaining a registered agent, and keeping records current. The primary difference is in federal tax filing: single member LLCs report on the owner's Schedule C, while multi-member LLCs file a separate Form 1065 partnership return and issue K-1s to each member. That partnership return typically costs $500–$2,000+ annually in CPA preparation fees — a real ongoing cost difference that should factor into your structure decision.

### Which structure provides better asset protection?

Both structures provide equivalent statutory liability protection when properly maintained. However, multi-member LLCs can benefit from stronger "charging order" protection in some states — where a personal creditor of one member can only obtain a right to future distributions, not direct control of LLC assets or management rights. This makes it substantially harder for a creditor to force a liquidation of the business. For maximum asset protection, consult a business attorney with specific expertise in your state's LLC statutes.

### Is a multi-member LLC better if I plan to raise investment?

It depends heavily on the type of investment. A multi-member LLC works well for small groups of co-founders or private investors, and ownership can be structured flexibly through the operating agreement. However, if you plan to pursue institutional venture capital, you'll almost certainly need to convert to a C-Corporation eventually — most VC funds require C-Corp structure due to the inability of LLCs to issue preferred stock and the tax complications K-1s create for tax-exempt fund investors. Plan your entity selection with your long-term capital strategy in mind.
