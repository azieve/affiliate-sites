If you are starting a limited liability company, you will run into one document before any other: the articles of organization. It is the filing that turns your idea into a legal entity. Until a state accepts it, you do not have an LLC. You have a plan.

So, what are articles of organization? They are a short public document you file with your state's business filing office (usually the Secretary of State) to officially create an LLC. Most states require only a few pieces of information, and filing fees generally run from about $35 to $500 depending on where you form. ## What Are Articles of Organization?

To answer "what are articles of organization" precisely: they are the formation document that legally registers a limited liability company with a state government. Once the state reviews and approves the filing, your LLC exists as a separate legal entity. That separation is the basis of the liability protection LLC owners want.

A few points worth knowing up front:

- **They are public.** Anyone can usually search the state's database and view them. Whatever you put in the filing, including addresses and sometimes names, may be visible.
- **They are brief.** Most are one to two pages. Many states use a fill-in-the-blank form or an online portal.
- **They are filed once.** You file at formation, then amend them later only if key details change.
- **They go by different names.** Several states use "certificate of formation" (Texas and Delaware, for example), "certificate of organization" (New York and others), or "articles of formation." They do the same job.

When people search for an articles of organization template, they usually want the exact wording. Your state's Secretary of State website publishes the official form, and that is the version to use. A generic template from a random website may not meet your state's requirements.

## What Information Goes in Your Articles of Organization

Requirements differ by state, but most filings ask for the same core items.

**1. LLC name.** It must be distinguishable from other registered entities in the state and usually must include "LLC" or "Limited Liability Company." Check availability on the state's business search before you file.

**2. Registered agent and address.** Every state requires a registered agent, a person or company with a physical address in the state who accepts legal papers and official notices on your behalf. For more detail, see our guide on [what a registered agent is](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/).

**3. Principal office or mailing address.** Some states require it. Others do not.

**4. Management structure.** Some states ask whether the LLC is member-managed or manager-managed.

**5. Organizer or member information.** The organizer is the person who signs and files the document. This person does not have to be an owner. Some states also require member or manager names, which affects privacy.

**6. Purpose and duration (sometimes).** Many states accept "any lawful purpose." Duration is often "perpetual" unless you say otherwise.

**7. Effective date (optional).** Some states let you choose a future effective date.

In my experience, the registered agent line is where first-timers slip. They list a home address, then discover it is now on a public record and every process server in the state knows where to find them.

## How to File Articles of Organization: Step by Step

Here is the process I walk people through. The order matters because each step feeds the next.

1. **Choose your state.** For most single-state businesses, form where you live and operate. Forming elsewhere often means paying twice, because you must also register as a foreign LLC where you do business.
2. **Check name availability.** Search the state database. Some states let you reserve a name for a fee while you prepare.
3. **Select a registered agent.** You can serve as your own agent if you have a physical in-state address and are reliably available during business hours. Many owners use a commercial agent instead.
4. **Complete the state form.** Use the official form or online portal. Double-check spelling and the registered agent address.
5. **Submit and pay the fee.** Online filing is usually fastest. Processing ranges from same-day or a few business days in many states to several weeks by mail in slower ones.
6. **Save your approved copy.** The state returns a stamped or certified document. Keep it. Banks and licensing offices ask for it.
7. **Get your EIN.** The IRS issues Employer Identification Numbers for free at [IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/get-an-employer-identification-number). Our guide on [getting an EIN for free](/blog/how-to-get-an-ein-for-your-llc-free-irs-method/) walks through it.
8. **Open a business bank account** and keep your finances separate from personal funds.

The U.S. Small Business Administration also provides a general overview of the LLC formation process at [SBA.gov](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/choose-business-structure), which is a useful cross-check.

## Articles of Organization vs. Certificate of Formation

This is the question that confuses people most. Functionally, there is no difference. The name depends on the state.

- **Articles of organization:** Florida, California, Arizona, Colorado, Georgia, and others
- **Certificate of formation:** Texas and Delaware
- **Certificate of organization:** New York and a few others

If a website says "certificate of formation" and you are in Florida, you are looking at the wrong state's terminology, not a different legal concept. Always use the name your state uses so you find the correct form.

## Common Mistakes When Filing

I have seen too many business owners lose weeks to avoidable errors. Watch for these:

- **Name conflicts.** A name that is too close to an existing entity gets rejected. Have a backup.
- **Wrong registered agent address.** A PO box generally will not work. It must be a physical street address in the state.
- **Skipping state-specific rules.** New York's publication requirement catches people off guard, and a missed deadline can suspend an LLC's authority to do business.
- **Listing personal information unnecessarily.** Once the filing is public, you cannot easily make it private. Our guide on [forming an anonymous LLC](/blog/anonymous-llc-how-to-form-one/) covers options.
- **Ignoring what comes after.** Many states require annual or biennial reports and fees. Missing them can lead to administrative dissolution.

## What Happens After the State Approves Your Filing

Approval is the starting line, not the finish. After you receive your approved articles:

- Get your EIN and open a dedicated business bank account.
- Calendar your annual report or franchise tax deadlines.
- Check whether your city or county requires a business license.
- Talk to a tax professional about how your LLC will be taxed. By default, a single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietorship and a multi-member LLC like a partnership, and you may elect other treatment. The IRS explains the classifications at [IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc).
- Keep your records current. If your address, registered agent, or name changes, you may need to file an amendment. See [how to amend LLC articles of organization](/blog/how-to-amend-llc-articles-of-organization/).

One timely note for 2026: federal beneficial ownership reporting rules have shifted considerably since they were first introduced, and domestic companies were largely carved out of the requirement under FinCEN's 2025 interim final rule. Rules in this area change, so check [FinCEN's current guidance](https://www.fincen.gov/boi) and see our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) for the latest status before assuming anything applies to you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are articles of organization in simple terms?

They are the document that legally creates your LLC. You file them with your state, and once approved, your business becomes a separate legal entity from you personally.

### How much does it cost to file articles of organization?

State fees range from about $35 to $500. For example, Texas charges $300, Delaware $110, Florida $125, and California $70, though some states add other costs such as annual taxes or publication fees. Formation services add their own charges on top of the state fee.

### Do I need a lawyer to file articles of organization?

No. Most owners file on their own or through a formation service. A lawyer is worth considering if you have multiple owners, unusual ownership terms, a regulated profession, or complex liability exposure.

### How long does it take for articles of organization to be approved?

Online filings in many states are processed within a few business days, and some are same-day with expedited service. Mail filings and busy states can take several weeks. Check your state's current processing times.

### What happens if I do not file articles of organization?

You do not have an LLC. If you operate anyway, you are likely treated as a sole proprietor or general partnership, which means no liability protection and personal exposure to business debts.

### Can I change my articles of organization after filing?

Yes. You file an amendment with the state, usually for a fee. Common reasons are a name change, a new registered agent, or a change in management structure.

### Are articles of organization the same as a business license?

No. Articles of organization create the entity. A business license or permit is a separate authorization, issued by your city, county, or state, that allows you to operate in a particular trade or location.

### Do I need to file articles of organization every year?

No. You file them once at formation. Many states do require a separate annual or biennial report, which is a different filing.
