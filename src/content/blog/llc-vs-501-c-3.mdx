If you are weighing an LLC vs 501(c)(3), the first thing to know is that you are not comparing two of the same thing. An LLC is a type of business entity created under state law. A 501(c)(3) is a federal tax-exempt status granted by the IRS. That mismatch is the source of most of the confusion, and sorting it out will save you real money and months of rework.

For a regular for-profit business, the answer is simple: form an LLC. Services like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) start at $0 plus your state filing fee, and a basic LLC can be filed in days. If your goal is to run a charity, a school, or a community organization that accepts tax-deductible donations, the answer is different, and an ordinary LLC will rarely get you there. This guide explains why, shows where the two structures overlap, and helps you choose in 2026.

## LLC vs 501(c)(3): The Core Difference

An LLC (limited liability company) is a legal structure. You file articles of organization with a state, and you get liability protection that separates your personal assets from business debts. If you want the basics first, see our guide on [what an LLC is](/learn/what-is-an-llc/).

A 501(c)(3) is a section of the Internal Revenue Code. It describes organizations that are exempt from federal income tax and can receive tax-deductible charitable contributions. To qualify, an organization must be organized and operated exclusively for purposes such as charitable, religious, educational, scientific, or literary work. The IRS explains the requirements on its [page for charitable organizations](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/charitable-organizations).

So the real comparison is this:

| Feature | LLC | 501(c)(3) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | State-created legal entity | Federal tax-exempt status |
| Created by | State filing (articles of organization) | IRS application (Form 1023 or 1023-EZ), after forming an entity |
| Owners | Members who share in profits | No owners; governed by a board |
| Profit distribution | Allowed | Prohibited (private inurement) |
| Tax treatment | Pass-through by default | Generally exempt on mission-related income |
| Donations deductible | No | Yes, for donors who itemize |
| Typical purpose | For-profit business | Public benefit mission |
| Public reporting | Minimal | Annual Form 990 filings are public |

Think of it as a vehicle versus a license. You still need a vehicle, which is usually a nonprofit corporation, before you can apply for the 501(c)(3) license.

## Can an LLC Be a 501(c)(3)?

This is the question behind most "llc vs 501c3" searches. The short answer is that it is possible, but only in narrow circumstances, and it is rarely the best route.

The IRS allows an LLC to apply for 501(c)(3) status only if every member is itself a 501(c)(3) organization, a government unit, or otherwise meets the strict ownership rules, and the LLC's operating agreement contains the required charitable-purpose and asset-dedication language. An LLC owned by individuals who expect a share of profits cannot qualify, because that conflicts with the ban on private benefit.

In my experience, the situation I see most is a founder who forms an LLC for a "nonprofit idea," starts collecting donations, and then discovers the donors cannot deduct anything. Fixing that after the fact means forming a new entity, transferring assets, and applying to the IRS anyway. Spending a few hundred dollars on the right structure upfront is far cheaper.

## Where an LLC and a 501(c)(3) Can Work Together

The two structures are not always rivals. Some of the most useful setups combine them.

- **A nonprofit-owned LLC.** A 501(c)(3) can be the sole member of an LLC. The LLC is generally treated as a disregarded entity, meaning its activity is reported as part of the nonprofit. This is common when a charity wants to run a program with its own liability shield, hold a building, or operate a revenue-generating activity.
- **A for-profit LLC supporting a charity.** A business can donate to a 501(c)(3) and deduct the gift in the usual ways, subject to IRS limits, without becoming a nonprofit itself.
- **Joint ventures.** Hospitals and universities sometimes form LLCs with for-profit partners, though the tax rules here are complex and need professional review.

If a nonprofit owning an LLC is your scenario, our guide on [whether a nonprofit can own an LLC](/blog/can-a-nonprofit-own-an-llc/) covers sole-member rules and unrelated business income tax (UBIT). Income from an LLC that is not substantially related to the charity's mission can be taxed even when the parent is exempt.

## Taxes: How Each Structure Is Treated

**LLC taxes.** By default, a single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietorship and a multi-member LLC like a partnership. Profits pass through to the owners, who pay income tax plus self-employment tax of 15.3% on net earnings, within IRS limits. An LLC can elect S-corporation treatment to reduce payroll tax in some situations. Our breakdown of [how LLCs are taxed](/blog/llc-taxes-how-are-llcs-taxed/) walks through each option.

**501(c)(3) taxes.** An approved 501(c)(3) generally pays no federal income tax on income related to its exempt purpose. It must still file an annual information return with the IRS: Form 990-N for very small organizations, Form 990-EZ for mid-sized ones, or the full Form 990 for larger ones. Those returns are open to public inspection. It must also pay payroll taxes for employees, and it may owe tax on unrelated business income.

Exemption from federal income tax does not automatically mean exemption from state income, sales, or property tax. Many states require a separate application. Check your state's rules and consult a tax professional before assuming anything.

**Forming a 501(c)(3).** The usual path has three layers:

1. **Form the entity.** Most 501(c)(3)s are nonprofit corporations filed with the state. Fees vary widely.
2. **Get an EIN.** This is free directly from the IRS.
3. **Apply for exemption.** The IRS user fee is currently $600 for Form 1023 and $275 for the shorter Form 1023-EZ, which is only available to smaller organizations that meet eligibility tests. Review the [IRS application guidance](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/application-for-recognition-of-exemption) before choosing.

**Timeline.** An LLC can be formed in a few business days. IRS review of a 501(c)(3) application can take several months, and complex applications take longer. If you file within 27 months of your formation date, the exemption can generally be retroactive to the date you formed, which is one reason not to wait.

**Ongoing costs.** LLCs may owe annual reports and state fees. A 501(c)(3) has annual Form 990 filings, state charitable solicitation registrations in many states, and often an annual state report. Missing three consecutive years of Form 990 filings causes the IRS to automatically revoke tax-exempt status, which is a painful and avoidable mistake.

## Control, Ownership and Personal Liability

**Ownership and money.** LLC members own the business and can take profits. A 501(c)(3) has no owners. Its assets must be permanently dedicated to the exempt purpose, so if it dissolves, remaining assets go to another exempt organization, not to the founders. Founders and officers can be paid reasonable compensation for actual work, but cannot extract profits.

**Governance.** An LLC can be run by its members or by a manager, with flexibility set out in an operating agreement (see our [operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/)). A 501(c)(3) needs a board of directors, bylaws, and a conflict-of-interest policy. The IRS asks about these on the application. Many founders feel the loss of control more than any other trade-off.

**Liability.** Both structures can protect personal assets if you respect the formalities. Commingling funds or ignoring governance can expose you either way. Nonprofit directors also face fiduciary duties and can face personal exposure for unpaid payroll taxes and certain misconduct, so directors-and-officers insurance is worth pricing.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Collecting "donations" through an LLC.** Contributions to an LLC are not deductible charitable gifts, and promising otherwise can create legal exposure.
- **Assuming a nonprofit means no taxes at all.** Payroll, unrelated business income, and state taxes can still apply.
- **Skipping state registrations.** Many states require charities to register before soliciting donations.
- **Mixing personal and organization funds.** This is a fast route to IRS trouble for a 501(c)(3) and to veil-piercing for an LLC.
- **Filing the wrong IRS form.** Using Form 1023-EZ when you are ineligible leads to rejection and lost time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between an LLC and a 501(c)(3)?

An LLC is a state-registered business entity that gives owners limited liability. A 501(c)(3) is an IRS tax status for charitable organizations. One describes how an organization is legally structured, and the other describes how it is taxed. Most 501(c)(3)s are actually nonprofit corporations that later applied for the status.

### Can an LLC be a 501(c)(3)?

Yes, but only in limited cases. Every member generally must be a 501(c)(3) or government entity, and the operating agreement must include charitable-purpose and dissolution language the IRS requires. An LLC owned by individuals who share profits cannot qualify.

### Is a nonprofit LLC the same as a 501(c)(3)?

No. "Nonprofit LLC" is an informal term. An LLC that does not pay out profits is not automatically tax-exempt, and without IRS recognition donors cannot deduct gifts. Exemption must be applied for and approved.

### How much does it cost to get 501(c)(3) status?

The IRS user fee is $600 for Form 1023 or $275 for Form 1023-EZ, plus state formation fees and any legal help. Confirm current fees on IRS.gov before filing, as they can change.

### Can I pay myself from a 501(c)(3)?

Yes, reasonable compensation for services actually performed is allowed. What is prohibited is distributing profits or giving insiders excessive benefits. The board should approve compensation based on comparable data.

### Do donations to an LLC qualify as tax-deductible?

No. Payments to a for-profit LLC are not charitable contributions. Some may be deductible to the payer as a business expense or may be treated as income to the LLC, depending on the facts. A tax professional can sort out the details.

### Can a nonprofit own an LLC?

Yes. A 501(c)(3) can own a single-member LLC, which is usually disregarded for tax purposes. Income unrelated to the exempt mission may be subject to UBIT.

### Should a small business choose an LLC or a 501(c)(3)?

If you plan to profit personally, choose an LLC. If your purpose is public benefit and you want tax-deductible donations, a 501(c)(3) is the better match. If you are unsure, [LegalZoom](/go/legalzoom/) and ZenBusiness can handle the LLC side, while an exempt-organization attorney is the right call for the nonprofit side.

## Final Thoughts

The LLC vs 501(c)(3) question mostly comes down to purpose and money flow. Want ownership, flexibility, and the ability to take profits? Form an LLC. Want to serve a public mission with donations and grants and accept the loss of ownership that comes with it? Build a nonprofit and apply for 501(c)(3) status.
