## What Does It Mean to Remove a Member from an LLC?

An LLC "member" is an owner. Removing one means ending that person's ownership interest and their rights to vote, manage, and share in profits. There are three common ways this happens:

1. **Voluntary withdrawal.** The member chooses to leave (sometimes called member withdrawal from an LLC) and sells or surrenders their interest.
2. **Buyout.** The company or the other members purchase the departing member's interest at an agreed price. This is the most common LLC member buyout scenario.
3. **Involuntary removal (expulsion).** The remaining members vote to expel a member from the LLC based on grounds in the operating agreement, or a court orders it.

It also helps to separate removal from two related concepts. Removing a *manager* in a manager-managed LLC only ends their management role, not their ownership. If you are unsure which structure you have, read our guide on [member-managed vs manager-managed LLCs](/blog/llc-member-vs-manager-managed/). And if the exiting owner is simply handing their share to someone else, that is a transfer, covered in [how to transfer LLC ownership](/blog/how-to-transfer-llc-ownership/).

## Step 1: Read Your Operating Agreement First

Every decision flows from this document. Before anyone sends an angry email, find the sections that address:

- **Withdrawal and removal rights.** Can a member leave at will? Can members be expelled, and for what causes (breach of duty, missed capital contributions, bankruptcy, death, disability)?
- **Voting thresholds.** Many agreements require a majority or supermajority of ownership interests, not a headcount. Some exclude the member being removed from the vote. Others do not. Check.
- **Buyout formula.** Look for a pre-agreed valuation method: fixed price, book value, a multiple of earnings, or an independent appraisal.
- **Payment terms.** Lump sum or installments, and whether the departing member's interest is secured.
- **Notice requirements.** Written notice, meeting requirements, and cure periods.

If you do not have a written operating agreement, your state's default LLC statute fills the gap. Defaults are often unfavorable: in many states a member cannot be expelled by a simple majority absent agreement, and a departing member may have limited rights to a payout. Our [LLC operating agreement guide](/blog/llc-operating-agreement-template-and-guide/) walks through what to include so you are not relying on defaults.

> **From the lawyer's chair:** A good operating agreement treats a member's exit like a prenuptial agreement treats divorce. It is uncomfortable to write when everyone is friendly, and invaluable when they are not.

In my experience, the single most common reason these disputes turn into litigation is not greed. It is ambiguity. I have seen business owners spend five figures in legal fees fighting over one sentence in a template agreement that nobody read carefully at formation.

## Step 2: Choose the Right Removal Path

Once you know what your agreement says, pick the path that fits.

### Voluntary withdrawal

If the member wants out, this is the cleanest route. The member gives written notice, the parties agree on a price, and the LLC or the other members buy the interest. Even in friendly exits, put everything in writing.

### Negotiated buyout

When the member is reluctant or the agreement is silent, negotiation is still usually cheaper than court. A typical LLC member buyout includes:

- A valuation of the business as of a specific date
- The percentage interest being purchased
- Payment structure (for example, 20% down and the balance over 24 months)
- Releases of claims on both sides
- Non-compete, non-solicitation, and confidentiality terms where enforceable

**Example:** Three members each own 33.3% of a landscaping LLC valued at $450,000. One member wants out. A straight pro-rata buyout would be about $150,000. If the agreement discounts minority interests by 20%, the price falls to roughly $120,000. That $30,000 gap is exactly why the valuation clause matters so much.

### Involuntary removal (expulsion)

If the operating agreement allows it, follow the procedure to the letter:

1. Document the grounds (missed contributions, breach of fiduciary duty, fraud, and so on).
2. Give written notice and any required cure period.
3. Hold a properly noticed meeting and vote, recording the result in written minutes or a written consent.
4. Deliver the buyout payment or terms the agreement requires.

Courts look closely at whether the process was followed. A technically valid vote can be unwound if notice was defective, so be strict.

### Court-ordered removal

If the agreement is silent and the member will not leave, some states let the other members or the company petition a court for judicial expulsion, typically on narrow grounds such as wrongful conduct that harms the business or makes it impracticable to carry on. This is slow and expensive, so treat it as a last resort.

## Step 3: Document the Removal

Paperwork is what protects you later. At minimum, prepare:

- **A written consent or meeting minutes** approving the removal and buyout
- **A withdrawal or redemption agreement** signed by the departing member
- **An amended operating agreement** (or an amendment) reflecting the new ownership percentages, capital accounts, and voting rights
- **An updated membership ledger** (your internal record of who owns what)
- **A release of claims**, ideally mutual

If the departing member also served as a manager, officer, or signer, add a resignation letter and remove them from authority documents.

If you ever bring a new owner in to replace them, you will go through the reverse process described in [how to add a member to an LLC](/blog/how-to-add-a-member-to-an-llc/).

## Step 4: File State Paperwork and Update Records

Many states do not require the LLC to publicly list members at all, so there may be nothing to file for a removal. Others do, so check the following:

- **Articles of Organization amendment.** Required in some states if members or managers are named on the original filing. Fees commonly range from $0 to $100+, depending on the state.
- **Annual or biennial report.** If your state lists members or managers on the report, update it at the next filing, or sooner if your state requires interim updates.
- **Registered agent and statements of information.** Some states, such as California, use periodic statements listing managers or members.
- **Beneficial ownership reporting.** Federal rules on FinCEN's Beneficial Ownership Information (BOI) reporting changed significantly in 2025, and requirements for U.S.-formed companies have been narrowed. Confirm what currently applies on [FinCEN's BOI page](https://www.fincen.gov/boi) and see our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) for context.
- **IRS records.** If the departing member was the "responsible party" on your EIN, file [Form 8822-B](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8822-b) within 60 days of the change.

### Don't forget the outside world

- **Bank accounts:** Remove the member as a signer and update the banking resolution.
- **Licenses and permits:** Some trade, professional, or liquor licenses list owners.
- **Contracts, leases, and loans:** Personal guarantees do not disappear automatically. Ask the lender or landlord for a written release of the departing member, and expect resistance.
- **Insurance, vendors, and websites:** Update contacts, authorized users, and public listings.

## Step 5: Understand the Tax Consequences

This is the section most people ignore until their accountant calls. A tax professional should review your specific situation, but here is the general landscape for 2026.

**Multi-member LLCs taxed as partnerships.** Payments to a departing member are generally governed by IRC Sections 736 and 741. The IRS explains partnership basics in [Publication 541](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p541). In general:

- The departing member recognizes gain or loss based on the amount received versus their tax basis. The character of that gain (capital vs. ordinary) depends on whether the LLC holds "hot assets" such as unrealized receivables or inventory.
- The LLC issues a final Schedule K-1 for the year of exit, marked as a final return for that member.
- The way the buyout is paid (by the LLC vs. by other members) affects who carries which tax consequences and basis adjustments.

**Example:** A member with a $60,000 tax basis receives $100,000 for their interest. They would generally report about $40,000 of gain, though the character depends on the asset mix.

**If only one member remains.** Under longstanding IRS guidance (Rev. Rul. 99-6), when a multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership becomes a single-member LLC, it typically becomes a disregarded entity by default. That changes how you file: Schedule C or E on the owner's personal return rather than Form 1065. How the EIN and final partnership return are handled depends on how the buyout is structured, so confirm with a CPA. For more on how structures differ, see [single-member vs multi-member LLCs](/blog/single-member-llc-vs-multi-member-llc/).

**S-corp elections.** If your LLC elected S-corp status, the departing member's shares and the buyout structure can affect eligibility and the one-class-of-stock rule. Get advice before closing the deal.

**Self-employment tax.** Some payments to a departing partner (such as continuing payments for services) can be treated differently from payments for the interest itself. Details matter.

## Step 6: Manage Personal Risk After the Exit

Removing a member does not erase the past. Think through these protections:

- **Liabilities incurred before the exit.** A departing member may remain exposed for pre-exit obligations, especially personally guaranteed debts. The remaining members should expect indemnification requests.
- **Indemnification and release.** The remaining owners should get a release of claims. The departing member should get indemnification for post-exit liabilities.
- **Continued authority.** Notify customers and vendors that the person is no longer authorized to act for the company.
- **Confidential data and access.** Revoke accounts, shared drives, and credentials the same day.
- **Fiduciary duties.** Members and managers owe duties that can persist for conduct during their tenure.

## Which Option Fits Which Situation?

| Situation | Typical path | Key document | Biggest risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Member wants to leave | Voluntary withdrawal and buyout | Redemption agreement | Undervaluation disputes |
| Member inactive but unwilling to leave | Negotiated buyout | Settlement + amendment | Claims of oppression |
| Member breached duties | Expulsion by vote (if allowed) | Written consent + notice | Procedural defects |
| No operating agreement, member refuses | Court petition or dissolution | Court order | Cost and delay |
| Business no longer viable | Dissolve instead | Articles of dissolution | Missed creditor notice |

If the real issue is that the partnership is broken beyond repair, dissolving may be cleaner than removing one person. See [how to dissolve an LLC](/blog/how-to-dissolve-an-llc/) for that process.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Skipping the operating agreement review.** Following the wrong procedure can void the removal.
2. **Informal handshake deals.** Verbal buyouts are the leading source of later disputes.
3. **Ignoring valuation.** Without a defensible method, expect a claim that the price was unfair.
4. **Forgetting personal guarantees.** The departing member still owes the bank unless released.
5. **Missing tax filings.** A final K-1 and updated IRS records are not optional.
6. **Not updating the agreement.** Leaving the departed member's name and percentages in place invites confusion.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you remove a member from an LLC without their consent?

Sometimes. If your operating agreement allows expulsion and sets out a vote and grounds, the remaining members can act without the member's consent by following the procedure. If the agreement is silent, state law may require unanimous consent or a court order.

### How much does it cost to remove a member from an LLC?

The legal and filing costs are modest: state amendment fees (if needed) typically run $0 to $100+, and attorney review might cost a few hundred to a few thousand dollars. The real cost is the buyout price, which depends on the value of the member's interest.

### What happens to a member's share when they are removed?

Their interest is either purchased by the company (redeemed and canceled), which increases the remaining members' percentages proportionally, or purchased by the other members directly. Your operating agreement controls which approach applies.

### Do I need to file anything with the state to remove an LLC member?

Not always. Many states do not list members publicly. If members or managers are named in your Articles of Organization or annual report, you may need to file an amendment or update the next report. Check with your state's Secretary of State.

### Can a member be removed from a single-member LLC?

A single-member LLC has only one owner, so there is no one to remove. The owner can transfer ownership instead, or add members first. See [how to add a member to an LLC](/blog/how-to-add-a-member-to-an-llc/) if you are restructuring.

### Is a member still liable for LLC debts after leaving?

Generally the member is not personally liable for LLC debts incurred after leaving, but may remain responsible for obligations they personally guaranteed and, in some cases, for conduct during their tenure. Get a written release where possible.

### What if there is no operating agreement?

Your state's default LLC law applies. Defaults vary, and many require unanimous consent to expel a member. Consider negotiating a written exit agreement and adopting a proper operating agreement going forward.

### Do I need a lawyer to remove a member from an LLC?

Not for a friendly, simple exit with a clear agreement. For contested removals, large buyouts, or complicated tax situations, a business attorney and a CPA are well worth the cost.
