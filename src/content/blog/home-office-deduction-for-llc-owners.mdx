## What Is the Home Office Deduction (and Does an LLC Change It)?

The home office tax deduction lets you subtract part of your home costs from business income when you use part of your home regularly and exclusively for business. The IRS lays out the rules in [Publication 587](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p587).

An LLC is a legal structure, not a tax classification. The IRS treats it differently depending on its members and elections:

- **Single-member LLC (default):** treated as a "disregarded entity." You report business income and expenses on Schedule C, and the home office deduction works the same as it does for a sole proprietor.
- **Multi-member LLC (default):** taxed as a partnership. Partners generally can't take the deduction on Form 8829 the way a Schedule C filer can. More on that below.
- **LLC taxed as an S corporation:** the company, not you personally, is the taxpayer, so you need a specific reimbursement setup.

If you're unsure how your LLC is classified, start with our guide to [how LLCs are taxed](/blog/llc-taxes-how-are-llcs-taxed/). Everything else in this article depends on that answer.

## Who Qualifies for the Home Office Deduction for LLC Owners?

Two tests apply, and both must be met.

**1. Regular and exclusive use.** The space must be used regularly for business and only for business. A desk in your living room that doubles as a gaming setup on weekends fails the exclusivity test. The space doesn't need to be a separate room, but it must be a clearly identifiable area. A dedicated corner with a desk, files and equipment can qualify.

**2. A qualifying purpose.** The space generally must be one of these:

- Your principal place of business. This includes a space where you do administrative or management work, as long as you have no other fixed location where you do substantial administrative work.
- A place where you regularly meet clients or customers.
- A separate structure, such as a detached garage or studio, used in your business.

There are also narrow exceptions for storing inventory and for daycare facilities.

**The income limit.** The deduction can't exceed the gross income from the business use of your home, less other business expenses. Under the actual-expense method, a loss caused by the home office itself generally can't be claimed in the current year, though the excess carries forward.

> **My take:** In my experience, the exclusive-use rule is where most freelancers and consultants stumble. I've seen business owners claim the whole spare room, then use it as a guest bedroom or a kids' study spot. If you can't honestly say the space is for business only, claim a smaller area or skip the deduction. The tax savings aren't worth a weak position if you're ever asked to substantiate it.

## Single-Member vs. Multi-Member LLC: Who Gets the Deduction?

This is the section most generic articles skip, and it matters most.

| LLC Tax Treatment | Can You Claim a Home Office Deduction? | How |
|---|---|---|
| Single-member LLC (disregarded) | Yes | Schedule C, with Form 8829 or the simplified method |
| Multi-member LLC (partnership) | Possibly, as a partner | Typically as an unreimbursed partnership expense, only if the operating agreement requires partners to cover their own expenses. Often better handled with an accountable plan |
| LLC taxed as S corp | Not directly | The company reimburses you under an accountable plan, or you rent the space to the company |
| LLC taxed as C corp | Not directly | Same approach: reimbursement under an accountable plan |

Two points deserve emphasis:

- **Employees can't take it.** Since the 2017 tax law, W-2 employees can't deduct unreimbursed home office costs. If you're an S corp owner paid through payroll, you are an employee of your own company for this purpose. The fix is an accountable plan: the company reimburses your documented home office costs, deducts them, and you don't pay income tax on the reimbursement.
- **Partners have a different path.** Multi-member LLC partners generally can't use Form 8829 the same way. The rules are technical, so talk to a CPA before filing.

## Simplified Method vs. Actual Expense Method

If you qualify, you choose between two ways to calculate the deduction. You can switch methods from year to year.

### The simplified method

The IRS's [simplified option](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/simplified-option-for-home-office-deduction) is straightforward:

- **$5 per square foot**, up to **300 square feet**
- **Maximum deduction: $1,500**
- No depreciation and no need to track every household bill
- Claimed directly on Schedule C (and the worksheet in the Form 1040 instructions), so no Form 8829

**Example:** Priya runs a single-member consulting LLC from a 150-square-foot room. Simplified method: 150 × $5 = **$750**.

### The actual expense method (Form 8829)

With this method, you calculate the percentage of your home used for business and apply it to your home expenses. You report it on **Form 8829**.

Eligible indirect expenses typically include:

- Mortgage interest or rent
- Property taxes
- Homeowners or renters insurance
- Utilities (electric, gas, water, trash)
- Repairs and maintenance affecting the whole home
- Depreciation, if you own the home

Direct expenses, such as painting only the office, are fully deductible. Expenses for the rest of the home aren't.

**Example:** Marcus has a 2,000-square-foot home with a 200-square-foot office, so business use is **10%**. His annual costs are:

| Expense | Annual Cost | 10% Business Share |
|---|---|---|
| Rent/mortgage interest + property tax | $24,000 | $2,400 |
| Utilities | $3,600 | $360 |
| Insurance | $1,200 | $120 |
| Repairs and maintenance | $800 | $80 |
| **Total** | **$30,000** | **$2,960** |

Under the simplified method, Marcus would get 200 × $5 = $1,000. The actual method yields $2,960, nearly three times as much, but requires records and brings depreciation considerations if he owns the home.

### Which should you choose?

- **Simplified** works well if your home costs are modest, your office is small, or you want minimal bookkeeping.
- **Actual** tends to win in higher-cost housing markets or when the office is large.

Run both numbers each year. It takes about ten minutes, and the difference can be a few hundred to a few thousand dollars.

## What the Deduction Is Actually Worth

A deduction reduces taxable income, not your tax bill dollar for dollar. For a single-member LLC owner, a deduction on Schedule C reduces income subject to both income tax and self-employment tax. The self-employment tax rate is 15.3% on net earnings up to the Social Security wage base, which makes the home office tax deduction more valuable than many people expect.

**Illustrative example:** a $2,960 deduction for someone in the 22% federal bracket could reduce federal income tax by roughly $651 and self-employment tax by roughly $418 (about 92.35% of the deduction × 15.3%), before accounting for the SE tax adjustment and state taxes. Your actual savings depend on your income, filing status and state. Our [self-employment tax guide](/blog/self-employment-tax-for-llc-owners/) shows the full calculation.

Savings you realize through the deduction also reduce what you owe on estimated payments. If you're adjusting your numbers mid-year, see our [quarterly tax payments guide](/blog/llc-quarterly-tax-payments-guide/).

## How to Claim the Deduction Step by Step

1. **Confirm your LLC's tax classification.** Single-member and default? Schedule C applies. Otherwise, plan for an accountable plan or partnership treatment.
2. **Measure the space.** Measure the office area and total home square footage. Take photos and keep a simple floor plan.
3. **Check regular and exclusive use.** Be honest about it.
4. **Pick a method.** Calculate both and compare.
5. **Gather records.** For the actual method, collect mortgage or rent statements, utility bills, insurance and repair receipts.
6. **File.** Use Form 8829 for the actual method, or the simplified worksheet.
7. **Keep documentation.** Hold supporting records for at least three years from the filing date, longer if you own the home and claim depreciation.

## Common Mistakes (and Hidden Gotchas)

- **Failing exclusive use.** The most common problem. Mixed-use spaces don't qualify.
- **Ignoring the income limit.** A new business with little revenue may not be able to use the full deduction in year one.
- **Forgetting depreciation recapture.** If you own your home and use the actual method with depreciation, selling later can trigger recapture on the depreciation you claimed (or were entitled to claim). The home sale exclusion doesn't shield that portion. The simplified method avoids this.
- **Assuming S corp owners can just deduct it.** Without an accountable plan, the company has no deduction and you have no personal deduction.
- **Mixing up personal and business accounts.** Unclear records make any deduction harder to defend. Our guide to [how to pay yourself from an LLC](/blog/how-to-pay-yourself-from-an-llc/) covers keeping owner draws and expenses clean.
- **Claiming 100% of utilities.** Only the business-use percentage qualifies.

### Is the home office deduction an audit trigger?

Commonly repeated advice says it is. In practice, the real risk isn't the deduction itself but claiming it without support. If your use is genuine and your records are organized, you're in a defensible position. I'd rather an owner claim a smaller, well-documented amount than a large number they can't back up.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a single-member LLC owner take the home office deduction?

Yes. A single-member LLC is a disregarded entity by default, so you report on Schedule C and can claim the deduction if you meet the regular and exclusive use requirements.

### Can I deduct my home office if my LLC is taxed as an S corp?

Not directly. Because S corp owners who work in the business are employees, the better route is an accountable plan, where the company reimburses your documented home office costs. Ask your CPA to set it up properly.

### How much is the home office deduction for an LLC?

Under the simplified method, up to $1,500 ($5 per square foot, capped at 300 square feet). Under the actual expense method, it's your business-use percentage multiplied by qualifying home expenses, and it can be higher or lower than the simplified amount.

### Do I need a separate room for a home office?

No, but the area must be a distinct, identifiable part of your home used regularly and exclusively for business. A defined corner can qualify; a shared dining table generally can't.

### Can I take the home office deduction if I also work at a client site or coworking space?

Possibly. If you do your administrative and management work at home and have no other fixed location where you do substantial administrative work, your home can still qualify as your principal place of business.

### Do I need Form 8829?

Only if you use the actual expense method. The simplified method doesn't require [Form 8829](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8829).

### What happens if I sell my home after claiming the deduction?

Under the actual method, depreciation you claimed (or could have claimed) may be recaptured when you sell. The simplified method avoids this issue. Discuss timing with a tax professional before selling.

### Can I deduct a home office if my LLC has a loss?

Generally, the deduction is limited by the business's income. Under the actual method, any excess may carry forward to future years. Under the simplified method, no carryforward is allowed.

The home office deduction for LLC owners is real and often worth claiming, but only when your LLC's tax classification and your use of the space line up with the rules. If you're a single-member LLC filing Schedule C, the process is relatively simple: confirm exclusive use, compare the simplified method with Form 8829, and keep good records. If you're in a multi-member LLC or taxed as an S corp, the route runs through partnership rules or an accountable plan instead.
