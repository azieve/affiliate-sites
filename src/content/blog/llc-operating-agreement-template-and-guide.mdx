## What Is an LLC Operating Agreement (and Why You Need One)

An operating agreement is a legal document that establishes the ownership structure and operating procedures of your LLC. Think of it as the company's constitution — it governs the relationship between members (owners) and, in multi-member LLCs, between members and managers.

Only five states currently require you to have one on file or in your records: California, Delaware, Maine, Missouri, and New York (New York additionally requires publication of formation notice, a separate and often-overlooked requirement). But "not legally required" doesn't mean "not necessary." Here's what a properly drafted operating agreement actually does for you:

1. **Preserves your liability shield.** Courts evaluating whether to "pierce the corporate veil" — i.e., hold you personally liable for business debts — look at whether you treated the LLC as a genuinely separate entity. An operating agreement is Exhibit A that you did.
2. **Overrides your state's default rules.** Without one, your LLC is governed by your state's default LLC statute, which may split profits equally regardless of investment, allow any member to bind the company to contracts, or dissolve the LLC automatically if a member leaves. Few owners would choose these defaults if they read them first.
3. **Prevents disputes between members.** I've seen too many two-person LLCs fall apart not because the business failed, but because there was no written agreement on what happens when one partner wants to sell, stops contributing, or dies. A $150 template drafted on day one would have saved tens of thousands in legal fees later.
4. **Satisfies banks and investors.** Most banks require an operating agreement to open a business bank account, and any investor or lender doing due diligence will ask for one before writing a check.

According to the U.S. Small Business Administration, LLCs remain the most commonly chosen business structure for new small businesses in the United States, and that trend has only accelerated as more solo founders and freelancers formalize their side businesses (see the SBA's [small business ownership guidance](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/choose-business-structure)). The more LLCs that get formed, the more operating agreement disputes end up in state court — which is exactly the outcome this document is designed to prevent.

## What to Include: The Essential Components of an LLC Operating Agreement Template

A solid LLC operating agreement template — whether you get it from a formation service, an attorney, or a reputable legal document site — should cover these core sections:

**1. Company formation details.** Legal name, principal address, registered agent information, formation date, and state of organization. If you're using a [registered agent](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/) service, this is where their name and address get referenced.

**2. Ownership structure (membership interests).** Who owns what percentage, and how those percentages were determined — cash contributed, services rendered, equipment contributed, etc. Get specific: "Member A contributed $30,000 cash and $10,000 in equipment for a 60% interest" is far stronger than "Member A owns 60%."

**3. Capital contributions.** What each member put in initially and — critically — what happens if the LLC needs more capital later. Are members required to contribute more, or is additional funding optional? This clause alone prevents a huge share of future member disputes.

**4. Profit and loss allocation.** By default, most states allocate profits/losses by ownership percentage, but your agreement can specify otherwise (this matters a lot for tax planning — more below).

**5. Management structure.** Is the LLC member-managed (all owners participate in daily decisions) or manager-managed (one or more designated managers run operations, similar to a board)? This single decision affects contracts, hiring authority, and who can legally bind the company.

**6. Voting rights and decision-making thresholds.** What requires unanimous consent (e.g., selling the company, taking on debt above a certain threshold) versus a simple majority (e.g., hiring a vendor)?

**7. Transfer of membership interests.** Can a member sell their stake to an outsider? Do existing members get right of first refusal? This is the single most litigated clause in multi-member operating agreements when it's missing or vague.

**8. Buy-sell provisions.** What happens when a member dies, becomes disabled, divorces, files bankruptcy, or simply wants to leave? Without this, a member's ex-spouse or estate could end up as your new business partner.

**9. Dissolution terms.** How and when the LLC can be wound down, and how remaining assets get distributed.

**10. Tax classification elections.** Whether the LLC is taxed as a disregarded entity, partnership, or has elected S-corp status with the IRS. This should align with what you actually filed on Form 8832 or Form 2553 — mismatches here confuse accountants and can create real tax-filing headaches.

A generic free LLC operating agreement template you download from a random site often skips sections 3, 7, and 8 entirely — the exact clauses that matter most once real money or real disagreements are on the table.

## Single-Member vs. Multi-Member Operating Agreements: Key Differences

This is where a lot of confusion happens, so let's separate the two clearly.

**Single-member LLC operating agreement.** If you're the only owner, your agreement is shorter and simpler — no voting thresholds between members, no profit-split negotiations, no buy-sell provisions between co-owners. But don't skip it. A single-member LLC operating agreement is still your best evidence, in an audit or lawsuit, that the LLC is a real, separate legal entity and not just a formality wrapped around your personal finances. It should still cover capital contributions, management authority, and what happens to the LLC if you become incapacitated or pass away (a surprisingly common gap — see our guide on [LLC vs. sole proprietorship](/learn/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/) structures for how liability protection actually works in practice).

**Multi-member operating agreement.** This is where the document earns its keep. With two or more owners, you need every clause listed above spelled out in detail — especially voting rights, profit allocation (which doesn't have to match ownership percentage if members agree otherwise and it's documented properly), and transfer restrictions. ## Common Mistakes That Make Operating Agreements Legally Weak

Having reviewed a lot of these documents over the years, the same handful of mistakes show up again and again:

**Never signing it.** An unsigned operating agreement sitting as a draft on your computer carries far less evidentiary weight than a signed one. Every member needs to sign, and you should keep the signed copy with your other LLC formation documents — not just in an email thread.

**Copy-pasting a template without adjusting state-specific defaults.** Every state's LLC statute differs slightly on default rules around member withdrawal, dissolution triggers, and fiduciary duties. A template written for Delaware law dropped into a Texas LLC without adjustment can create direct conflicts with Texas's default statute.

**Forgetting to update it after major changes.** Added a member? Changed the profit split? Switched from member-managed to manager-managed? If the operating agreement doesn't reflect reality, it stops protecting you and starts creating ambiguity — which is worse than having no agreement at all in some disputes.

**Vague or missing dispute resolution clauses.** Without a clause specifying mediation, arbitration, or a specific venue for lawsuits, a disagreement between members can turn into an expensive, drawn-out court battle in whatever jurisdiction happens to have standing.

**Treating it as a formality rather than a governing document.** This is the big one. An operating agreement that just gets filed away and never referenced isn't doing its job. It should actually govern how decisions get made — and members should know what's in it.

According to the American Bar Association's guidance on small business formation, inadequate internal governance documents remain one of the most common issues that surface when small businesses face litigation or ownership disputes — a pattern echoed across small business legal clinics nationwide (see general guidance from the [ABA's small business resources](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/)). The fix costs a few hundred dollars up front. The absence of it can cost tens of thousands later.

## How to Create, Sign, and Update Your Operating Agreement (Step-by-Step)

1. **Decide your management structure first.** Member-managed or manager-managed — this decision shapes almost every other clause, so lock it in before drafting anything else.
2. **Gather ownership and contribution details.** Get exact dollar figures, dates, and percentages from every member before you start filling in a template.
3. **Choose your template source.** For most straightforward LLCs, a formation service like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) or [LegalZoom](/go/legalzoom/) that generates a state-specific template as part of your formation package is the fastest, most cost-effective route in 2026. For anything with outside investors or unequal ownership splits, loop in an attorney before finalizing.
4. **Fill in every section — don't leave placeholders.** A template with "[insert profit split here]" left blank is worse than useless if it ever needs to hold up in a dispute.
5. **Have every member review it independently.** Ideally each member (or at least the majority owners) has their own advisor look it over, especially for multi-member LLCs with meaningful capital at stake.
6. **Sign and date it — physically or via e-signature.** Keep signed copies with your Articles of Organization, EIN confirmation letter, and other formation documents.
7. **Store it somewhere permanent.** Your registered agent's portal, a shared company drive, and your accountant's files are all reasonable places — just don't let it live in one person's inbox.
8. **Revisit it any time something material changes.** New member, changed ownership split, new manager, or a shift in how profits are distributed — amend the agreement in writing each time, with all members signing off on the amendment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I legally need an LLC operating agreement?**
Only five states — California, Delaware, Maine, Missouri, and New York — require one by statute. Every other state treats it as optional. That said, banks, investors, and courts evaluating your liability protection all treat it as a practical necessity, so "optional" shouldn't mean "skip it."

**How much does an LLC operating agreement cost in 2026?**
A free generic template costs $0. A template bundled into a formation service package typically runs $0–$349 depending on the tier, since it's included with mid-range and premium plans rather than sold separately. An attorney-drafted agreement for a multi-member LLC generally runs $500–$2,500+ depending on complexity.

**Can I write my own operating agreement without a lawyer?**
Yes, for simple single-member LLCs or straightforward multi-member LLCs with equal ownership and no outside investors, a well-built template is usually sufficient. Once you have unequal contributions, outside capital, or real estate involved, an attorney review is worth the cost.

**What happens if my LLC doesn't have an operating agreement?**
Your LLC falls back on your state's default LLC statute, which governs profit splits, member withdrawal, and dissolution automatically — often in ways owners wouldn't choose if given the option. It also weakens your argument that the LLC is a genuinely separate legal entity if your liability shield is ever challenged in court.

**Do single-member LLCs need an operating agreement?**
Yes. Even with one owner, it strengthens your liability protection, is often required by banks to open a business account, and clarifies what happens to the business if you become incapacitated or pass away.

**Can I change my operating agreement after it's signed?**
Yes — amendments are normal and expected as a business grows. Any change should be documented in writing and signed by all members (or the threshold your original agreement specifies for amendments), then stored with the original document.

**Does my operating agreement need to be filed with the state?**
No. Unlike your Articles of Organization, the operating agreement is an internal document — you keep it in your own records rather than filing it with the Secretary of State. The exception is that a few states require you to simply have one on file internally, not that it be publicly filed.

**Is an operating agreement the same as bylaws?**
No. Bylaws are the equivalent governance document for corporations. LLCs use operating agreements instead, which tend to be more flexible in structure since LLC law generally gives owners more latitude to customize internal rules than corporate law does.
