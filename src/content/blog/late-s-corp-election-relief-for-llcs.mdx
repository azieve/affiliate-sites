## What Is Late S Corp Election Relief for LLCs?

An LLC is not automatically an S corporation. By default, a single-member LLC is taxed as a "disregarded entity" (a sole proprietorship for tax purposes), and a multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership. An S corp is a *tax status*, not a business structure. To get it, your LLC files **Form 2553** with the IRS. For a deeper comparison of the two, see our guide to [LLC vs S-Corp taxes](/blog/llc-vs-s-corp-which-is-better-for-taxes/).

The standard **S corp election deadline** is no more than 2 months and 15 days after the beginning of the tax year the election is to take effect. For a calendar-year LLC wanting S status starting January 1, that's March 15. For a brand-new LLC, the 2-month-15-day clock starts on the date it first has shareholders, acquires assets, or begins doing business, whichever comes first.

Miss that window and the IRS treats your election as late. Without relief, your LLC would stay taxed as a sole proprietorship or partnership, and you'd have to wait until the next year to try again.

That's where relief comes in. Under **Rev. Proc. 2013-30** (available on [IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-13-30.pdf)), the IRS lets eligible businesses request a retroactive effective date for an S election that was filed late. In practical terms, a **late S corp election** can be treated as if it had been made on time, as long as you meet the conditions below.

## Who Qualifies for Late S Corp Election Relief in 2026?

The IRS doesn't hand out relief to everyone who forgot a deadline. Under Rev. Proc. 2013-30, you generally must meet all of these requirements:

1. **You intended to be an S corp** as of the effective date you're requesting.
2. **The only reason you weren't one** is that Form 2553 wasn't filed on time (or, for LLCs, that a related entity classification election wasn't made).
3. **You have reasonable cause** for the late filing. This is a short written explanation, such as relying on a tax adviser who didn't file, not knowing about the deadline, or an administrative error.
4. **Everyone treated the business as an S corp.** The LLC and all owners reported their income consistently with S corp status on their returns. In practice, that usually means a Form 1120-S was filed for the year, and each owner's Schedule K-1 income was reported on their personal return.
5. **You file within the time limit** described in the next section.

The LLC must also be eligible to be an S corp in the first place. That means no more than 100 shareholders, only eligible shareholders (generally U.S. individuals, certain trusts and estates), and only one class of stock. A nonresident alien owner, a partnership owner, or an LLC with disproportionate distribution rights can disqualify you regardless of timing. This catches more people than you'd think, and I'll come back to it below.

### The 3 Years and 75 Days Rule

The main deadline for relief is **3 years and 75 days after the intended effective date** of the election. Here's an example:

- You formed your LLC in January 2024 and intended S corp status effective January 1, 2024.
- You missed the March 15, 2024 deadline.
- Your 3-year-and-75-day window runs through roughly mid-March 2027.

So if you're reading this in late 2026, you probably still have time, but the window is not forgiving, and it closes on a fixed date.

There is a notable exception. The 3-year-75-day limit may not apply if the corporation isn't also seeking late entity classification relief, the corporation and all shareholders reported consistently with S status, at least six months have passed since the Form 1120-S for the first intended year was filed, and the IRS hasn't notified anyone of a problem with S status within that period. This exception is technical, so confirm it with a tax professional before leaning on it.

## How to Request Late S Corp Election Relief: Step by Step

The good news is that this process has no IRS user fee under Rev. Proc. 2013-30. The IRS instructions for [Form 2553](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i2553) walk through the filing mechanics. Here's the practical sequence.

### Step 1: Confirm your intended effective date

Pick the date S status should have started. For most LLCs that's the first day of the tax year (January 1) or the date of formation. Your business records, bank statements, payroll setup, and prior tax filings should be consistent with that date.

### Step 2: Complete Form 2553

Fill out Form 2553 as you normally would. Every owner must sign the consent section. At the top of the form, write **"FILED PURSUANT TO REV. PROC. 2013-30."** This tells the IRS you're requesting late-election relief rather than submitting a routine election.

Our walkthrough of [how to file Form 2553 for an LLC](/blog/how-to-file-form-2553-for-an-llc/) covers each box, including the tax year and shareholder information sections that trip people up.

### Step 3: Add the reasonable cause statement

Attach a statement, signed under penalties of perjury by an officer or authorized owner, explaining:

- That you intended to be treated as an S corporation as of the effective date
- The reasonable cause for failing to file on time
- That you've not received an IRS notice rejecting the election for that period
- That all owners reported income consistently with S status

Keep it factual and short. A plain explanation like "The company relied on its prior bookkeeper, who confirmed the election had been filed but did not file it" is the kind of reason the IRS routinely accepts. Avoid dramatic language or excuses you can't back up.

### Step 4: File the required returns

If you haven't already filed Form 1120-S for the year in question, file it with your Form 2553 package. If you filed Form 1120-S for the intended S corp year already, that's actually a good sign, because it shows consistent treatment. The IRS expects a Form 1120-S for each year covered by the retroactive election, and each owner's personal return should reflect their share of income through Schedule K-1.

### Step 5: Submit and keep proof

Mail or fax the package to the service center listed in the Form 2553 instructions. Use certified mail or another trackable method, and keep a full copy. The IRS typically responds with a CP261 acceptance letter, which is your proof that S status is in place. Processing often takes 60 days or longer, so don't assume silence means rejection.

## What If You Don't Qualify? Other Late S Corp Election Options

Not everyone fits neatly into Rev. Proc. 2013-30. Here are the other paths.

**Private Letter Ruling (PLR).** If you're outside the 3-year-75-day window and the exception doesn't apply, you can request a PLR under Section 1362(b)(5) of the Internal Revenue Code. This is slow and costs thousands of dollars in IRS user fees plus professional fees. It's usually a last resort.

**Waiting until next year.** For a small LLC with modest profits, paying the extra self-employment tax for one more year may cost less than the time and professional fees of a complex relief filing. Run the numbers before assuming relief is worth it.

**Late entity classification relief.** If your LLC never filed Form 8832 to be taxed as a corporation, filing Form 2553 on time generally includes a deemed election to be taxed as a corporation. Rev. Proc. 2013-30 also covers cases where both the corporate classification and the S election were late. A tax professional can confirm which pieces apply.

## Costs and Risks to Weigh Before You File

Late S corp election relief for LLCs is free from the IRS side, but the full picture includes other costs.

**Payroll catch-up.** Once S status applies, you must pay yourself a reasonable salary for the period covered. If your retroactive date reaches back into a prior year when you took only owner draws, you may owe back payroll taxes and need to file amended payroll forms. See our guide on [reasonable salary for S corp LLC owners](/blog/reasonable-salary-for-s-corp-llc-owners/) for how to set that number without inviting an audit.

**Late filing penalties.** If you should have filed Form 1120-S but didn't, the IRS can assess a penalty per shareholder per month, up to 12 months. For returns due in 2026, that amount is roughly $255 per shareholder per month (verify the current figure on IRS.gov). Rev. Proc. 2013-30 does not automatically erase this penalty, though you can request abatement for reasonable cause.

**State tax consequences.** Some states don't recognize S corps the same way the IRS does, and a few impose their own entity-level taxes. California charges an $800 minimum franchise tax and a 1.5% S corp tax, for instance. Check your state before electing.

**Tax savings may be smaller than you expect.** The S corp election saves self-employment tax only on the portion of profit that isn't salary. As a rough example, an LLC owner earning $90,000 in net profit who pays a $50,000 reasonable salary would avoid self-employment tax on the remaining $40,000. At 15.3%, that's roughly $6,000 on paper, before payroll costs, extra tax filings, and state taxes. Read our breakdown of [self-employment tax for LLC owners](/blog/self-employment-tax-for-llc-owners/) before deciding the election is worth the hassle.

> **A note from experience:** In my experience, the most common reason I see late elections fail isn't the missed deadline, it's inconsistent behavior afterward. I've seen too many business owners file Form 2553 late, then keep taking uneven owner draws and never run payroll. If the IRS can see you didn't act like an S corp, your "intent" argument falls apart. If you plan to request relief, start running payroll and keeping your books in S corp form right away.

## Common Mistakes That Sink Late Election Requests

1. **Filing the wrong form or no reasonable cause statement.** Forgetting the "FILED PURSUANT TO REV. PROC. 2013-30" notation or the signed statement is an easy way to get a rejection letter.
2. **Missing owner signatures.** Every person who was an owner during the period must consent. Getting a former partner to sign years later can be awkward but is required.
3. **Inconsistent tax returns.** If you filed Schedule C or a partnership return (Form 1065) for the year instead of Form 1120-S, you have a conflict to resolve. Talk to a professional before filing amended returns.
4. **Ineligible owners or share classes.** A partnership or corporation owning part of your LLC kills S eligibility. So can an operating agreement that gives some members priority distributions. Review your [LLC operating agreement](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/) before you submit.
5. **Waiting too long.** The 3-year-75-day deadline is firm in most cases. Don't coast to the end of the window.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is late S corp election relief for LLCs?

It's an IRS procedure, mainly under Rev. Proc. 2013-30, that lets an eligible LLC request an S corp effective date retroactively when Form 2553 was filed after the normal deadline. The IRS treats the election as timely if you show reasonable cause and meet the other requirements.

### How long do I have to file for late S corp election relief?

Generally within 3 years and 75 days of the intended effective date. Some businesses that meet additional conditions may be able to file outside that window, but you should confirm with a tax professional.

### Does the IRS charge a fee for a late S corp election?

No. Relief under Rev. Proc. 2013-30 has no IRS user fee. A Private Letter Ruling, by contrast, carries a significant user fee. You may still pay a CPA or attorney for help.

### What happens if I missed the S corp election deadline and do nothing?

Your LLC stays taxed under its default classification (sole proprietorship or partnership), and you keep paying self-employment tax on all net profit. If you've been filing as an S corp without a valid election, you may face penalties, so don't leave it unresolved.

### Do I need a reasonable cause statement for a late S election?

Yes. You must include a statement signed under penalties of perjury explaining why the election was late and confirming that you intended S status from the effective date.

### Can I get late S corp election relief if I filed taxes as a sole proprietor?

It's more complicated. Rev. Proc. 2013-30 generally expects returns to have been filed consistently with S corp status. If you filed as a sole proprietor, speak with a tax professional about whether amended returns or another route fits your situation.

### Is it worth requesting relief or should I wait until next year?

It depends on your profit level and the cost of fixing past filings. For lower-profit LLCs, waiting for the next election window may be cheaper. For higher-profit businesses, retroactive relief can recover meaningful tax savings.

## The Bottom Line

Late S corp election relief for LLCs is one of the more forgiving corners of the tax code, but only if you act inside the window and keep your records consistent.
