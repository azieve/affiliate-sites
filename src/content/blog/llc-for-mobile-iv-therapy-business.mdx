A nurse arrives at a client's home, hotel, or office with a cooler of fluids and vitamins, and the visit is billed at $150 to $350 or more. The startup costs look manageable, which is why so many nurses and entrepreneurs jump in. The legal exposure is much larger than the startup costs suggest. You are putting needles in people, in places you don't control, under rules that differ in every state.

## Why an LLC Makes Sense for a Mobile IV Therapy Business

A mobile IV company has risks that a typical consulting LLC doesn't:

- **Clinical risk:** infiltration, infection, allergic reactions, and the occasional medical emergency in someone's living room.
- **Vehicle and equipment risk:** you drive between appointments, carry supplies, and may own or lease a vehicle.
- **Contractual risk:** event contracts, corporate wellness agreements, and vendor or supply agreements.
- **Employment risk:** hiring nurses, medics, or admin staff brings payroll, classification, and workplace claims.

An LLC separates the business's debts and lawsuits from your personal assets. If a supplier sues over unpaid invoices or someone slips at an event you staffed, the claim generally targets the company rather than you personally.

There are limits. Courts won't protect you from your own professional negligence. If you personally start an IV and something goes wrong, you can be sued individually no matter what entity you have. This is why the LLC is one layer, and professional liability insurance is the layer that matters most. In my experience, new IV business owners overestimate what an LLC does and underestimate what insurance does. They need both.

## The Big Question: Corporate Practice of Medicine and Who Can Own the Business

This is the issue most generic LLC guides skip, and it's the one that can sink an IV business.

Many states follow some version of the **corporate practice of medicine (CPOM)** doctrine. In general terms, it limits who may own or control an entity that provides medical services. In stricter states, a company owned by a non-physician can't employ clinicians to deliver medical care, or can only do so through a specific structure. Other states are far more permissive.

IV therapy sits in a gray zone. Many states treat IV administration as the practice of medicine or nursing, and the drugs, fluids, and vitamins involved typically require a prescription or standing order. Whether that makes your company a "medical practice" for CPOM purposes depends on your state's law and its medical and nursing boards' positions.

The typical structures look like this:

| Structure | Who owns it | Where it's used |
|---|---|---|
| Standard LLC | Anyone, including a non-clinician entrepreneur | States with no CPOM restriction, or where the board treats IV hydration as a wellness service |
| PLLC (professional LLC) | Licensed professionals only | When the owner is a licensed professional (e.g., NP, physician) and state law requires or allows a professional entity |
| MSO + PC/PLLC ("friendly PC") model | Physician-owned practice entity, plus a separate management company owned by the entrepreneur | Stricter CPOM states where a non-physician wants to own the business side |

A **PLLC** is a professional LLC. Some states restrict what name you can use and who can be a member, and some limit the types of professions that can share a PLLC. The right model for an **IV therapy PLLC** or MSO setup depends on the state and on who the owner is. If you're an NP, see our guide to an [LLC for nurse practitioners](/blog/llc-for-nurse-practitioners/). ## Medical Director, Standing Orders, and Licensing

Even the best entity structure won't help if the clinical side isn't set up correctly. While exact requirements vary by state, most mobile IV operations need to address these items:

1. **A medical director or supervising physician.** Many states require physician oversight, and the medical director for IV therapy typically signs standing orders and protocols. Fees vary widely, and they may be flat monthly retainers or per-patient charges. Get the arrangement in writing, and have counsel check how it fits your state's rules on fee-splitting and control.
2. **Clinician licensure.** Whoever places the IV needs the appropriate license in the state where the patient is located. That matters for mobile services crossing state lines, such as a metro area that straddles two states.
3. **Patient assessment and orders.** Many states require a screening or provider evaluation before treatment, which can be done via telehealth in some places. Others require an in-person exam.
4. **Drug sourcing.** Fluids and medications generally need to come from licensed pharmacies or distributors. The FDA publishes information on [drug compounding and its regulatory framework](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding), and it's worth understanding the sourcing rules that apply to the products you use.
5. **Medical waste and sharps handling.** Regulations on disposal vary by state and locality, and a mobile operation still needs a compliant plan.
6. **Local business licenses.** Whether you need a **mobile IV therapy business license** specifically depends on the state and city. Many places require a general business license, and some health departments or boards also require facility or practice registration. Check with your state's board of nursing, board of medicine, and board of pharmacy.

Expect some of these to take weeks, so start them while your LLC filing is in process.

## Insurance and Risk Management

Insurance is the real protection for an IV business. At minimum, discuss these with a broker who understands mobile healthcare:

- **Professional liability (malpractice) insurance** for the company and for each clinician, since individual coverage may not extend to your business operations.
- **General liability** for non-clinical claims such as property damage.
- **Commercial auto** if the company owns or leases a vehicle, or **hired and non-owned auto** if staff use their own cars.
- **Workers' compensation**, which most states require once you have employees.
- **Cyber/privacy coverage** if you store patient information. You'll likely handle health data, and HIPAA may apply to you depending on how you operate and bill.

Premiums vary by state, services offered, and claims history, so get several quotes. Be wary of "IV hydration business insurance" policies that exclude the exact services you offer, such as high-dose vitamin infusions or services at events. Read the exclusions, not just the premium.

Pair insurance with good habits: written consent forms, documented screening, emergency protocols, and consistent charting. Also keep company and personal money apart. Mixing them is one of the quickest ways to weaken an LLC's liability protection, and our [LLC bank account guide](/blog/llc-bank-account-how-to-open-one/) walks through opening a business account.

## Taxes: How a Mobile IV Therapy LLC Is Taxed

By default, the IRS treats a single-member LLC as a "disregarded entity," so profits flow to your personal return and are subject to self-employment tax. A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default. The [IRS's LLC overview](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc) explains the classification options.

Many owners later look at an S corporation election once profits are consistent. In an S corp, you pay yourself a "reasonable salary" and take the rest as distributions, which can reduce self-employment tax. It also adds payroll, extra filings, and costs. For illustration only, suppose a mobile IV business nets $120,000 after expenses. As a default single-member LLC, the full amount could be subject to self-employment tax. With an S election, only the reasonable salary portion would be subject to payroll taxes. The actual savings depend on your salary level, state taxes, and administrative costs, so this isn't a promise of any particular result. Our guides on [how LLCs are taxed](/blog/llc-taxes-how-are-llcs-taxed/) and [LLC vs S-Corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) cover the mechanics.

Common deductions for this type of business include:

- Medical supplies, IV bags, tubing, and sharps containers
- Vehicle expenses (mileage or actual costs)
- Malpractice and business insurance premiums
- Medical director fees
- Software for scheduling, payments, and charting
- Marketing and advertising
- Continuing education and licensing fees

Sales tax treatment varies. Some states tax certain wellness services or supplies, and others don't, so ask your CPA. If you hire nurses, be careful about worker classification. Treating someone as a contractor when they work set schedules under your protocols can invite scrutiny. ## Step-by-Step: Setting Up Your LLC for a Mobile IV Therapy Business

Here's a practical order of operations for 2026:

1. **Consult a healthcare attorney** about your state's CPOM rules and the right ownership structure (standard LLC, PLLC, or MSO model).
2. **Choose your state.** In most cases you'll form where you operate, because you'll be doing business there anyway. Forming elsewhere often means paying twice, since you'd still register as a foreign LLC where you work.
3. **Pick a compliant name** and confirm availability. PLLC names often have specific suffix requirements.
4. **Appoint a registered agent.** It must be a real address that can receive legal papers during business hours. Read our [registered agent guide](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/) if you're unsure.
5. **File your articles of organization** directly with the state or through a service like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/).
6. **Draft an operating agreement.** It matters even for a single-member LLC, and it is essential when you have partners or a physician co-owner. See our [operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/).
7. **Get an EIN.** The IRS issues them for free. Our guide to [getting an EIN for free](/blog/how-to-get-an-ein-for-your-llc-free-irs-method/) shows the process.
8. **Open a business bank account** and keep finances separate.
9. **Line up your medical director, clinicians, protocols, insurance, and permits** before you take your first paying client.
10. **Calendar your compliance dates**, such as annual reports and license renewals.

On beneficial ownership reporting: federal rules have changed several times. FinCEN's March 2025 interim rule removed the reporting requirement for U.S.-formed companies and their owners. Rules can change again, so check the current status in our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) or at FinCEN directly.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Assuming an LLC covers malpractice.** It doesn't protect you from your own professional negligence.
- **Skipping the medical director agreement** or letting it be informal.
- **Ignoring state lines.** Serving clients across a border can trigger licensing issues in a second state.
- **Using a standard LLC where a PLLC or MSO is required.**
- **Commingling funds.** Pay yourself with draws or payroll, not by using the business card for groceries.
- **Skipping the operating agreement.**
- **Making health claims in marketing** that the FDA, FTC, or your state board might view as unsupported. Have counsel review your advertising language.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need an LLC to start a mobile IV therapy business?

No law says you must have one, but operating as a sole proprietor leaves your personal assets exposed to business debts and claims. Most owners choose an LLC, a PLLC, or a corporation. Because of CPOM and licensing rules, the right choice depends on your state and your credentials.

### Can a non-medical person own an IV therapy business?

In some states, yes. In others, a non-clinician can't directly own an entity that provides medical services and may need an MSO structure with a physician-owned practice. Consult a healthcare attorney in your state before you form.

### What is an IV therapy PLLC?

A PLLC is a professional limited liability company, available in many states for licensed professionals. Members generally must hold the relevant license, and each state sets its own rules about eligible professions, naming, and filings.

### How much does it cost to start an LLC for a mobile IV therapy business?

The filing fee alone ranges from roughly $35 to $500 depending on the state. Add a registered agent, any formation service plan, an attorney consult, and insurance. The LLC filing is typically the smallest part of your total startup budget. Medical supplies, insurance, and director fees usually cost more.

### Do I need a medical director for IV therapy?

Many states require physician oversight or standing orders for IV administration, though the details vary. Confirm your state's requirements with its medical and nursing boards and a qualified attorney.

### Does an LLC protect me from malpractice claims?

Not entirely. An LLC generally shields you from the company's business debts and from other people's liability. It doesn't remove responsibility for your own professional negligence, which is why professional liability insurance is essential.

### Should I elect S corporation status for my IV business?

It can make sense once profit is steady, because it may reduce self-employment tax. It also adds payroll and compliance costs and requires a reasonable salary. Talk to a CPA before filing Form 2553. See our [Form 2553 guide](/blog/how-to-file-form-2553-for-an-llc/).
