You may work in three states in a single year, collect tax-free stipends, and wonder whether a business entity would help or hurt. The honest answer is that an LLC for travel nurses is useful in some situations and a waste of money in others. This guide explains which is which.

## Does a Travel Nurse Need an LLC? The Short Answer

Most travel nurses are **W-2 employees of a staffing agency**. In that arrangement, the agency withholds payroll taxes, carries workers' compensation, and generally covers the employer-side obligations. Forming an LLC does not change any of that. If you take a W-2 assignment, your paycheck still flows through payroll, and an LLC sitting beside it adds filing costs without adding protection or tax savings.

An LLC starts to make sense when you earn income outside the W-2 relationship. Common examples:

- **1099 contract assignments.** Some facilities, per diem platforms, and smaller agencies pay nurses as independent contractors. This is the main scenario where a travel nurse LLC makes sense. See the [LLC for freelancers and independent contractors guide](/blog/llc-for-freelancers-and-independent-contractors/) for the broader picture.
- **Side businesses.** Nurse consulting, legal nurse consulting, health coaching, content creation, telehealth education, or a blog about travel nursing.
- **Rental property.** Many travelers keep a home base or buy a rental while on the road. An LLC can separate that real estate liability from your other assets.
- **Planning for a future practice.** Nurse practitioners who plan to open a clinic should read our guide to an [LLC for nurse practitioners](/blog/llc-for-nurse-practitioners/), because professional entity rules differ.

I've seen too many healthcare workers form an LLC because a coworker or a social media post said they "must" have one. Then they pay a state annual fee for years on an entity that never receives a dollar of income. In my experience, the nurses who benefit are the ones with a specific business reason, not the ones chasing a vague tax break.

## What an LLC Will Not Do for a Travel Nurse

A few misconceptions come up repeatedly, so it's worth clearing them up.

**It won't protect you from your own malpractice.** An LLC shields your personal assets from the business's debts and from other people's negligence. It does not shield you from liability for your own professional mistakes. Your nursing license, your employer's coverage, and your own professional liability insurance carry that risk. An LLC is not a replacement for any of them.

**It won't make your stipends tax-free.** Housing and meals & incidental expense (M&IE) stipends are generally non-taxable only if you maintain a legitimate "tax home" and incur duplicate living expenses while working away from it. The IRS explains the rules in [Publication 463](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p463). An LLC has no effect on whether you qualify. If you can't show a tax home, the stipends can be reclassified as taxable wages, and no entity structure fixes that. Review these rules with a tax professional who works with traveling healthcare workers.

**It won't convert a W-2 job into a business expense account.** If your agency treats you as an employee, many job-related deductions are limited under current federal law. An LLC does not reopen them.

**It won't automatically lower your taxes.** By default, a single-member LLC is taxed as a "disregarded entity," meaning the IRS treats your income as if you earned it directly. Our breakdown of [how LLCs are taxed](/blog/llc-taxes-how-are-llcs-taxed/) covers the default rules and the elections available.

## Travel Nurse Taxes: How an LLC Fits In

Understanding travel nurse taxes is the key to deciding on an entity. There are three layers to think about.

### 1. Your tax home and state income tax

You generally owe tax to the state where you earn income, plus possibly your home state. Many travelers file multiple state returns. Some assignments land in states with no income tax, such as Texas, Florida, or Tennessee, while others, like California or New York, do not. Your LLC's formation state doesn't change which states tax your wages. It is your work location and residency that matter.

### 2. W-2 versus 1099 income

On a W-2 assignment, the agency pays half of your Social Security and Medicare taxes. If you work through a 1099 arrangement, which is common enough that "travel nurse 1099" is a frequent search, you pay the full self-employment tax yourself. That's 15.3% on net earnings up to the Social Security wage base (roughly $184,500 in 2026), then 2.9% above it, plus a possible 0.9% additional Medicare tax at higher incomes. You'll also need to make quarterly estimated payments; our [LLC quarterly tax payments guide](/blog/llc-quarterly-tax-payments-guide/) walks through the deadlines. Learn more about the math in [self-employment tax for LLC owners](/blog/self-employment-tax-for-llc-owners/).

### 3. The S-Corp election

If you have substantial 1099 income, your LLC can elect S-Corp taxation, which may reduce self-employment tax by splitting your income into a "reasonable salary" and distributions. This only tends to pay off at higher profit levels, usually after accounting for payroll costs and added accounting fees. Compare the trade-offs in [LLC vs S-Corp: which is better for taxes](/blog/llc-vs-s-corp-which-is-better-for-taxes/). Do not make this election without advice from a CPA, as the IRS scrutinizes unreasonably low salaries.

Here is a simple illustration, using hypothetical numbers only. A nurse earns $90,000 net from a 1099 contract. Self-employment tax on that is roughly $12,700 at the 15.3% rate (applied to 92.35% of net earnings). If the nurse instead elects S-Corp status and pays a reasonable salary of $65,000, payroll taxes apply to the salary rather than the full amount, which might reduce the bill. However, you must offset that against payroll software, a separate business return, and state-level costs. Results vary widely, so this is an illustration, not a prediction.

## LLC vs Sole Proprietorship for 1099 Nurses

If you earn 1099 income and do nothing, you're a sole proprietor by default. That's not illegal or unusual, but it leaves your personal assets exposed to business debts, such as a contract dispute, a vehicle accident on business use, or a lease problem. Our [LLC vs sole proprietorship](/blog/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/) guide covers this in depth.

An LLC gives you:

- A liability barrier between business obligations and personal assets (if you keep finances separate)
- A cleaner way to open a business bank account; see [how to open an LLC bank account](/blog/llc-bank-account-how-to-open-one/)
- A more professional vendor and client presence
- Flexibility to elect different tax treatment later

The drawbacks are real, too: formation fees, annual report fees in many states, and the need to keep business and personal money separate. If you commingle funds, a court can disregard the liability shield.

For most travelers, the answer is **your home state**, the same state as your tax home. Forming in Delaware or Wyoming rarely makes sense for a small operation, because you'd then typically need to register as a foreign LLC in your home state and pay fees in both. ## Professional Licensing: A Critical Check

Before forming anything, check whether your state and your type of work permit nurses to operate through an LLC. Registered nurses are often not allowed to provide nursing services through a standard LLC, and some states require a professional LLC (PLLC) or restrict ownership to licensed professionals. If your LLC is intended for non-clinical activities such as consulting, education, or rental property, this is usually less of a concern. But if the LLC would bill for hands-on nursing, ask your state board of nursing and an attorney first. Advanced practice nurses should start with the [nurse practitioner PLLC guide](/blog/llc-for-nurse-practitioners/).

The [U.S. Small Business Administration](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/choose-business-structure) offers a useful overview of business structures if you want a neutral source to compare against.

## Step-by-Step: Setting Up a Travel Nurse LLC Efficiently

1. **Confirm your purpose.** Write down exactly what income the LLC will receive (1099 contracts, consulting, rental). If you can't, wait.
2. **Check licensing rules.** Contact your state board of nursing if the LLC will touch clinical work.
3. **Choose your state.** Usually your home state and tax home.
4. **Pick a name and check availability** through your Secretary of State's search.
5. **Appoint a registered agent.** Use a professional service rather than your own address.
6. **File your articles of organization.** Your formation service will do this, or you can file directly. See [what are articles of organization](/blog/what-are-articles-of-organization/).
7. **Draft an operating agreement.** Even single-member LLCs benefit from one. The [operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/) explains what to include.
8. **Get an EIN.** It's free directly from the IRS; see [how to get an EIN for free](/blog/how-to-get-an-ein-for-your-llc-free-irs-method/).
9. **Open a dedicated business bank account** and never mix funds.
10. **Calendar your deadlines**, including annual reports and estimated tax payments.

Total cost for a typical home-state formation: your state filing fee (often $50 to $300), plus any optional service fees and a registered agent if not included. Our [cost breakdown](/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-form-an-llc/) goes through the range.

## Common Mistakes Travelers Make

- **Forming the LLC but still taking W-2 pay personally.** Then the LLC has no income and no purpose.
- **Assuming the LLC solves tax-home problems.** It doesn't. Keep documentation of your permanent residence, bills, and duplicate living expenses.
- **Skipping the annual report.** Missing it can lead to administrative dissolution; see [what happens if you don't renew your LLC](/blog/what-happens-if-you-don-t-renew-your-llc/).
- **Using a temporary address as the registered agent.** Legal notices get lost.
- **Not carrying professional liability insurance.** The LLC is not a substitute.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do travel nurses need an LLC?

Not usually. If you work W-2 assignments through an agency, an LLC generally won't change your taxes or your liability for your own nursing practice. It becomes useful if you have 1099 income, a side business, or rental property.

### Can a travel nurse have an LLC and still be a W-2 employee?

Yes. Many nurses work W-2 assignments and run a separate side business through an LLC. The two are independent, but be sure your employment contract does not restrict outside work.

### Can an LLC protect me from malpractice claims as a nurse?

No. An LLC protects personal assets from business debts and other people's actions, but it does not shield you from liability for your own professional negligence. Professional liability insurance is the main protection there.

### What state should a travel nurse form an LLC in?

Typically your home state and tax home state. Forming elsewhere often means paying fees in two states. Evaluate costs using our state-by-state cost guides and consult a tax professional.

### How much does an LLC cost for a travel nurse?

State filing fees range from roughly $35 to $500, plus ongoing annual fees in many states. Formation services such as [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) start at $0 plus the state fee, with paid tiers adding services.

### Are travel nurse stipends taxable if I have an LLC?

An LLC doesn't change the stipend rules. Stipends are generally non-taxable only if you maintain a valid tax home and incur duplicate expenses. Check IRS Publication 463 and speak with a CPA.

### Do I need to register my LLC in every state I work in?

Not necessarily. If you're a W-2 employee working in another state, your LLC usually isn't "doing business" there. If the LLC itself provides services or owns property in that state, registration may be required. Rules vary by state.

### Should a 1099 travel nurse elect S-Corp status?

It can reduce self-employment tax at higher profit levels, but it adds payroll and filing costs. Run the numbers with a CPA before electing.
