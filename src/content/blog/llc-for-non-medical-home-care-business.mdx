It also puts you in a client's home, often with a vulnerable adult, so the liability exposure is real. Forming an LLC is the standard first step toward separating that exposure from your personal assets.

## Why an LLC Fits a Non-Medical Home Care Business

An LLC is a legal entity separate from you. If it is set up and run properly, a business debt or a lawsuit against the company generally can't reach your house or personal savings. Our [What Is an LLC?](/learn/what-is-an-llc/) guide covers the basics.

For a **non-medical home care agency LLC**, three features matter most:

- **Liability separation.** A client falls, a family member alleges neglect, or an item goes missing. The claim is typically against the company, not you personally.
- **Credibility.** Families, referral sources, long-term care insurers and some state programs expect a registered business with an EIN, a business bank account and insurance.
- **Tax flexibility.** An LLC is taxed as a sole proprietorship (single member) or partnership by default. You can later elect S-corp taxation if profits justify it. See our [LLC vs S-Corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) comparison.

In my experience, the most common mistake new owners make is treating the LLC as a magic shield. I've seen caregivers form an LLC, then run client payments through a personal account and skip insurance. That can invite a court to disregard the entity. The LLC protects you only if you operate it as a separate business, and it does nothing about your own negligence. Insurance is what covers that risk.

## Do You Need a License for Non-Medical Home Care?

An LLC is not a license. This is the single biggest compliance question, and the answer depends on your state.

- Some states require a license for any agency that provides personal care, even without medical tasks. California, for example, requires Home Care Organizations to be licensed and caregivers to be registered under its Home Care Services Consumer Protection Act.
- Some states have a lighter registration or a "homemaker/companion" category with fewer rules.
- Some states regulate only agencies that touch the body (bathing, toileting, transferring) and leave pure companion care largely unlicensed.
- Medicaid waiver programs, VA contracts and long-term care insurers typically add their own provider enrollment and training requirements.

Wording matters. Whether a task counts as "companion care" or "personal care" can determine which license applies, so check your state health department or aging agency before you advertise services. If you plan to offer anything medical (wound care, medication administration, skilled nursing), you're in home health territory. ## Step-by-Step: Forming Your LLC

Expert tip from the CFO side of the table: do the steps in this order, because licensing and banking depend on the entity existing first.

1. **Choose your state.** For a local service business, form in the state where you operate. Forming elsewhere and registering as a foreign LLC in your home state means paying fees in both. Our [best state to form an LLC](/blog/best-state-to-form-an-llc/) guide explains why.
2. **Pick a name.** Check availability with your Secretary of State and consider your future marketing. A name that says "home care" or "senior care" may trigger extra review or licensing disclosures in some states.
3. **Appoint a registered agent.** You need a physical address in the state to receive legal papers during business hours. Many formation services include one for the first year.
4. **File the Articles of Organization.** State fees typically range from about $35 to $500 depending on the state. See [how much an LLC costs](/learn/how-much-does-an-llc-cost/) for a state-by-state view.
5. **Write an operating agreement.** Even single-member LLCs need one. It supports your liability protection and helps with bank accounts. See our [operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/).
6. **Get an EIN.** You need one to hire caregivers. The IRS issues them free at [IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/apply-for-an-employer-identification-number-ein-online).
7. **Open a business bank account.** Keep client payments and payroll separate from personal funds.
8. **Apply for state and local licenses.** Add business licenses, tax registrations and any home care license required by your state.

## Insurance: The Part That Actually Protects You

The LLC limits personal exposure to business liabilities. It does not pay claims. Home care owners generally need to look at:

- **General and professional liability.** Covers claims of injury, property damage, or errors in care. Some clients' families and referral partners will ask for a certificate of insurance.
- **Abuse and molestation coverage.** Often a separate endorsement or policy. Many insurers ask for it for agencies that serve vulnerable adults.
- **Non-owned auto liability.** If caregivers drive clients or run errands in their own vehicles, a personal auto policy may not respond to a business-use claim.
- **Workers' compensation.** Required in most states once you have employees.
- **Fidelity bond / crime coverage.** Covers theft by employees. Many agencies carry it because caregivers have access to client homes and belongings.

Premiums vary widely by state, services offered, number of caregivers and claims history. Get quotes from agents who specialize in home care before you take on your first client.

## Hiring Caregivers: Employees vs. Contractors

This is where small agencies get into expensive trouble. Many new owners want to treat caregivers as 1099 contractors to save on payroll taxes. For an agency that sets schedules, assigns clients and controls how work is done, that classification is often hard to defend.

- The Department of Labor's wage-and-hour rules for home care workers have shifted over the years. The 2013 rule that limited the "companionship" overtime exemption for third-party employers such as agencies was the subject of proposed changes in 2025. Check the [U.S. Department of Labor's home care page](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/direct-care) for the current rule before you set pay policy.
- State rules can be stricter than federal ones, including overtime and live-in worker rules.
- Misclassification can lead to back taxes, penalties and unpaid wages claims.

An LLC can have employees, and most agencies should plan on W-2 caregivers. Our guide on [whether an LLC can have employees](/blog/can-an-llc-have-employees/) covers payroll registration and withholding. Budget for employer payroll taxes, workers' comp and background checks, and run payroll through a service rather than by hand.

## Taxes for a Home Care LLC

A single-member LLC is taxed as a "disregarded entity" by default: profit flows to your personal return, and you pay income tax plus self-employment tax (15.3% on net earnings up to the Social Security wage base, then 2.9% Medicare above that). See the IRS's [self-employed tax guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employed-individuals-tax-center) for current thresholds.

A worked example, for illustration only: suppose your agency nets $90,000 in a year. As a default LLC owner, self-employment tax applies to most of that figure. If you elect S-corp status and pay yourself a "reasonable" salary, say $55,000, payroll taxes apply to the salary and the remaining distribution generally avoids self-employment tax. The savings can be real but are offset by payroll costs, additional filings and the reasonable-salary requirement. For most owners, the break-even point comes after the business is consistently profitable, not in year one. Talk to a CPA before filing Form 2553. Our [LLC tax deductions list](/blog/llc-tax-deductions-list/) covers common write-offs, such as mileage between client visits, background checks, training and liability insurance.

Also watch for state-level costs: some states charge annual LLC fees or franchise taxes, and some localities have business license taxes. For example, [California charges an $800 minimum annual franchise tax](/blog/california-llc-800-franchise-tax/) on LLCs doing business there.

## What About the BOI Report?

Beneficial ownership reporting has changed repeatedly. In March 2025, FinCEN issued an interim final rule that removed the requirement for U.S.-formed companies and U.S. persons to file BOI reports. Rules may change again, so check [FinCEN's BOI page](https://www.fincen.gov/boi) and our [BOI report changes and updates](/blog/boi-report-changes-and-updates-2026/) article before assuming you have no filing duties.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Advertising services you aren't licensed for.** Calling something "companion care" doesn't exempt you if your caregivers actually provide hands-on personal care.
- **Skipping the operating agreement.** Banks, insurers and courts all look to it.
- **Commingling funds.** Pay yourself through draws or payroll and keep business funds separate.
- **Missing annual reports.** A lapsed LLC can lose good standing, which can hold up licenses and contracts. See [what happens if you don't renew your LLC](/blog/what-happens-if-you-don-t-renew-your-llc/).
- **Using a client-facing service agreement you wrote from scratch.** Have a local attorney review it, including limits on liability, scope of services, and what happens when a client's condition changes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need an LLC to start a non-medical home care business?

No law requires one, but it is a common choice because it separates business liabilities from your personal assets. Pair it with insurance. An LLC alone doesn't cover claims arising from your own or your caregivers' actions.

### How much does it cost to form an LLC for a home care business?

State filing fees typically run from about $35 to $500. Formation services such as ZenBusiness and LegalZoom charge extra for optional bundles, and some offer a $0 base package plus state fees. Licensing, insurance and payroll costs are separate and often larger than the filing fee.

### Do I need a license to run a non-medical home care agency?

It depends on your state. Some states require an agency license even for non-medical personal care, while others regulate only certain services or none at all. Check with your state health or aging department before you take clients.

### Should caregivers be employees or independent contractors?

Agencies that control schedules, client assignments and how work is performed will usually need W-2 employees. Classification rules vary by state and by federal agency, so confirm with an employment attorney or the Department of Labor.

### Should I elect S-corp status for my home care LLC?

Possibly, once profits are steady and high enough that payroll-tax savings outweigh the added costs. It requires paying yourself a reasonable salary. A CPA can model it for your numbers.

### Can I run the business from my home address?

Often yes, but your LLC's address and registered agent address can become public record. A registered agent service, such as [Northwest Registered Agent](/go/northwest/), can keep your home address off the filing. Check local zoning and licensing rules for home-based offices too.
