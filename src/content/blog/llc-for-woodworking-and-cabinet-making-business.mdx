A woodworking shop is a business built on sharp blades, fine dust, expensive lumber, and customers who are trusting you with their homes. A cabinet that pulls off the wall, a table saw injury to an employee, or a finish that triggers a dispute over a $40,000 kitchen can all land on you personally if you operate as a sole proprietor. An LLC for a woodworking and cabinet making business separates your business obligations from your house, savings, and personal vehicles, and in 2026 it is one of the most cost-effective protections available to a shop owner.

This guide covers what an LLC does and does not do for a woodworker, what it costs, how it is taxed, which insurance you still need, and how to set it up without wasting time. ## Why a Woodworking or Cabinet Shop Needs an LLC

Woodworking carries risks that many home-based crafters underestimate. The business is physical, the products are installed in other people's property, and the contracts often involve large deposits.

**Product and installation liability.** If a built-in bookcase tips and injures a child, or a custom stair rail fails, the claim is against the maker and installer. Without an entity, a judgment can reach your personal assets.

**Contract disputes.** Custom cabinetry commonly involves deposits of 30% to 50% before work starts. A $60,000 kitchen job with a late delivery, a measurement error, or a color mismatch can turn into a lawsuit or a chargeback fight. An LLC keeps that dispute at the business level, as long as you keep it properly separate.

**Workplace injuries.** Even one part-time helper or apprentice changes your risk profile. Table saws, jointers, and spray finishing are among the most common sources of serious shop injuries, and the U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration publishes standards and guidance for woodworking operations at [OSHA.gov](https://www.osha.gov/).

**Fire and equipment risks.** Dust collection, finishing solvents, and rags soaked in oil finishes create real fire hazards. A shop fire that spreads to a neighboring unit can generate claims far beyond your own losses.

In my experience, the woodworkers who regret not forming an LLC are rarely the ones who were careless. They are the ones who did $15,000 a year in side work, grew into $150,000 without noticing, and never updated their structure. If you are taking deposits or installing work in customers' homes, you are past the hobby stage. For a broader look at the basic trade-off, see our guide on [LLC vs sole proprietorship](/blog/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/).

## What an LLC Will and Won't Protect

A woodworking LLC offers limited liability, which generally means that business debts and lawsuits are satisfied from business assets rather than your personal ones. That is valuable, but it has limits you should understand before you rely on it.

**What it generally protects:**
- Personal savings, home, and vehicles from business contract debts, supplier balances, and lease obligations
- Your personal assets from many customer claims arising from the business, subject to the next section

**What it does not protect:**
- **Your own negligence.** Courts generally hold individuals responsible for their own tortious acts. If you personally install a cabinet badly and someone is hurt, you can still be named. This is why insurance matters alongside the entity.
- **Personal guarantees.** Landlords, equipment lenders, and lumber suppliers often require them. You are on the hook regardless of the LLC.
- **Piercing the veil.** If you mix personal and business funds, skip required filings, or undercapitalize the company, a court may disregard the LLC. Keeping a separate bank account and clean records is non-negotiable.
- **Taxes.** Payroll taxes withheld but not paid can create personal liability for "responsible persons."

## Cabinet Shop Liability Insurance and Other Coverage

The LLC is the legal shield. Insurance is what actually pays the claims. For a cabinet maker LLC, a typical coverage stack in 2026 looks like this:

| Coverage | What it covers | Typical need |
|---|---|---|
| General liability | Third-party injury or property damage, including at install sites | Almost every shop; many clients and landlords require it |
| Products/completed operations | Claims after the work is delivered or installed | Critical for installed cabinetry |
| Commercial property / inland marine | Tools, machinery, and inventory, including items in transit | Shops with significant equipment |
| Workers' compensation | Employee injuries | Required in most states once you have employees (rules vary) |
| Commercial auto | Vehicles used for delivery and installation | If you drive a work truck or van |

Premiums vary widely by state, revenue, payroll, and whether you install. Rather than quote a single number, I recommend getting at least three quotes and telling each broker exactly whether you build only, install, or both. Installation typically changes how an insurer classifies you.

A quick gotcha: some policies exclude or limit coverage for work done by uninsured subcontractors. If you hire installers on a 1099 basis, confirm they carry their own coverage and ask for certificates of insurance.

## Licensing: When a Cabinet Maker Needs a Contractor License

This is the part many woodworkers overlook. Building cabinets in your shop is generally unlicensed manufacturing. Installing them in a customer's home can trigger contractor licensing in some states, depending on job size and scope.

- Some states require a contractor license once the total project price exceeds a set threshold, often including both labor and materials.
- Some states have specific specialty classifications for cabinetry or finish carpentry.
- Many cities and counties also require a general business license and may require a home-occupation permit if you work from a garage.

Requirements differ significantly state to state, so check with your state contractor licensing board before taking installation jobs. If you do a lot of installs and remodel work, the guidance in our [LLC for construction company](/blog/llc-for-construction-company/) and [LLC for handyman and home services](/blog/llc-for-handyman-and-home-services/) articles is relevant. Note that a license is typically issued to a specific legal entity, so form the LLC first, then apply in the LLC's name.

## How a Woodworking LLC Is Taxed

By default, the IRS treats a single-member LLC as a "disregarded entity." That means profits flow to your personal return (Schedule C), and you pay income tax plus self-employment tax on net earnings. A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default. The IRS explains these classifications on its [LLC page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc).

**Self-employment tax.** For 2026, self-employment tax is generally 15.3% on net earnings up to the Social Security wage base, with the Medicare portion continuing above it. Confirm current thresholds with the IRS or your tax advisor. Our [self-employment tax guide for LLC owners](/blog/self-employment-tax-for-llc-owners/) goes deeper.

**The S-corp question.** Once your shop is netting consistently above roughly $80,000 to $100,000 a year, it is worth asking a CPA whether an S-corporation election could reduce self-employment tax. You must pay yourself a "reasonable salary," and you take on payroll compliance. It is not automatically better. A rough illustration, not advice: if a shop nets $120,000 and a CPA determines a reasonable salary is $70,000, only that salary is subject to payroll taxes, while the remaining distribution is not. Added payroll and tax-prep costs can eat into the savings. Read [LLC vs S-Corp](/blog/llc-vs-s-corp-which-is-better-for-taxes/) before deciding.

**Deductions woodworkers commonly use.** Examples include lumber and hardware, blades and bits, shop rent or a qualifying home-shop portion, equipment (including depreciation or Section 179 where eligible), shop insurance, software for design and cut lists, vehicle expenses for deliveries, and trade-show or marketing costs. Keep receipts and mileage logs. Whether a given item qualifies depends on your facts, so confirm with a tax professional.

**Quarterly estimates.** If you expect to owe $1,000 or more, you generally need to make quarterly estimated payments. Many cabinet makers get surprised by a large April bill after a strong year. Our [LLC quarterly tax payments guide](/blog/llc-quarterly-tax-payments-guide/) covers the schedule.

**Sales tax.** Many states tax sales of tangible goods, including finished furniture and cabinets, and a few treat installed cabinetry as a real-property improvement with different rules. Get your seller's permit where required and confirm how your state treats installed work.

## Step-by-Step: Setting Up Your Woodworking LLC

1. **Pick your state.** For a shop that operates from one location, form the LLC in the state where you physically work. Forming elsewhere usually means paying two sets of fees because you must register as a foreign LLC at home anyway.
2. **Choose a name.** Check availability with your Secretary of State. Include "LLC" and avoid names too close to existing businesses. Many shop owners also register a trade name.
3. **Appoint a registered agent.** This can be you, but you must be available at a physical address during business hours. Many owners hire a service so a notice is not missed while they are on an install. See [what a registered agent is](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/).
4. **File Articles of Organization.** A formation service can submit them for you, or you can file directly with the state.
5. **Write an operating agreement.** Even for a single owner, this document supports your liability protection. Partners need it even more to define who owns the tools, who covers losses, and what happens if one leaves. Our [operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/) and [template article](/blog/llc-operating-agreement-template-and-guide/) can help.
6. **Get an EIN.** You need it to open a business bank account and hire employees.
7. **Open a business bank account.** Run all deposits and supplier payments through it. See our guide on [opening an LLC bank account](/blog/llc-bank-account-how-to-open-one/).
8. **Get licenses, permits, and insurance.** Business license, sales tax permit, contractor license if needed, and your policy stack.
9. **Calendar your compliance dates.** Annual reports, franchise taxes, and renewals vary by state.

A note on federal beneficial ownership reporting: rules for domestic companies changed in 2025, and requirements have been shifting. Check the current status at [FinCEN.gov](https://www.fincen.gov/boi) and see our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) before assuming you do or do not need to file.

## Keeping the LLC Protection Intact

Forming the entity is the easy part. Keeping it protective is where I have seen shops fail. A few practices make a real difference:

- **Contract in the LLC's name.** Sign proposals and installation contracts as "Your Shop Name, LLC, by [your name], Member."
- **Use written contracts with clear scope.** Include dimensions, materials, finish, lead time, payment schedule, change-order process, and a warranty limitation.
- **Don't commingle.** Do not pay for groceries from the business account. If you take money out, call it an owner draw and record it.
- **Separate equipment ownership clearly.** If you own the table saw personally and lease it to the LLC, document it.
- **Keep your records.** Maintain books, receipts, and a simple minutes log of major decisions.
- **Use a deposit account approach.** Customer deposits tied to specific jobs should be tracked so they are not spent on unrelated expenses.

If you also run an online shop for smaller items such as cutting boards or furniture kits, the rules for [online business and ecommerce](/blog/llc-for-online-business-and-ecommerce/) apply too.

## When You Might Not Need an LLC Yet

Honesty matters here. If you build occasional pieces as a hobby, sell a few items at craft fairs, have no employees, and do no installs, an LLC may be more paperwork than value, especially in a state with a high annual fee. A good product liability policy and a sole proprietorship might be enough for a short period.

But the moment you take large deposits, do on-site installs, hire anyone, lease a commercial space, or work with contractors and designers who want a proper entity, form the LLC. The cost of waiting is paid in risk, not dollars.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need an LLC for a woodworking business?
There is no general legal requirement to form an LLC, but it is strongly advisable once you sell custom work, take deposits, install in customers' homes, or hire help. It limits your personal exposure to business debts and many claims, though it does not replace insurance.

### How much does it cost to start an LLC for a cabinet making business?
Expect to pay your state's filing fee, typically between about $35 and $500, plus optional service fees. Some services such as [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) offer a $0 entry plan plus the state fee. Ongoing costs include annual report fees and, in some states, franchise taxes.

### Does an LLC protect me if a cabinet I installed injures someone?
It can protect your personal assets from the business's liabilities in many situations, but you can still be personally liable for your own negligence. That is why general liability and products/completed operations coverage are essential for installers.

### Should I form my LLC as an S-corp?
Not automatically. An S-corp election can reduce self-employment tax once profits are high enough, but it requires running payroll and paying a reasonable salary. Many shops do not benefit until net profit reaches the range of roughly $80,000 to $100,000 or more. Talk to a CPA.

### Do I need a contractor license to install the cabinets I build?
It depends on your state, the project value, and the scope of work. Some states require a license above a dollar threshold, while others have specialty classifications. Check with your state licensing board before bidding installation work.

### What insurance does a woodworking LLC need?
At minimum, most shops carry general liability with completed-operations coverage, property or inland marine coverage for tools, and workers' compensation if they have employees. Add commercial auto if you use a vehicle for deliveries.

### Can I run my woodworking LLC from my garage?
Often yes, but check zoning rules, your HOA, and your homeowner's policy. Standard home policies commonly exclude business operations, so you may need a separate commercial policy. Noise, dust, and fire code rules can also apply.
