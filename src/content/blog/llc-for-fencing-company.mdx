Fencing looks like simple work from the street: dig holes, set posts, hang panels, collect the check. Anyone who has run a fence crew knows better. Every job starts with digging into ground you can't see, on a property line you didn't survey, often next to a neighbor who never agreed to the project. One nicked gas line, one fence set two feet onto the wrong lot, or one pool gate that doesn't latch can produce a claim many times larger than the contract. An LLC for fencing company owners is the most common way to keep those business risks from reaching your house, your truck, and your savings.

The formation part is the easy bit. [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) will file your Articles of Organization for $0 plus your state's filing fee on its Starter plan, and most states approve a new LLC within a few business days to two weeks. The harder questions are the ones generic formation guides skip: what an LLC actually protects when you're the one running the auger, how it fits with your fence contractor license and bond, and how to set up taxes so a strong spring season doesn't hand the IRS more than its share.

This guide covers those questions for 2026. It's written for owner-operators running residential install crews, commercial chain-link and ornamental shops, and fence-and-deck outfits that want to grow.

## Why Fencing Contractors Need an LLC in 2026

A fence contractor LLC is a separate legal entity that holds your contracts, equipment, debts, and obligations. If the business gets sued or can't pay a supplier, creditors are generally limited to what the LLC owns, not your personal property. A sole proprietor has no such separation: legally, the business *is* you, so a judgment against the business is a judgment against you.

Fence work has a few risks that make that separation worth more than in many trades:

- **Underground utility strikes.** Every post hole is a small excavation. Federal law and state "dig safe" laws expect you to call 811 before you dig ([Call 811](https://call811.com/) explains the process), and a single gas, fiber, or electrical strike can bring repair bills, service-outage claims from the utility, and sometimes fines. Even with a locate ticket, private lines such as sprinkler mains, invisible dog fences, and lines to detached garages usually aren't marked.
- **Property line and encroachment disputes.** Install a fence six inches onto the neighbor's lot and you may be named in a trespass or quiet-title fight between two homeowners, even if your customer pointed to the "line."
- **Pool barrier and code compliance.** Pool fences are life-safety equipment. Most local codes based on the International Swimming Pool and Spa Code set minimum heights, gap limits, and self-closing, self-latching gate rules. A gate that fails and a child who gets through is the worst-case claim in this industry.
- **Vehicle, equipment, and jobsite injuries.** Skid steers, augers, post drivers, and trailers full of steel posts all create injury and property-damage exposure for your crew, customers, and passersby.
- **Supplier credit.** Fence distributors often extend net-30 terms on lumber, vinyl, and aluminum. If a large commercial job goes unpaid, you want that supplier debt sitting with the business, not with you.

In my experience, the fencing contractors who get hurt worst aren't the ones doing sloppy work. They're the ones who did a clean job, got pulled into a boundary fight between two neighbors, and learned that everything they owned was fair game because the business and the owner were legally the same person.

### What an LLC Does Not Protect

An LLC isn't a force field, and anyone who tells you it is wants to sell you something. It generally does **not** protect you from:

- **Your own negligence.** If you personally ran the auger through a marked gas line, a plaintiff can sue you individually as well as the LLC.
- **Personal guarantees.** Most fence suppliers, equipment lenders, and landlords ask new LLCs for a personal guarantee. Sign one and that debt is yours regardless of the entity.
- **Unpaid payroll taxes.** The IRS can hold "responsible persons" personally liable for withheld payroll taxes the business didn't pay over.
- **Commingling and sloppy records.** If you run personal expenses through the business account or skip basic formalities, a court can "pierce the veil" and treat the LLC as your alter ego.

That's why the LLC works best as one layer in a stack: entity + insurance + good contracts + clean books.

## How to Set Up an LLC for Your Fencing Company

Forming an LLC for fencing company operations takes about an hour of paperwork if you're organized. Here's the order I recommend.

### 1. Choose Your State and Name

Form in the state where you actually do the work. Owner-operators sometimes read that Delaware or Wyoming is "better," but if you install fences in Ohio, you'll still have to register as a foreign LLC in Ohio, pay both states' fees, and keep a registered agent in both. For a local trade business, a home-state LLC is almost always the right call.

Check that your name is available with your Secretary of State, and search your state's contractor license database too. Some licensing boards require that the name on your license exactly match the legal name of the entity, so decide on "Ridgeline Fence LLC" versus "Ridgeline Fence & Gate LLC" before you file, not after.

### 2. Appoint a Registered Agent

Every LLC needs a [registered agent](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/) with a physical address in the state to accept lawsuits and official notices. You can serve as your own, but your address goes on the public record, and you need to be there during business hours. Fence owners are rarely at a desk. A professional agent typically runs $100 to $300 a year; ZenBusiness includes its registered agent service free for the first year, and [Northwest Registered Agent](/go/northwest/) is a strong choice if keeping your home address off public filings is your main priority.

### 4. Write an Operating Agreement

Even single-member fence companies should have an [LLC operating agreement](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/). It's what proves to a court, a bank, or a licensing board that the LLC is a real, separate business. If you have a partner, perhaps one person sells and estimates while the other runs crews, the operating agreement should spell out ownership percentages, who can sign contracts, how profits are distributed, and what happens if one of you wants out or gets hurt and can't work.

### 5. Get an EIN, Bank Account, and Books

Get a free Employer Identification Number from the IRS, open a dedicated business checking account, and run every deposit and every material purchase through it. Get a business credit card for supply-yard runs so you're never tempted to put a pallet of pickets on your personal card. Basic bookkeeping software like QuickBooks or Xero, with job-costing turned on, will pay for itself the first time you figure out which fence styles actually make you money.

## Fence Contractor License, Bonds, and Permits

Forming an LLC does not license you to build fences. Licensing for fencing is a patchwork, and it's one of the biggest compliance gaps I see in this trade.

- **California** has a dedicated C-13 Fencing classification through the Contractors State License Board. You generally need a license for any job where labor and materials total $1,000 or more (the minor-work threshold was raised from $500 in 2025), and you'll need a contractor bond, currently $25,000. The license can be held by the LLC, but California LLCs also have to carry additional liability coverage under CSLB rules. Check the details on the [CSLB website](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/).
- **Texas and Florida** don't issue a statewide fence contractor license, but many cities and counties require local registration, a business tax receipt, or a permit for each job. Florida counties such as Miami-Dade have their own contractor licensing programs.
- **Many other states** fold fencing into a general "home improvement" or residential contractor registration, often triggered by a dollar threshold, and frequently require a bond or a contribution to a homeowner recovery fund.

Apply for your license in the LLC's name once the entity exists, or update an existing sole-proprietor license to the LLC. Contracts signed by an unlicensed entity can be unenforceable in some states, which means you might not be able to sue to collect payment. That's a nasty surprise after you've already set 200 feet of posts.

Permits are separate from licensing. Many municipalities require a permit for fences over a certain height (often 4 feet in front yards and 6 feet in back yards), for any pool barrier, and for fences in easements or corner-lot sight triangles. Build into your contract who pulls the permit, who confirms HOA approval, and who is responsible for locating the property line. My strong preference: put the burden on the owner to provide a survey or stake the line, in writing, and have them sign off on the layout before you dig.

## Fencing Business Insurance You Need on Top of the LLC

The LLC protects your personal assets from business debts and claims. Insurance pays the claims so the business itself survives. You need both. A typical fencing business insurance stack looks like this:

- **General liability.** Covers third-party bodily injury and property damage, such as a post driver cracking a customer's patio or a neighbor tripping over materials. Small fence contractors commonly pay roughly $1,000 to $3,500 a year for $1 million per occurrence / $2 million aggregate, depending on state, payroll, and claims history. Ask specifically about coverage for underground utility damage; some policies exclude or sublimit it.
- **Commercial auto.** Your personal auto policy likely won't cover a truck hauling a trailer of posts and concrete for business use.
- **Workers' compensation.** Required in nearly every state once you have employees, and fencing rates are high because of lifting, power tools, and auger injuries. Misclassifying crew members as 1099 contractors to dodge comp is one of the fastest ways to get an audit bill.
- **Inland marine / tools and equipment.** Covers augers, skid steers, and trailers stolen from a jobsite or damaged in transit.
- **Umbrella.** An extra $1 million to $2 million of liability above your primary policies is often a few hundred to around a thousand dollars a year and is well worth it if you do pool fencing or commercial work.

Commercial general contractors and property managers will usually require you to name them as additional insured and to provide certificates of insurance before you start. Get a broker who works with specialty trades, not just whoever handles your homeowner's policy.

## Taxes: Choosing the Right Structure for a Fence Contractor LLC

By default, a single-member LLC is taxed as a sole proprietorship and a multi-member LLC as a partnership. The [IRS treats single-member LLCs as "disregarded entities"](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/single-member-limited-liability-companies) for income tax purposes, so profit flows through to your Schedule C and you pay ordinary income tax plus about 15.3% self-employment tax on net earnings.

Once your fencing business is reliably profitable, electing S-corp taxation can reduce that self-employment tax. Here's a simplified illustration (not tax advice; your numbers will differ):

- Net profit: $140,000
- **Default LLC:** self-employment tax applies to roughly the whole $140,000 (subject to the Social Security wage base), around $19,800.
- **LLC taxed as S-corp:** you pay yourself a reasonable salary, say $70,000, and payroll taxes apply to that salary (about $10,700 combined employer and employee share). The remaining $70,000 comes out as distributions not subject to payroll tax.
- **Potential savings:** roughly $9,000 before the extra costs of running payroll, a separate S-corp return, and higher bookkeeping fees (often $1,500 to $3,000 a year).

The key word is "reasonable." The IRS expects your salary to reflect what you'd pay someone else to run crews and sell jobs, and a lowball salary invites scrutiny. Our [LLC vs S-Corp guide](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) goes deeper on when the switch makes sense. For most fence contractors, the break-even point lands somewhere around $60,000 to $80,000 in consistent annual net profit.

A few other tax items specific to fencing:

- **Equipment depreciation.** Section 179 and bonus depreciation can let you write off a new skid steer, auger attachment, or work truck faster. Bonus depreciation rules have shifted several times recently, so confirm the current-year percentage with your CPA before you buy equipment for tax reasons.
- **Sales tax on materials.** Depending on your state, fence installation may be treated as a real property improvement (you pay sales tax on materials) or as a taxable sale (you collect from the customer). Getting this wrong creates a back-tax liability that the LLC won't make disappear.
- **Quarterly estimates.** Fencing is seasonal in much of the country. Set aside 25% to 30% of spring and summer profit in a separate account so your fourth-quarter estimate doesn't arrive during your slowest month.
- **BOI reporting.** FinCEN's interim rule in March 2025 exempted U.S.-formed companies from beneficial ownership reporting under the Corporate Transparency Act. As of 2026, a domestic fence LLC generally doesn't need to file, but rules have changed repeatedly, so check our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) for the latest.

Always consult a CPA or tax attorney before electing S-corp status or making major equipment purchases for tax reasons.

## Running Your Fencing Company LLC the Right Way

Forming the entity is a one-time event. Keeping its protection is ongoing work. These habits matter most:

- **Sign everything as the LLC.** Contracts, estimates, and change orders should read "Ridgeline Fence LLC, by Jane Doe, Member," not just your name.
- **Use a written contract on every job.** Include scope, materials, layout sign-off, property line responsibility, permit responsibility, a utility-locate clause, a payment schedule, and a limited warranty with clear exclusions such as wood shrinkage, ground heave, and damage from mowers or storms.
- **Document the site before you dig.** Photos of 811 marks, existing landscaping, sprinkler heads, and the agreed layout stakes will win you more disputes than any lawyer.
- **Keep business and personal money separate.** No paying the mortgage from the business account. Pay yourself a draw or salary, then spend it.
- **File your annual reports on time.** A lapsed LLC can lose its liability protection and, in some states, its ability to hold a contractor license.
- **Revisit insurance yearly.** As you add trucks, crews, or commercial work, your coverage needs grow.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to start an LLC for a fencing company?

Expect $50 to $500 in state filing fees, depending on your state, plus optional formation service costs. With ZenBusiness's Starter plan, the service fee is $0, so a Texas fencing LLC would cost about $300 up front. Budget separately for your fence contractor license, bond, permits, and insurance, which usually cost far more than the LLC itself.

### Do I need a license to start a fencing business?

It depends on your state and city. California requires a C-13 Fencing license for jobs of $1,000 or more in labor and materials. Texas and Florida have no statewide fence license, but many local governments require registration or permits. Check your state contractor board and your city or county building department before you take deposits.

### Is an LLC or sole proprietorship better for a fence contractor?

For most fence contractors, an LLC is the better choice because it separates business liability from personal assets for a modest cost. A sole proprietorship is simpler but leaves your home and savings exposed. See our [LLC vs sole proprietorship](/learn/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/) comparison for a full breakdown.

### Does an LLC protect me if I hit a gas line while digging post holes?

Partly. The LLC can shield your personal assets from claims against the business, but if you personally operated the equipment negligently, you can still be sued individually. That's why general liability insurance with underground utility coverage, plus a documented 811 locate ticket, matters as much as the LLC itself.

### Can my fencing LLC hire subcontractors instead of employees?

Yes, but classification depends on how much control you have over the work, not what you call the worker. Installers who use your tools, follow your schedule, and work only for you may legally be employees, which triggers payroll taxes and workers' compensation. Collect certificates of insurance from any true subcontractors.

### When should a fencing company LLC elect S-corp status?

Typically once consistent net profit reaches roughly $60,000 to $80,000 a year, when payroll-tax savings outweigh the extra accounting costs. Run the numbers with a CPA first, since a reasonable salary is required and state rules vary.
