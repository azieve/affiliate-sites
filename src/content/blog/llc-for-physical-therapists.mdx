## Do Physical Therapists Need an LLC?

Not legally in every case, but most people running a **physical therapy private practice** should seriously consider one. Here is what a properly formed entity can do for you:

- **Shield personal assets from business debts.** If your clinic signs a lease, buys equipment, or takes out a loan, creditors generally can't reach your house or savings for those obligations, as long as you've kept finances separate.
- **Protect you from a partner's or employee's professional mistakes.** In many states, a PLLC or similar entity shields you from liability for another provider's malpractice. It generally does not shield you from your own.
- **Give you tax flexibility.** You can start as a default pass-through entity and later elect S corporation treatment if profits justify it.
- **Look more professional.** Payers, landlords, and referral sources take a registered entity more seriously than a sole proprietor.

The honest caveat: an LLC is not a substitute for malpractice insurance. If you personally treat a patient negligently, you can be sued and held liable regardless of your entity. In my experience, the most common mistake new clinic owners make is assuming the LLC "covers everything" and then under-insuring. Treat the entity as protection for the *business* side of your risk and insurance as protection for the *clinical* side.

## PLLC vs. LLC: What Your State Allows

This is the part most generic LLC guides skip. Because physical therapy is a regulated profession, many states require licensed professionals to use a specific entity type, or restrict who can own the business.

Broadly, states fall into a few camps:

- **PLLC required or available.** Many states have a professional LLC (PLLC) statute. A **PLLC for physical therapists** typically requires that all members be licensed in the profession, and the entity name often must include "PLLC" or "Professional Limited Liability Company."
- **Standard LLC permitted.** Some states allow licensed professionals to use a regular LLC, sometimes with added requirements such as limits on non-licensed ownership.
- **Professional corporation only.** A few states steer licensed health professionals toward a professional corporation (PC) rather than an LLC.
- **Board approval or certificate of authorization.** Some states require sign-off from the state physical therapy board, or a certificate from the licensing authority, before or after you file with the Secretary of State.

Ownership rules matter too. If you want a non-PT investor, a business partner who isn't licensed, or a spouse who isn't a therapist to hold a stake, a PLLC may prohibit it. Confirm this before you file, because fixing the wrong structure later means amending documents or reforming the entity.

**Action step:** Before choosing a name or filing, check your state's physical therapy practice act and your state board's website, then confirm with your Secretary of State what entity type and name format they require. If you're unsure, a short consult with a healthcare attorney in your state is worth the fee. ## What an LLC Won't Protect You From

Two clear limits to keep in mind:

1. **Your own professional negligence.** Courts generally hold licensed professionals personally liable for their own malpractice. A **physical therapy practice LLC** or PLLC doesn't change that.
2. **Personal guarantees.** Lenders and landlords often ask new clinic owners to personally guarantee loans and leases. If you sign, you're on the hook regardless of the entity.

Veil-piercing is another risk. If you mix personal and business funds, skip formalities, or leave the entity undercapitalized, a court may disregard the LLC. Open a dedicated business bank account, pay yourself through documented draws or payroll, and keep records. You should also carry:

- Professional liability (malpractice) insurance
- General liability insurance
- Workers' compensation if you have employees
- Cyber liability coverage, given the patient data you handle

## How a Physical Therapist LLC Is Taxed

By default, a single-member LLC is taxed as a "disregarded entity" and a multi-member LLC as a partnership. Profits pass through to your personal return. You'll pay income tax plus self-employment tax (15.3% on net earnings up to the Social Security wage base, and 2.9% Medicare above that, plus an additional Medicare tax at higher incomes). The IRS explains the rules on its [LLC classification page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc).

### When an S Corp Election Might Make Sense

Physical therapists earn solid incomes. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports median pay for the profession at roughly $100,000 per year ([BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/physical-therapists.htm)), and practice owners can earn more. At that level, an S corp election can sometimes reduce self-employment tax. Under an S corp, you pay yourself a "reasonable salary" subject to payroll tax and take remaining profits as distributions that aren't subject to self-employment tax.

An illustrative example, not a tax projection: say your clinic nets $150,000 in a year. As a default pass-through, the whole amount is generally subject to self-employment tax. With an S corp election and a $90,000 reasonable salary, only the salary carries payroll tax, and the remaining $60,000 comes out as a distribution. The savings can be several thousand dollars, but you'll also pay for payroll processing, a separate business tax return, and possibly higher state fees. The IRS scrutinizes unreasonably low salaries, so don't guess.

For the deeper math, read [LLC vs S-Corp: which is better for taxes](/blog/llc-vs-s-corp-which-is-better-for-taxes/) and our guide to [reasonable salary for S corp owners](/blog/reasonable-salary-for-s-corp-llc-owners/). The IRS also publishes guidance on [S corporations](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/s-corporations). Tax rules differ by state (some states impose franchise taxes, gross receipts taxes, or minimum fees on LLCs and S corps), so run your numbers with a CPA before electing.

Many therapists also benefit from the qualified business income (QBI) deduction on pass-through income. Eligibility and limits depend on your income and the rules in effect, which is another reason to get a tax professional's input.

## How to Set Up Your LLC Step by Step

Here's the efficient order I recommend for a physical therapy private practice:

1. **Confirm your entity type.** Check your state's practice act and Secretary of State rules (LLC, PLLC, or PC).
2. **Pick a compliant name.** Include any required designator ("PLLC") and check availability. Some boards also restrict the use of certain words.
3. **Choose a registered agent.** You need a physical address in the state for legal notices. Our [registered agent guide](/blog/what-is-a-registered-agent-and-do-i-need-one/) explains your options.
4. **File your Articles of Organization** with the state, along with any required professional certificate or licensing board approval.
5. **Draft an operating agreement.** This matters especially for multi-owner clinics, where it should address buy-outs, disability, death of a member, and patient records on departure. See our [operating agreement template and guide](/blog/llc-operating-agreement-template-and-guide/).
6. **Get an EIN** from the IRS, free of charge. Our [free EIN walkthrough](/blog/how-to-get-an-ein-for-your-llc-free-irs-method/) shows how.
7. **Open a business bank account** and keep personal funds out of it.
8. **Update credentials.** Get your NPI, payer enrollments, and malpractice policy tied to the new entity, and update your state license records if required.
9. **Track ongoing compliance.** Annual reports, franchise taxes, and local business licenses vary by state.

On beneficial ownership reporting: as of 2025, FinCEN issued an interim final rule exempting U.S.-formed companies from Corporate Transparency Act BOI reporting. Rules have shifted several times, so check [FinCEN's BOI page](https://www.fincen.gov/boi) for the current status.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid in 2026

- **Forming the wrong entity.** Filing a plain LLC in a state that requires a PLLC or PC can invalidate your liability protection or trigger a board complaint.
- **Ignoring ownership restrictions.** Adding an unlicensed partner to a PLLC can violate state law.
- **Skipping the operating agreement.** Single-member owners often do, then regret it during a lender review or dispute.
- **Not updating payer and credentialing records.** Billing under the wrong entity name or tax ID causes payment delays.
- **Forgetting annual filings.** Missing a state annual report can lead to penalties or administrative dissolution.
- **Over-relying on the LLC.** As noted, it's not a replacement for malpractice coverage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a physical therapist form an LLC?

In many states, yes, though some require a professional LLC (PLLC) or professional corporation instead of a standard LLC. Check your state's practice act and Secretary of State rules before filing.

### What is the difference between an LLC and a PLLC for physical therapists?

A PLLC is a professional version of an LLC designed for licensed professions. It typically limits ownership to licensed members and requires a specific name designator. A standard LLC usually has no ownership license requirement, but not every state allows it for health professionals.

### Does an LLC protect me from malpractice lawsuits?

Not from your own negligence. An LLC or PLLC may protect you from business debts and from other providers' mistakes, but you remain personally responsible for your own professional conduct. Carry malpractice insurance.

### How much does it cost to start an LLC for a physical therapy practice?

State filing fees range from roughly $35 to $500, plus any annual report or franchise tax. Formation services may add fees for registered agent service, expedited filing, or compliance tools. Budget extra for a professional certificate if your state requires one.

### Should a physical therapist elect S corp status?

It can reduce self-employment tax once profits are high enough to support a reasonable salary plus distributions, but it adds payroll and tax-filing costs. Run the numbers with a CPA first.

### Do I need an LLC for a mobile or cash-based PT practice?

Even a small or mobile practice carries lease, contract, and equipment risk, so an entity is usually worthwhile. The structure still has to comply with your state's professional rules.

### Can I have a non-PT partner or investor in my practice?

Often not in a PLLC, and some states restrict this even for standard LLCs in healthcare. Verify ownership rules with your state board or an attorney before bringing in outside owners.

### Do I need a registered agent for my LLC?

Yes, every state requires one. You can serve as your own if you have a physical in-state address and are reliably available, or hire a commercial service. See our [registered agent guide](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/).
