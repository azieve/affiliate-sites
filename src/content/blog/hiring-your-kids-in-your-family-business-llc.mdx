Hiring your kids in your family business LLC is one of the oldest, most legitimate tax strategies available to small business owners. It is also one of the most commonly botched. Done properly, you shift income from your tax bracket to a child's near-zero bracket. Done carelessly, you hand an auditor a ready-made adjustment.

This guide covers who qualifies, how much you can pay, which entity structures keep the best tax benefits, and the paperwork that makes the arrangement defensible in 2026. ## How Hiring Your Kids in Your Family Business LLC Actually Works

The idea is simple. Your business pays your child a reasonable wage for real work. The business deducts that wage. Your child, who usually has little other income, pays little or no federal income tax on it.

Your business income is taxed at your marginal rate, which might be 22% or 24% federally, plus self-employment tax. Your child's first dollars of earned income are effectively taxed at 0% because of the standard deduction. For 2026, the standard deduction for a single filer is $16,100. A dependent child's standard deduction on earned income can reach that same amount. Unlike a dependent's investment income, earned wages are not subject to the "kiddie tax."

Here is a worked example. Say you own a single-member LLC that nets $120,000 and you pay your 14-year-old $12,000 a year to handle social media, filing, and cleaning the office.

- **Income tax savings:** $12,000 × 24% bracket ≈ $2,880
- **Self-employment tax savings:** $12,000 × 92.35% × 15.3% ≈ $1,700
- **Child's federal income tax:** $0 (the wages fall under the standard deduction)
- **Approximate combined savings:** about $4,500 per year, before state taxes and the qualified business income deduction, which can shrink the benefit somewhat

Those numbers are illustrative, not a promise. Your actual result depends on your bracket, state, and how your LLC is taxed. If you want to see how your entity choice drives these results, our guide on [how LLCs are taxed](/blog/llc-taxes-how-are-llcs-taxed/) explains the baseline.

## The Child Employee Tax Exemption: FICA and FUTA Rules

The biggest advantage, beyond income tax, is the **child employee tax exemption** from payroll taxes. According to the IRS, wages paid to your child under age 18 are not subject to Social Security and Medicare (FICA) taxes when the business is a sole proprietorship or a partnership owned only by the child's parents. Wages paid to a child under age 21 are also exempt from federal unemployment tax (FUTA) in that situation. You can confirm the details on the IRS [family employees page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/family-employees) and in [IRS Publication 15](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15).

Notice that the exemption turns on how the business is taxed, not on the fact that it is an LLC. That is where LLC owners get tripped up.

### Which LLC structures keep the exemption?

| LLC tax classification | Child under 18 FICA exemption | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Single-member LLC (disregarded, parent is owner) | Often claimed, but less clear-cut | Federal employment tax rules treat a disregarded LLC as a separate entity for payroll. Confirm your position with a CPA before relying on it. |
| Husband-and-wife LLC taxed as a partnership | Generally yes | Applies when the only partners are the child's parents. |
| LLC taxed as an S corporation | No | Wages are subject to FICA, so the payroll tax savings disappear. |
| LLC taxed as a C corporation | No | Same result as an S corporation. |
| LLC with a non-parent partner | No | The exemption requires that each partner is a parent of the child. |

I want to be direct about the single-member row, because it is where I see the most overconfident advice online. Plenty of blog posts state flatly that a single-member LLC always qualifies. The IRS has historically treated disregarded entities as separate for employment tax purposes, so the safer practice is to get written sign-off from a tax professional before you skip FICA withholding. In my experience, the owners who get into trouble are the ones who copied a template from a forum and never asked.

If you are weighing an S corporation election for other reasons, understand the tradeoff. An S corp can reduce your own self-employment tax, but it ends the child payroll tax exemption. Our [LLC vs S-Corp tax comparison](/blog/llc-vs-s-corp-which-is-better-for-taxes/) walks through when that makes sense, and our [self-employment tax guide](/blog/self-employment-tax-for-llc-owners/) shows the numbers.

## What the IRS Expects: Reasonable Pay for Real Work

Every dollar you pay a child must satisfy the standard "ordinary and necessary" business expense test. Practically, that means three things.

**1. The work must be real and age-appropriate.**
A 9-year-old can plausibly model for your product photos, shred documents, or help with packaging. A 9-year-old "managing your finances" will draw questions. A teenager running your Instagram account, updating inventory spreadsheets, or doing light bookkeeping is easy to defend.

**2. The pay must be reasonable.**
Pay what you would pay an unrelated person for the same task. If you would pay a stranger $15 an hour for filing and cleaning, paying your child $75 an hour is a red flag. Overpaying converts a deduction into a gift, and the IRS can disallow the excess.

**3. The pay must actually be paid.**
Transfer wages to an account in your child's name by check or direct deposit. Paying yourself back through a shared account, or not paying at all and "accruing" wages, undermines the deduction.

Document everything. Keep a simple timesheet, a short job description, and a note of the tasks performed each week. This paperwork is your defense in an audit, and it costs almost nothing.

## Family Business Payroll: Setting It Up Correctly

Proper **family business payroll** is what separates a defensible arrangement from a questionable one. Treat your child like any other employee.

1. **Get the right entity in place.** Make sure your LLC is formed, has an EIN, and has a business bank account. If you are still choosing between providers, [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) is our top pick for most small business owners. [LegalZoom](/go/legalzoom/) is a solid alternative if you want a more established brand. Compare them side by side in our [ZenBusiness vs LegalZoom breakdown](/blog/zenbusiness-vs-legalzoom/).
2. **Complete a Form W-4 and Form I-9.** Your child fills out both, just like any hire. The W-4 can claim exempt from federal withholding only if your child had no tax liability last year and expects none this year.
3. **Run payroll on a schedule.** Use a payroll service or software and pay on a regular cycle, not in one lump sum in December.
4. **File required forms.** You will report the wages on Form W-2 and the usual payroll filings. Whether you file Form 941 depends on whether you have withholding to report. A CPA can tell you which applies.
5. **Check state requirements.** Many states require new-hire reporting, workers' compensation coverage, and sometimes unemployment insurance, regardless of the federal exemptions. Our guide on [whether an LLC can have employees](/blog/can-an-llc-have-employees/) covers the general obligations.
6. **Respect child labor rules.** The federal Fair Labor Standards Act generally exempts children working for a parent-owned business in non-hazardous jobs, but hazardous work and state rules still apply. The U.S. Department of Labor's [YouthRules site](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/youthrules) is the best starting point, and your state labor department may be stricter.

## Pros, Cons, and Side Effects Most Articles Skip

### The upside beyond income tax

- **Retirement head start.** A child with earned income can contribute to a Roth IRA, up to the lesser of their earnings or the annual limit ($7,500 for 2026). Parents often fund this from the wages or as a matching gift. Compounding over decades is the real prize, not the one-year deduction.
- **Financial education.** A teenager who gets a real paycheck, a W-2, and a tax return learns more than any classroom lesson. I've watched this change how kids think about money, which is a benefit no calculator captures.

### The downsides

- **Dependency and filing.** Your child can usually remain your dependent, but they may need to file their own return, especially to recover any withheld tax.
- **Financial aid.** A student's earned income can affect need-based aid formulas. If college aid is on the horizon, ask a financial aid advisor before paying large amounts.
- **State taxes.** A few states tax small incomes differently than federal law does.
- **Audit exposure.** Paying children is a known audit focus. Documentation matters far more than the dollar amount.
- **Cost of compliance.** If you need a payroll service, factor in the fees. For a very small wage, the administrative cost can eat part of the savings.

### Common mistakes to avoid

- Paying a young child far above market rates
- Paying in cash with no records
- Hiring a child for "work" that never happens
- Electing S-corp status without recognizing it ends the FICA exemption
- Skipping state workers' compensation or payroll registration
- Paying wages after the fact, at year-end, to "catch up"

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I pay my child from my LLC?

Yes, you can pay children from an LLC as long as they perform real work, the pay is reasonable for that work, and you handle payroll properly. How the LLC is taxed determines whether payroll tax exemptions apply.

### How old does my child have to be to work for my family business LLC?

Federal law has no single minimum age for work in a parent-owned business in non-hazardous jobs, but the IRS expects age-appropriate tasks. Most advisors consider ages 7 and up defensible when the work is simple and documented. State child labor laws may set stricter limits.

### How much can I pay my child without paying income tax?

In 2026, a single filer's standard deduction is $16,100. A dependent child's earned income up to that amount is generally not subject to federal income tax. The deduction for dependents has its own rules, so confirm your child's situation with a tax professional.

### Do I have to withhold Social Security and Medicare from my child's pay?

Not always. Wages paid to a child under 18 are exempt from FICA when the business is a sole proprietorship or a partnership owned only by the child's parents. If your LLC is taxed as an S corporation or C corporation, FICA applies. Single-member LLCs deserve a CPA's review.

### Do I need a payroll service to hire my child?

You are not legally required to use one, but it reduces errors in W-2 reporting, tax filings, and recordkeeping. For one part-time family employee, the cost is usually modest compared with the audit risk of doing it wrong.

### Can hiring my kids trigger an audit?

It can draw attention if the wages look inflated or the work looks fictional. Reasonable pay, real duties, and thorough records make the arrangement far easier to defend.

### Can my child still be my dependent if they earn wages?

Usually yes. Earning wages does not automatically end dependent status, though the support test and filing requirements still apply. A tax preparer can confirm this for your specific facts.

### Can I hire my child if my LLC is an S corporation?

Yes, but the payroll tax exemption is lost, so the wages are subject to FICA. The income tax benefit may still be worthwhile, but the savings shrink.
