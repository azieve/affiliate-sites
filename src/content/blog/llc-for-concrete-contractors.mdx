Concrete is unforgiving. Once a slab is poured, finished, and cured, your mistakes are literally set in stone, and so is your liability. A driveway that heaves after one freeze-thaw season, a foundation that cracks under a new home, or a patio that pitches water toward the house instead of away from it can each produce a claim that dwarfs the original contract. An LLC for concrete contractors is the most common way to keep those business risks from following you home to your house, your savings, and your family's assets.

Setting one up is easier than most finishers expect. A formation service like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) will file your Articles of Organization for $0 plus your state's filing fee on its entry-level plan, and you can usually be fully formed within a few business days to two weeks, depending on your state. The harder questions are the ones the formation sites gloss over: what an LLC actually protects in a trade where you personally hold the bull float, how it interacts with your contractor license and bond, and how to structure taxes so a good season doesn't hand the IRS more than it should.

This guide answers those questions for 2026, written for owner-operators running a flatwork crew, decorative and stamped concrete shops, and foundation subs looking to grow.

## Why Concrete Contractors Need an LLC in 2026

A concrete contractor LLC creates a separate legal entity that owns your business contracts, equipment, debts, and obligations. If the business gets sued or can't pay a supplier, creditors are generally limited to the assets inside the LLC rather than your personal property. For a sole proprietor, there is no such wall: the business *is* you, so a judgment against the business is a judgment against you.

Concrete work carries risks that make this separation more valuable than in most trades:

- **Long-tail defect claims.** Cracking, scaling, spalling, and settlement often show up months or years after the job. Many states have statutes of repose for construction defects that run anywhere from about 4 to 10 years or more after substantial completion, so a pour you did in 2026 can surface as a lawsuit long after you've cashed the check.
- **Property damage during the pour.** Washout on a neighbor's landscaping, a pump truck cracking a customer's existing driveway, or overspray from a sealer on a new car can each turn into a claim.
- **Subcontractor and supplier disputes.** Ready-mix suppliers, rebar fabricators, and pump operators can file mechanics' liens or sue for unpaid invoices if a general contractor stiffs you on a draw.
- **Workplace safety exposure.** Concrete cutting, grinding, and drilling create respirable crystalline silica dust, which is regulated under [OSHA's construction silica standard](https://www.osha.gov/silica-crystalline/construction). Citations and injury claims are a real cost of doing business.

In my experience, the concrete contractors who get hurt worst aren't the ones who did bad work. They're the ones who did decent work, got pulled into a dispute between a homeowner and a GC, and discovered that everything they owned was on the table because they never separated the business from themselves.

### What an LLC Does Not Protect

An LLC is not a force field, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. It generally does **not** protect you from:

- **Your own negligence.** If you personally finished a slab badly or personally caused an injury, a plaintiff may sue you individually as well as the LLC.
- **Personal guarantees.** Most equipment lenders and many ready-mix suppliers will ask the owner to personally guarantee credit for a new business. Sign that, and the LLC shield doesn't cover that debt.
- **Veil piercing.** If you mix personal and business money, skip basic formalities, or leave the company badly undercapitalized, a court may disregard the LLC entirely.
- **Unpaid payroll taxes.** The IRS can pursue "responsible persons" personally for trust fund taxes withheld from employee paychecks and not remitted.

That is why an LLC works best as one layer in a system that also includes insurance, contracts, and clean bookkeeping. We'll cover each below.

## How to Set Up an LLC for Concrete Contractors (Step by Step)

Forming a concrete contractor LLC follows the same core process in every state, though fees and timelines vary. If you want a deeper primer on the entity itself, start with our explainer on [what an LLC is](/learn/what-is-an-llc/).

### Step 1: Choose and Check Your Business Name

Your LLC name must be distinguishable from other registered entities in your state and must include a designator such as "LLC" or "Limited Liability Company." Search your Secretary of State's business database first. Then check that the matching domain name and social handles are available, since "Smith Concrete LLC" is a lot less useful if someone else owns smithconcrete.com.

If your contractor license board requires the licensed name to match the entity name exactly, decide on the final name *before* you apply for or transfer your license. Changing it later can mean refiling with both agencies.

### Step 2: Appoint a Registered Agent

Every LLC needs a registered agent with a physical address in the state of formation who can accept legal papers during business hours. Concrete contractors are rarely sitting at a desk at 10 a.m. on a Tuesday; you're on a job site watching a slab set. Missing a served lawsuit because nobody was at your listed address can lead to a default judgment.

Most formation services include a registered agent for the first year on mid-tier plans, then charge roughly $99 to $199 per year afterward. If keeping your home address off public records matters to you, [Northwest Registered Agent](/go/northwest/) is a strong privacy-focused option. Our guide to [what a registered agent is](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/) explains the role in more detail.

### Step 3: File Articles of Organization

This is the official filing that creates the LLC. State fees in 2026 range from around $35 to $50 in the cheapest states to roughly $500 in Massachusetts. A few common examples:

You can file directly with the state yourself or use a service. A service doesn't change the state fee; it saves you time, catches errors, and bundles add-ons like the registered agent, EIN, and operating agreement.

### Step 4: Draft an Operating Agreement

Even a single-member concrete contractor LLC should have a written operating agreement. It documents who owns the company, how decisions are made, and what happens if an owner dies, becomes disabled, or wants out. If you're bringing in a partner, maybe a finisher who handles the crew while you handle bids, this document matters even more: it should spell out capital contributions (who paid for the skid steer and the power trowels), profit splits, and buyout terms. Our [LLC operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/) covers what to include.

### Step 5: Get an EIN

An Employer Identification Number from the IRS is free and takes minutes online. You'll need it to open a business bank account, run payroll for your crew, and file business tax returns. The IRS explains how single-member LLCs are treated in its [guidance on single member limited liability companies](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/single-member-limited-liability-companies). Beware of third-party sites that charge $200 or more for a free EIN.

### Step 6: Open a Business Bank Account and Separate Everything

Open a dedicated business checking account in the LLC's name and run every deposit, supplier payment, and fuel purchase through it. This single habit does more to protect your liability shield than almost anything else. Paying for your family's groceries out of the same account you pay your ready-mix supplier from is exactly the kind of commingling that invites veil-piercing arguments.

## Concrete Contractor License, Bonding, and Permits

Licensing rules for concrete work vary widely by state and sometimes by city or county. The core point for your LLC: **in many states, the license must be held by or associated with the entity that signs the contract.** If you form an LLC but your license is still in your personal name, you may not be legally authorized to contract in the LLC's name until you update it.

A few examples of how this plays out in 2026:

- **California** requires a state license for jobs where labor and materials total $500 or more. Concrete work typically falls under the C-8 Concrete specialty classification through the [Contractors State License Board](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/). LLC licensees must carry a $25,000 contractor bond and an additional LLC employee/worker bond, plus liability insurance that meets CSLB minimums for LLCs.
- **Florida** licenses many structural and general contractors at the state level, but specialty concrete work is often licensed locally by county or city.
- **Texas** has no statewide general contractor or concrete license, but cities such as Houston, Dallas, and Austin require permits and sometimes local registration for flatwork in the right-of-way, such as sidewalks, curb cuts, and driveway approaches.

Many municipalities also require a separate **right-of-way or sidewalk contractor registration**, often with a dedicated bond, before you can pour anything touching a public sidewalk or street.

**Bonds are not insurance.** A contractor license bond protects your customers and the state, not you. If the surety pays a claim, it will come back to you (and often to you personally, through an indemnity agreement you signed) for reimbursement. That indemnity is another example of a personal obligation the LLC won't shield.

Check your state's licensing board and your local building department before you bid your first job under the new entity.

## Concrete Business Insurance: The Coverage That Actually Protects You

If the LLC is the wall between your business and your personal assets, concrete business insurance is the first line of defense that pays for claims before they ever reach that wall. Here's what most concrete contractors carry:

- **General liability.** Covers third-party bodily injury and property damage. Many GCs and commercial owners require $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate. For small residential flatwork contractors, premiums often fall roughly in the $2,000 to $8,000 per year range, though concrete's defect exposure can push that higher.
- **Completed operations coverage.** Make sure your GL policy covers claims that arise *after* the job is done. For concrete, that's where most of the risk lives.
- **Workers' compensation.** Required in nearly every state once you have employees (thresholds vary). Concrete class codes carry some of the higher rates in construction because of back injuries, silica exposure, and heavy material handling.
- **Commercial auto.** Your personal auto policy likely excludes a truck hauling a trailer with a mixer or skid steer for business. You need commercial coverage.
- **Inland marine / equipment coverage.** Protects tools and equipment like power trowels, concrete saws, screeds, and mixers against theft and damage on job sites.
- **Umbrella or excess liability.** Adds a layer above your GL and auto limits, often $1 million to $5 million, and is increasingly required on commercial work.

One gotcha I see constantly: concrete contractors buy a GL policy with a broad "subsidence" or "earth movement" exclusion and assume they're covered for settlement claims. Read the exclusions. If a foundation settles and the policy excludes earth movement, you may be defending that claim on your own, and that is exactly the scenario where the LLC structure matters most.

## Taxes for a Concrete Contractor LLC

By default, the IRS treats a single-member LLC as a "disregarded entity." Profits pass through to your personal return on Schedule C, and you pay income tax plus self-employment tax of 15.3% on net earnings (up to the Social Security wage base, then 2.9% Medicare above it). A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default.

### The S-Corp Election

Once your concrete business consistently nets around $60,000 to $80,000 or more after expenses, an S-corp election may reduce your self-employment tax. You pay yourself a reasonable salary (subject to payroll taxes) and take the remaining profit as distributions, which aren't subject to self-employment tax.

Here's a simplified example. Say your concrete contractor LLC nets $150,000. As a default LLC, you'd owe roughly $21,000 in self-employment tax. With an S-corp election and a $75,000 reasonable salary, payroll taxes on the salary run about $11,500, potentially saving you around $9,000 to $10,000 before added costs like payroll processing and a separate business return, which might run $1,500 to $3,000 a year.

The IRS scrutinizes "reasonable compensation," so don't pay yourself $20,000 to dodge taxes on a $150,000 business. Our comparison of [LLC vs S-Corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) explains the trade-offs, and you should run your specific numbers with a CPA.

### Equipment Deductions

Concrete is equipment-heavy. Trucks, trailers, skid steers, mixers, saws, and forms can often be deducted quickly through Section 179 expensing or bonus depreciation rather than over many years. The exact limits and percentages depend on current tax law, which has shifted several times in recent years, so confirm the 2026 figures with a tax professional before a big purchase. Just as important: buy equipment in the LLC's name and pay for it from the LLC's account so the deduction, and the asset protection, line up.

### Other Common Deductions

- Fuel, maintenance, and insurance for business vehicles
- Forming materials, rebar, wire mesh, sealers, and admixtures
- Licensing, bonding, and permit fees
- Safety gear, respirators, and silica-control equipment
- Formation costs (up to $5,000 can generally be deducted in the first year, subject to limits)

*Tax rules are complex and change frequently. This section is general information, not tax advice; consult a qualified CPA or tax advisor about your situation.*

## Common Mistakes Concrete Contractors Make With Their LLC

After reviewing dozens of small contractor setups, these are the mistakes I see most often:

1. **Signing contracts in their own name.** Every bid, proposal, and contract should be in the LLC's name, signed by you as "Member" or "Manager." Signing "John Smith" instead of "Smith Concrete LLC, by John Smith, Member" can make you personally liable.
2. **Forgetting to update the license.** Forming the LLC but leaving the license in your personal name can create a gap where the LLC is technically unlicensed.
3. **Commingling funds.** Running personal expenses through the business account is the fastest way to weaken your liability protection.
4. **Skipping annual reports.** Miss your state's annual report and the LLC can be administratively dissolved, which can leave you operating as an unprotected sole proprietor without realizing it.
5. **Underinsuring.** An LLC is not a substitute for adequate general liability and workers' comp coverage.
6. **Using weak contracts.** Your contract should address cracking expectations (concrete cracks; your contract should say so), drainage, site prep responsibilities, change orders, and payment terms.

One more 2026 note on compliance: following FinCEN's March 2025 interim rule, U.S.-formed LLCs and their U.S. owners are currently exempt from federal Beneficial Ownership Information reporting. Rules can change, so check our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) for the latest status.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do concrete contractors need an LLC?

No state requires concrete contractors to form an LLC; you can legally operate as a sole proprietor. But given the long-tail defect risk, property damage exposure, and supplier lien risk in concrete work, an LLC is a low-cost way to separate business liabilities from your personal assets. Most established concrete contractors operate through an LLC or corporation.

### How much does it cost to form an LLC for a concrete business?

Expect to pay your state's filing fee (roughly $35 to $500 depending on the state), plus any formation service fee. With [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) or [LegalZoom](/go/legalzoom/), basic formation starts at $0 plus state fees. Ongoing costs include annual reports, registered agent fees if you don't serve yourself, and in some states, franchise taxes like California's $800 minimum. Our [LLC cost guide](/learn/how-much-does-an-llc-cost/) has a full breakdown.

### Does an LLC protect me if a slab I poured cracks?

Partly. If the LLC is sued for a defective slab, your personal assets are generally protected from the LLC's liabilities. But if you personally performed the work negligently, a plaintiff may also sue you individually. That's why completed operations coverage under a general liability policy is essential alongside the LLC.

### Do I need a new contractor license if I form an LLC?

Often, yes, or at least an update. Many states issue licenses to a specific entity, so a license held in your individual name may not automatically transfer to your LLC. Check with your state licensing board before you start contracting under the LLC's name.

### Should my concrete LLC elect S-corp status?

It depends on profit. Once your concrete contractor LLC consistently nets roughly $60,000 to $80,000 or more, an S-corp election may reduce self-employment taxes. Below that, the extra payroll and accounting costs often outweigh the savings. Talk to a CPA before electing.

### Can I put my equipment in the LLC?

Yes, and you generally should. Buying or transferring trucks, mixers, and tools into the LLC aligns depreciation deductions with the business and keeps the assets on the business side of the ledger. If you transfer equipment you already own personally, document the contribution in writing and talk to a tax advisor about how to handle it.

### What insurance does a concrete contractor LLC need?

At minimum, most concrete contractors carry general liability (with completed operations), workers' compensation if they have employees, commercial auto, and equipment coverage. Many commercial jobs also require umbrella coverage and specific limits written into the contract.

### Can I form an LLC in Delaware or Wyoming instead of my home state?

You can, but it rarely makes sense for a local concrete contractor. If you do business in your home state, you'll still need to register there as a foreign LLC and pay that state's fees, essentially doubling your costs. For most contractors, forming in the state where you pour concrete is the simpler, cheaper choice.
