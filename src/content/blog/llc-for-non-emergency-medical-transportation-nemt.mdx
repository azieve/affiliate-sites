Driving a patient to dialysis three times a week is steady, meaningful work. It also puts you in a vehicle with someone who may be frail, in a wheelchair, or unsteady on their feet. That combination of vulnerable passengers, a commercial vehicle, and public or private payers is why an LLC for non-emergency medical transportation (NEMT) is one of the first decisions to make, before your first ride.

Forming the entity is the easy part. A service like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) starts at $0 plus your state's filing fee, and most states process filings within days. The harder part is understanding what an LLC does and doesn't protect in this industry, and how to build the insurance, contracts, and tax structure around it. ## Why an LLC for Non-Emergency Medical Transportation (NEMT) Makes Sense

NEMT companies move people who can't drive themselves, or can't safely use ordinary transit, to medical appointments. Typical riders include dialysis patients, people recovering from surgery, seniors using wheelchairs or stretchers, and Medicaid members with no other way to get to care. The service is non-emergency, but your exposure resembles a small ambulance-adjacent business: bodily-injury risk, strict regulatory oversight, and payers who audit.

An LLC separates your personal assets from the business's debts and most lawsuits. If a vendor sues over an unpaid invoice, or a lease on a wheelchair van goes into default, creditors generally reach the company's assets and not your home or personal savings. For a deeper foundation, see our [What Is an LLC?](/learn/what-is-an-llc/) explainer.

Here is the most important caution, and I'll be blunt about it: **an LLC does not protect you from your own negligence.** If you personally drive and cause an accident, you can still be named in the lawsuit. In my experience, new NEMT owners often assume the LLC is a magic shield and underinsure as a result. The LLC protects you from the *business's* liabilities. Insurance protects you from the claims that actually happen on the road.

An LLC also does practical work:

- **Credibility with payers.** Medicaid brokers, hospitals, senior-living facilities, and managed-care plans want to contract with a formal legal entity, not an individual.
- **Clean separation of finances.** NEMT has high vehicle costs, fuel, and insurance. A separate business bank account makes bookkeeping and audits far simpler.
- **Flexibility on taxes.** You can start as a default pass-through entity and later elect S-corp status as profits grow.

## How to Start an NEMT Company: Setup Steps in the Right Order

Order matters. I've seen owners buy a van, wrap it with branding, and only then discover their state requires a specific license or vehicle inspection before they can transport a single paid passenger. If you want to start an NEMT company efficiently, work in this sequence.

**1. Research your state's rules first.** NEMT regulation varies widely. Some states require a state-level license or permit, some leave it to counties or cities, and some require specific vehicle inspections or driver certifications. Start with your state's Medicaid agency and transportation or public utilities commission.

**2. Choose a business name and check availability.** Search your state's business registry, then confirm the domain and social handles. Avoid words like "ambulance" or "emergency" in your name; many states restrict them and they signal the wrong service type.

**3. File your Articles of Organization.** This creates the LLC. Filing fees range from about $35 (Kentucky) to $500 (Massachusetts), with many states between $50 and $200. See our [How Much Does an LLC Cost?](/learn/how-much-does-an-llc-cost/) breakdown for the state-by-state picture, or use a state guide such as [Texas](/start-llc/texas/), [Florida](/start-llc/florida/), or [California](/start-llc/california/).

**4. Appoint a registered agent.** Every LLC needs one. Because NEMT owners are on the road all day, missing a service-of-process notice at a fixed office address is a real risk. Read [What Is a Registered Agent?](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/) if you're unsure.

**5. Get your EIN.** It's free directly from the IRS, which we cover in our [free EIN guide](/blog/how-to-get-an-ein-for-your-llc-free-irs-method/). You'll need it for payroll, bank accounts, and payer enrollment.

**6. Open a business bank account.** Keep every dollar of revenue and expense out of your personal accounts. Our [LLC bank account guide](/blog/llc-bank-account-how-to-open-one/) walks through what banks ask for.

**7. Write an operating agreement.** Even single-member LLCs should have one. It documents that the business is a separate entity, which strengthens your liability protection. See the [LLC operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/).

**8. Secure insurance, vehicles, and licenses, then enroll with payers.** This is the long pole, and the next sections explain why.

## NEMT Insurance Requirements: Where Your Real Protection Lives

If the LLC is your legal shield, insurance is your armor. NEMT insurance requirements are stricter and costlier than ordinary business coverage, and they are frequently the largest line item in a new operator's budget.

Expect to carry or evaluate:

- **Commercial auto liability.** Personal auto policies typically exclude paid passenger transport. NEMT operators often face premiums in the thousands of dollars per vehicle per year, and wheelchair-accessible or stretcher vehicles can cost more. Quotes vary heavily by state, driving records, and claims history.
- **General liability.** Covers incidents not involving the vehicle itself, such as a passenger injured while being assisted at a doorway, depending on policy language.
- **Professional liability / errors and omissions.** Some payers require it.
- **Workers' compensation.** Required in most states once you have employees. Misclassifying drivers as contractors to avoid it is a common and expensive mistake.
- **Hired and non-owned auto.** Relevant if drivers use their own vehicles for any part of the work.
- **Cyber or privacy coverage.** Useful because you'll handle patient names, addresses, and appointment details.

Payers and states often set minimum limits, and some require you to name them as additional insured. Don't guess. Get the contract's insurance clause in writing before you bind a policy, and work with a broker who specifically writes NEMT or passenger-transport business. For general guidance on liability limits, speak with a licensed insurance professional.

## Licensing, Medicaid Enrollment, and Compliance Gotchas

Medicaid is the largest NEMT payer in many markets. Federal rules require state Medicaid programs to ensure transportation to and from covered medical appointments, and the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services explains the program on [Medicaid.gov's NEMT page](https://www.medicaid.gov/medicaid/benefits/non-emergency-medical-transportation/index.html). Most states deliver the benefit through regional brokers, who contract with local transportation providers like you.

That means a typical onboarding path looks like this:

1. Form the LLC and obtain an EIN.
2. Meet vehicle, insurance, and driver standards set by the state or broker.
3. Apply to become a contracted provider with the broker or managed-care plan.
4. Complete credentialing, which often includes background checks, driving-record reviews, and vehicle inspections.

Common requirements include:

- **Driver background checks and motor-vehicle record reviews.**
- **Driver training**, often CPR and first aid, plus passenger assistance training such as the PASS program, depending on the payer.
- **Drug testing policies**, which some brokers require.
- **HIPAA awareness.** Even where you aren't a covered entity yourself, you handle protected details. Train staff and keep trip logs secure. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services provides guidance at [HHS.gov](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/index.html).
- **Vehicle standards**, such as inspection stickers, wheelchair securement systems, and age or mileage limits.
- **Trip documentation.** Brokers audit trip logs, GPS data, and signatures. Missing records can lead to denied payments, so organize this from day one.

One more gotcha: payment timing. Broker reimbursements can lag 30 to 60 days or more, and denied claims can stretch that further. Many NEMT startups fail not from lack of riders but from lack of working capital to cover fuel, wages, and insurance while waiting to be paid. Plan for several months of operating cash, not weeks.

## Taxes for an NEMT Business LLC

By default, a single-member LLC is taxed as a "disregarded entity": profits flow to your personal return. A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership. In either case, you generally owe self-employment tax of 15.3% on net earnings (up to the Social Security wage base), plus federal and state income tax. The IRS explains the classification rules on its [LLC page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc).

Key tax considerations for NEMT owners:

- **Vehicle deductions.** You can usually deduct either actual vehicle expenses (fuel, repairs, insurance, depreciation) or a standard mileage rate, but you generally must choose a method carefully, and some methods limit your options for the same vehicle later. Keep a mileage log.
- **Depreciation and Section 179.** Purchasing a qualifying work vehicle may allow accelerated deductions, subject to weight, use, and annual limits. Ask a CPA before buying.
- **Estimated quarterly payments.** There's no employer withholding on your own profit, so plan for quarterly estimated taxes. See our [quarterly tax payments guide](/blog/llc-quarterly-tax-payments-guide/).
- **S-corp election.** Once net profit is consistently strong, electing S-corp status may reduce self-employment tax, but it requires running payroll and paying yourself a reasonable salary. Compare in our [LLC vs S-Corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) guide.
- **Employees versus contractors.** Drivers who work set schedules, use your vehicles, and follow your protocols are often employees under IRS tests. Misclassification can bring back taxes and penalties.

Tax outcomes depend on your facts and state, so a CPA who knows transportation businesses is worth the fee.

## Mistakes I See Too Often

A few patterns repeat among new operators:

- **Skipping the operating agreement.** It's a few pages and costs little. Without it, state default rules govern your company, and courts may question whether you kept the business separate.
- **Mixing personal and business money.** This weakens the liability shield. Pay yourself through formal draws or payroll. Our [how to pay yourself from an LLC](/blog/how-to-pay-yourself-from-an-llc/) guide shows how.
- **Titling vehicles in your personal name.** Title and insure vehicles in the LLC's name where lenders and insurers permit it, and confirm how the policy names the insured.
- **Ignoring annual reports.** Many states require annual or biennial filings. Miss them and your LLC can fall out of good standing, which can disrupt payer contracts. Our guide on [what happens if you don't renew your LLC](/blog/what-happens-if-you-don-t-renew-your-llc/) spells out the consequences.
- **Underestimating startup cash.** Between insurance deposits, vehicle outfitting, and slow reimbursement, new operators often need far more than the formation fee suggests.

## Is an LLC Enough? When to Consider More

For most small NEMT operators, a single LLC with strong insurance is a sound starting point. As you grow into multiple vehicles, consider separating the entity that owns your vehicles from the one that operates the service, so a claim against operations has a harder path to your fleet. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need an LLC to start a non-emergency medical transportation business?

Legally, you may be able to operate as a sole proprietor, but it is rarely wise. An LLC separates business liabilities from your personal assets, and many payers and brokers prefer or require a formal business entity. It doesn't replace insurance.

### How much does it cost to form an LLC for NEMT?

Expect your state's filing fee, typically $35 to $500 depending on the state, plus optional formation-service fees. ZenBusiness offers a $0 base tier plus state fees; paid tiers add services. Total startup costs for a working NEMT company are much higher once vehicles and insurance are included.

### What insurance do I need for an NEMT company?

At minimum, plan on commercial auto liability, general liability, and workers' compensation if you have employees. Payers may also require professional liability and specific minimum limits. Check your contract and state rules before buying.

### Does an LLC protect me if a passenger is injured?

Only partly. The LLC generally protects personal assets from the business's debts and contract claims, but you can still be personally liable for your own negligence, such as an accident you cause while driving. Insurance is your main protection for injury claims.

### Can I be a sole proprietor and still contract with Medicaid brokers?

Some brokers accept sole proprietors, but many prefer entities and have specific credentialing rules. Check each broker's provider requirements in your state.

### Should my NEMT business LLC elect S-corp status?

Possibly, once profits are steady. An S-corp election can reduce self-employment tax but adds payroll and compliance costs. A CPA can model the break-even for your numbers.

### Which state should I form my NEMT LLC in?

Generally, the state where you operate. Forming elsewhere usually forces you to register as a foreign LLC at home anyway, adding cost without benefit. See our [best state to form an LLC](/blog/best-state-to-form-an-llc/) comparison for more.

### How long does it take to launch an NEMT business after forming the LLC?

Formation can take days, but credentialing, insurance, vehicle outfitting, and payer enrollment often take several weeks to a few months. Start those in parallel with your LLC filing.
