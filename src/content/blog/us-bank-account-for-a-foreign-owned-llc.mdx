## Why Banks Scrutinize Foreign-Owned LLCs

US banks must follow "Know Your Customer" rules under the Bank Secrecy Act. For a business account, that means identifying the company and the people who own or control it. When an owner lives abroad and has no Social Security number, the bank's compliance team has more to verify, and many large banks simply decline to take on that work.

In my experience, rejections rarely come from anything the founder did wrong. They come from a mismatch between the founder's situation and the bank's appetite. I've seen founders spend a month applying to three traditional banks in a row, when a single application to a bank built for remote owners would have been approved in days.

Banks typically want to see:

- A properly formed LLC that is active and in good standing
- An EIN issued by the IRS
- Ownership details for each member (and often each person with 25% or more)
- Proof of identity for the owner(s), usually a passport
- Proof of address, which can be a foreign address
- A clear description of what the business does and where its money comes from

The last item matters more than most people expect. A vague "consulting" description with no website or client list is a common reason for follow-up questions.

## Step 2: Get an EIN Without a Social Security Number

The EIN is the key that unlocks everything else. Banks require it, and you can't file US business tax forms without it.

The IRS online EIN application generally requires a US taxpayer ID for the responsible party, so non-residents usually can't use it. Instead, an EIN for a foreign owner is typically obtained by:

1. **Faxing or mailing Form SS-4** to the IRS, writing "Foreign" or "N/A" on the line for the SSN/ITIN
2. **Calling the IRS** international line (267-941-1099), where an agent can complete the application by phone

The IRS explains the process on its [EIN page for businesses](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/get-an-employer-identification-number). Processing by fax can take roughly one to two weeks; mail takes longer.

## Step 3: Pick a Bank That Serves Non-Resident Owners

This is where most of the time is saved or lost. Broadly, you have three options.

### Option A: Fintech Platforms Built for Remote Owners

Several US fintech companies accept foreign-owned LLCs and open accounts entirely online. These are typically not banks themselves. They partner with FDIC-member banks that hold the deposits. The FDIC explains how [deposit insurance works](https://www.fdic.gov/deposit/deposits/) and the limits of coverage, which is worth reading before you park significant funds.

| Provider type | Examples | Remote opening | Typical strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fintech business banking | Mercury, Relay | Often yes | Fast approval, modern dashboards |
| Multi-currency account | Wise Business | Yes | International transfers, local account details |
| Traditional bank | Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo | Usually in person | Branch access, cash handling |

Acceptance policies change often. Some fintechs have tightened requirements for certain countries, and others require a US address for at least one control person. Always verify current eligibility on the provider's own site before you apply.

### Option B: Multi-Currency Accounts for International Payments

If your customers or income are outside the US, a multi-currency account can reduce conversion costs. These accounts may not be full bank accounts in the legal sense, so check whether the provider supports the things you need, such as ACH, wires, and card payments through Stripe or PayPal.

### Option C: A Traditional Bank, In Person

Large banks can be a fit if you can visit the US. Some will open an account for a foreign-owned LLC during a single branch visit with your passport, formation documents, EIN letter, and operating agreement. The upside is stability and a physical relationship. The downside is minimum balances and monthly fees that fintechs often waive.

My honest take: for most non-resident founders, start with a fintech and add a traditional bank later if the business grows. Chasing a big-bank account first is the most common time sink I see.

## Step 4: Prepare Your Document Package

Assemble these before you apply, and save them as clean PDFs:

- **Articles of Organization** (certificate of formation) from the state
- **EIN confirmation letter** (IRS CP 575 or 147C)
- **Operating agreement** showing owners and percentages. See our [LLC operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/).
- **Passport** for each owner
- **Proof of address**, such as a utility bill or bank statement under three months old
- **Business description**, ideally with a website, LinkedIn page, or client contract

Names must match across every document. A one-letter difference between your passport and your operating agreement can stall an application. ## Step 5: Understand the Tax and Reporting Obligations

Opening the account is only half the job. A foreign-owned LLC has reporting duties that apply even if the company earns nothing.

### Form 5472 and the $25,000 Penalty

A single-member LLC with one foreign owner is generally treated as a "disregarded entity" for income tax purposes, but for reporting it is treated as a domestic corporation. The company must file Form 5472, attached to a pro forma Form 1120, to report certain transactions between the LLC and its foreign owner.

Per the IRS, the penalty for failing to file, or filing incorrectly, is $25,000 per form. You can read the agency's instructions on its [Form 5472 page](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-5472). Our [Form 5472 filing guide](/blog/form-5472-for-foreign-owned-single-member-llc/) breaks down the deadline and what counts as a reportable transaction.

Bank activity can create reportable transactions. Money you move from your personal foreign account into the LLC, or owner draws you take out, may count. Keeping clean records from day one makes the filing painless.

### Income Tax Treatment

Tax outcomes depend on whether your income is "effectively connected" with a US trade or business, your country of residence, and any tax treaty. A non-resident running a purely online consulting business from abroad may owe no US income tax in some cases, while others face US filing requirements. I'd rather be direct here: don't guess. A cross-border tax professional is worth the fee.

### Beneficial Ownership Reporting in 2026

Beneficial ownership information (BOI) reporting has changed significantly. In March 2025, FinCEN issued an interim final rule that removed the BOI reporting requirement for US-formed companies and their US-citizen owners, leaving it in place only for entities formed under foreign law that register to do business in the US. Rules in this area have shifted repeatedly, so confirm the current status on [FinCEN's BOI page](https://www.fincen.gov/boi) before assuming anything. We track updates in our [BOI report changes for 2026](/blog/boi-report-changes-and-updates-2026/).

Even where FinCEN doesn't require a filing, your bank will still ask you who owns the company. Those are two separate requirements.

## Common Reasons Applications Get Rejected

After watching a lot of applications succeed and fail, these are the patterns:

1. **Using a registered agent address as your business address.** Some banks reject this. A registered agent address is for legal notices, not operations.
2. **Inconsistent information.** Different spellings, owner percentages, or business descriptions across documents.
3. **Unclear source of funds.** Be ready to say how the account will be funded and from where.
4. **High-risk industries or countries.** Crypto, gambling, and certain jurisdictions face extra review or automatic denial.
5. **No online presence.** A basic website helps more than people expect.
6. **Applying to many banks at once.** Several hard declines in a row can make later approvals harder.

If one provider declines you, ask why, fix the gap, and try a different type of provider rather than reapplying immediately.

## Practical Tips for Day-to-Day Banking

- **Keep personal and business money separate.** Mixing funds can weaken your liability protection and complicates Form 5472.
- **Document owner contributions and draws.** Label wires clearly.
- **Watch international wire costs.** Incoming and outgoing wire fees add up. A multi-currency account may be cheaper if you transfer frequently.
- **Set up payment processors after the bank account.** Stripe and PayPal usually require a US bank account and EIN.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a non-US resident open a US bank account for an LLC?

Yes. Non-residents can open a business account for a US LLC, often remotely through a fintech provider. Some traditional banks require an in-person visit. Approval depends on your country, business type, and documentation.

### Do I need a Social Security number to open a business bank account?

Usually not. A business account relies on the LLC's EIN, and the bank verifies the owner with a passport. A business bank account without an SSN is common for foreign-owned LLCs, although each bank sets its own rules.

### Can I get an EIN as a foreign owner without an SSN or ITIN?

Yes. You can apply with Form SS-4 by fax, mail, or phone, noting that you don't have an SSN or ITIN. The IRS online tool typically requires a US taxpayer ID, so the online route is generally not available.

### How long does it take to open a US bank account for a foreign-owned LLC?

Fintech accounts can be approved in a few business days once you have your EIN. Traditional banks can take longer, and the full timeline, including forming the LLC and getting the EIN, often runs two to six weeks.

### Do I need to visit the US to open the account?

Not always. Many online providers let you apply with scanned documents and a video or document verification. Large traditional banks more often require a branch visit.

### What happens if I don't file Form 5472?

The IRS can assess a $25,000 penalty per form for a failure to file or incorrect filing, with additional penalties for continued non-compliance. Filing late is far cheaper than ignoring it, so speak to a tax professional if you've missed a deadline.

### Is my money safe in a fintech business account?

Fintech platforms usually hold funds at partner FDIC-member banks, and FDIC insurance covers deposits up to applicable limits if the partner bank fails. Coverage details vary by provider, so review the provider's disclosures.

### Can I use my personal foreign bank account instead?

You can technically, but it's a poor idea. Mixing funds undermines the liability shield, makes Form 5472 reporting harder, and most US payment processors expect a US business account.
