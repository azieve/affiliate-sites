Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetists are among the highest-paid nurses in the country, and a growing share of them work as independent contractors, locum providers, or through their own anesthesia staffing arrangements. If you are one of them, an LLC for CRNAs can be a useful tool for organizing your business, separating contract liabilities, and opening up tax planning options. It is not a substitute for malpractice insurance, and in some states you cannot use a standard LLC at all. This guide explains how it works in 2026.

The short version: if you are a W-2 employee of a hospital or anesthesia group, you probably do not need your own entity. If you take 1099 work, do locums, or run a pain management or office-based anesthesia practice, forming an entity is worth serious consideration. ## Do CRNAs Actually Need an LLC?

It depends on how you are paid. I have seen too many clinicians form an LLC because a colleague told them to, then discover it did nothing for them because they were still a W-2 employee with no business income to shelter.

**You likely do not need one if:**
- You are a W-2 employee of a hospital, surgery center, or anesthesia group
- You do not have any side business income

**You likely benefit from one if:**
- You work as a 1099 independent contractor for one or more facilities or groups
- You do locum tenens assignments in multiple facilities
- You provide anesthesia in an office-based or pain management setting under contract
- You earn side income from consulting, teaching, expert witness work, or a related business

An important point: an LLC does **not** protect you from your own professional negligence. If you personally commit malpractice, you remain personally liable. What an entity can do is protect you from business debts, contract disputes, a landlord claim, or the negligence of others you do not supervise. This is why professional liability insurance remains non-negotiable. For a broader look at the question, see our guide on [whether you need an LLC for your business](/blog/do-i-need-an-llc-for-my-business/).

## LLC vs. PLLC: What Your State May Require

Many states regulate how licensed professionals can form business entities. Depending on the state, a CRNA may be required to form a professional LLC (PLLC), a professional corporation (PC), or may be limited in who can own the entity.

Key points to verify before filing:

- **Name and licensure rules.** Some states require the entity name to include "PLLC" or "Professional Limited Liability Company."
- **Ownership restrictions.** Professional entities are commonly required to be owned by licensed professionals in the relevant field. Some states have rules specific to nursing, others group nurses with other professions.
- **State board approval.** Certain states require a certificate of authorization or a letter from the licensing board before the Secretary of State will accept the filing.
- **Scope of practice.** CRNA supervision rules vary by state. Opt-out states and non-opt-out states treat the CRNA relationship with a supervising anesthesiologist or surgeon differently, and that can affect how you structure a practice.

A standard single-member LLC is fine in many states for a CRNA who is simply invoicing facilities for services. Other states require the professional version. Check with your state board of nursing and Secretary of State before you file. ## How an LLC for CRNAs Is Taxed in 2026

By default, a single-member LLC is a "disregarded entity" for federal tax purposes. Your income flows through to your personal return on Schedule C, and you pay self-employment tax (roughly 15.3% on the first portion of net earnings up to the Social Security wage base, then 2.9% Medicare above it, plus an additional 0.9% Medicare tax at higher incomes). You can find the current figures on the [IRS self-employed individuals page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employed-individuals-tax-center).

For a CRNA earning well into six figures as a contractor, that self-employment tax is the single biggest reason people look at entity options.

### The S Corp Election

An LLC can elect to be taxed as an S corporation by filing Form 2553. Under that election, you pay yourself a "reasonable salary," which is subject to payroll taxes, and take remaining profit as distributions that are not subject to self-employment tax.

An illustrative example, not a projection: suppose a CRNA nets $240,000 from 1099 contracting. If a reasonable salary were set at $160,000, the remaining $80,000 could pass through as a distribution without self-employment tax. The potential payroll tax savings on that portion could be in the range of several thousand dollars per year, before accounting for added costs such as payroll service fees, state-level payroll filings, and possibly higher accounting fees. Your actual results depend on your state, income, and facts.

Important cautions:
- The IRS expects salary to be reasonable for the work performed. CRNA compensation is high, so a very low salary is a red flag. See our guides on [reasonable salary for S corp LLC owners](/blog/reasonable-salary-for-s-corp-llc-owners/) and [how to file Form 2553](/blog/how-to-file-form-2553-for-an-llc/).
- Some states tax S corporations differently or add entity-level taxes.
- The S corp usually makes sense only after profit reaches a level where savings outweigh the extra compliance cost. Many advisors discuss thresholds starting around $60,000 to $100,000 in net profit, but this is a rule of thumb, not a rule.
- Consult a CPA who works with healthcare professionals before electing.

## Liability Protection: What an LLC Does and Does Not Cover

Here is where precision matters, because this is the point most often misunderstood.

**An LLC or PLLC may help protect against:**
- Contract disputes and business debts
- Claims arising from other people's conduct (such as a partner or employee), depending on state law
- Lease and vendor obligations

**It will not protect you from:**
- Your own professional negligence
- Personal guarantees you sign
- Claims where the "corporate veil" is pierced because of commingled funds

To keep the protection intact, keep a separate business bank account, never pay personal expenses from it, and maintain basic records. Our guide to opening an [LLC bank account](/blog/llc-bank-account-how-to-open-one/) walks through that step.

Equally important, carry your own professional liability coverage even if a facility provides coverage under a contract. Check whether the facility's policy covers you as a contractor, whether it is claims-made or occurrence-based, and who pays for tail coverage. That is the kind of detail an attorney reviewing your contract can spot quickly.

## How to Set Up an LLC for CRNAs: Step by Step

1. **Confirm your state's rules.** Check with your board of nursing and Secretary of State about whether you need a PLLC or can use a standard LLC.
2. **Choose a name.** Include any required professional designator, and check availability.
3. **Pick your formation state.** Most CRNAs should form in the state where they primarily practice. Forming in a "friendly" state and then registering as a foreign LLC where you work generally adds cost without benefit. See [foreign LLC registration](/blog/foreign-llc-registration-doing-business-in-another-state/) if you work across state lines.
4. **Appoint a registered agent.** See [what a registered agent is](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/).
5. **File articles of organization.** Details are in our guide to [articles of organization](/blog/what-are-articles-of-organization/).
6. **Draft an operating agreement.** Even single-member entities benefit from one. See the [LLC operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/).
7. **Get an EIN.** The IRS issues them free; see [how to get an EIN for your LLC](/blog/how-to-get-an-ein-for-your-llc-free-irs-method/).
8. **Open a business bank account** and route all contract income through it.
9. **Update licensure and credentialing records** as needed, and notify payers and facilities of your new entity name and EIN.
10. **Add compliance reminders** for annual reports and any required professional license renewals.

Also check whether you must file a beneficial ownership report. Rules around this have shifted repeatedly, so review our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) for the current status before relying on any older information.

## Common Mistakes CRNAs Make With Their Entity

- **Forming an LLC while still W-2.** There is nothing to shelter, and you add annual fees.
- **Skipping the PLLC rules.** Filing the wrong entity type in a regulated state can lead to rejection or compliance problems.
- **Electing S corp too early.** Payroll costs can erase the benefit at lower income levels.
- **Mixing funds.** Paying personal bills from the business account weakens liability protection.
- **Ignoring estimated taxes.** Contractors without withholding often face penalties.
- **Assuming the LLC replaces malpractice insurance.** It does not.
- **Missing annual reports.** A lapsed entity can lose good standing and, in some cases, be administratively dissolved.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do CRNAs need an LLC?
Not necessarily. W-2 employees generally do not need one. CRNAs who work as 1099 contractors, take locum assignments, or run a practice often benefit from forming an entity, subject to state rules.

### Can a CRNA form a regular LLC, or does it have to be a PLLC?
It depends on the state. Some states allow a standard LLC for nurses, while others require a professional entity or board approval. Check with your state board of nursing and Secretary of State before filing.

### Does an LLC protect a CRNA from malpractice claims?
No, not from your own professional negligence. An LLC may help with business debts and certain third-party claims, but you still need professional liability insurance.

### How much does it cost to form an LLC for a CRNA?
State filing fees range from roughly $50 to $500 depending on the state, plus any formation service fees and annual report costs. See [how much an LLC costs](/learn/how-much-does-an-llc-cost/) for a breakdown.

### Should a CRNA elect S corp status?
Possibly, if net profit is high enough that payroll tax savings exceed added costs, and you pay yourself a reasonable salary. Speak with a CPA who understands healthcare professionals before deciding.

### Can I use an LLC for locum tenens work in several states?
Yes, but you may need to register as a foreign LLC in states where you do business, and licensing and tax rules in each state apply. Our [guide for independent contractor physicians and locum tenens](/blog/llc-for-independent-contractor-physicians-and-locum-tenens/) discusses this in more detail.

### What happens if I don't file my annual report?
You can face late fees and eventually administrative dissolution, which may remove your liability protection. Reinstating takes time and money, so calendar your deadlines.

### Is an LLC better than operating as a sole proprietor?
For 1099 CRNAs, an LLC generally offers more separation and more tax planning flexibility. See [LLC vs sole proprietorship](/learn/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/) for the full comparison.
