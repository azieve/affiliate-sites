The appeal is simple: you split your income into a W-2 salary (subject to payroll taxes) and distributions (not subject to payroll taxes). ## What "Reasonable Compensation" Means for an S Corp LLC Owner

An LLC that elects S corp status (via [IRS Form 2553](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-2553)) is taxed as an S corporation. If you work in the business, you're both a shareholder and an employee. The IRS position, stated plainly in its guidance on [S corporation compensation](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/s-corporation-compensation-and-medical-insurance-issues), is that shareholder-employees must receive reasonable compensation for the services they provide **before** taking non-wage distributions.

Here's why the IRS cares. In 2026, payroll taxes look like this:

| Tax | Rate | Applies to |
|---|---|---|
| Social Security (employer + employee) | 12.4% | Wages up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500 |
| Medicare (employer + employee) | 2.9% | All wages |
| Additional Medicare | 0.9% | Wages above $200,000 (single) / $250,000 (joint) |
| Distributions | 0% payroll tax | Profit paid out after reasonable salary |

The Social Security wage base comes from the [Social Security Administration](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/cola/cbb.html), which adjusts it each year. Every dollar you move from salary to distributions saves up to 15.3% in payroll tax. Pay yourself $0 in salary and take everything as distributions, and you've effectively avoided all employment taxes — which is exactly what the reasonable compensation rule exists to stop.

What the rule does **not** say: that salary must equal your profit, that salary must be a set percentage of profit, or that there's one correct number. Reasonable compensation is a range. Your job is to land inside that range and be able to explain how you got there.

## The Factors the IRS Uses to Judge an S Corp Owner Salary

The IRS and the courts have looked at reasonable compensation for decades, and the factors are consistent. When an examiner reviews S corp reasonable compensation, they're asking: *what would this business have to pay an unrelated person to do the work you do?*

The factors the IRS lists include:

- **Training and experience.** A licensed CPA with 15 years in practice commands more than a first-year bookkeeper.
- **Duties and responsibilities.** Are you doing the core revenue-generating work, managing staff, handling sales, or all three?
- **Time and effort devoted.** Full-time owner-operator versus a passive owner who checks in quarterly.
- **Dividend (distribution) history.** Large, regular distributions alongside a tiny salary is the classic red flag.
- **Payments to non-shareholder employees.** If you pay a staff designer $75,000 and yourself $30,000 as the lead designer and owner, that's hard to defend.
- **Timing and manner of paying bonuses** to key people.
- **What comparable businesses pay** for similar services.
- **Compensation agreements** and the use of a formula to determine pay.

The reality is that the "comparable businesses" factor tends to drive the outcome. Examiners and courts lean on market wage data, and the most commonly cited public source is the [Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/oes/) database, which publishes wage percentiles by occupation and metro area.

### The Case Everyone Cites: Watson v. United States

The cautionary tale in this area is *David E. Watson, P.C. v. United States* (8th Cir. 2012). Watson, a CPA, paid himself a $24,000 salary while taking roughly $200,000+ a year in distributions from his accounting firm's S corp. The IRS brought in a valuation expert, who concluded a reasonable salary for an accountant of his experience was about $91,000. The court agreed, and the difference was recharacterized as wages — with back payroll taxes, interest, and penalties.

The lesson isn't "salaries must be high." It's that a salary wildly out of line with what you'd pay someone else for the same job will not survive scrutiny, no matter how the entity is labeled.

## How Much Should I Pay Myself in an S Corp? A Step-by-Step Method

When owners ask me how much should I pay myself in an S corp, I walk them through the same process a good CFO would use. It's not complicated, but it does need to be written down.

### Step 1: List what you actually do

Break your work into roles with an approximate percentage of your time. A typical solo marketing consultant might look like this:

- 60% — client strategy and delivery (marketing manager)
- 20% — sales and business development (sales rep)
- 15% — admin, bookkeeping, scheduling (administrative assistant)
- 5% — management of contractors (general manager)

### Step 2: Pull market wage data for each role

Look up each role in the BLS data for your metro area. Use a percentile that matches your experience — the 25th percentile for a newer professional, the median for an established one, the 75th for a recognized specialist. Paid tools like RCReports exist specifically for this and produce a report you can keep in your files; many CPAs use them.

### Step 3: Weight and prorate

Multiply each wage by your time percentage, then adjust for whether you work full-time. If the market rate for a marketing manager in your area is $95,000 and that's 60% of your time, that contributes $57,000. Do this for each role and add them up.

### Step 4: Sanity-check against profit

Reasonable salary can't exceed what the business can pay. If your LLC netted $55,000, a $90,000 salary isn't "reasonable" — it's impossible. In lean years, a salary that consumes most or all of the profit can be appropriate. In strong years, salary will typically be a smaller share of profit.

### Step 5: Document it

Write a short memo — even one page — showing your roles, the data sources, the math, and the date. Approve it in a written consent of the members (or the board, if you've set one up) and keep it with your [LLC operating agreement](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/) records. Update it annually.

## Worked Example: S Corp Payroll Tax Savings at Three Profit Levels

Numbers make this real. Below are simplified scenarios for a single-owner LLC taxed as an S corp in 2026. "Sole prop / default LLC" assumes self-employment tax on 92.35% of net earnings. These figures ignore state taxes, the employer-side deduction for payroll taxes, and QBI effects to keep the comparison clean.

| Net profit before owner pay | Reasonable salary | Payroll tax on salary (15.3%) | SE tax as default LLC | Approx. payroll tax savings |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $60,000 | $45,000 | $6,885 | $8,478 | $1,593 |
| $120,000 | $60,000 | $9,180 | $16,955 | $7,775 |
| $250,000 | $110,000 | $16,830 | $29,040* | $12,210 |

*At $250,000, self-employment Social Security tax caps at the $184,500 wage base, so the default-LLC figure reflects 12.4% on $184,500 plus 2.9% Medicare on $230,875 (92.35% of profit). It also excludes the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax, which may apply in either structure depending on filing status.

Now subtract the real cost of running an S corp:

- **Payroll service:** roughly $500–$1,000/year for a single-employee plan from a provider like Gusto or ADP.
- **Separate S corp tax return (Form 1120-S):** often $800–$2,500 with a CPA, versus a Schedule C you might have prepared yourself.
- **State costs:** some states add fees or taxes for S corps (California's 1.5% franchise tax on S corp net income, with an $800 minimum, is the well-known example; see our [California LLC $800 franchise tax guide](/blog/california-llc-800-franchise-tax/)).
- **State unemployment insurance** on your own wages, in most states.

At $60,000 of profit, a ~$1,600 savings can be wiped out entirely by $1,500–$3,000 in added costs. At $120,000, you're likely ahead by several thousand dollars. That's why most advisors start the S corp conversation somewhere in the $70,000–$100,000+ net profit range — and why the salary you pick directly determines whether the election pays off.

### The 60/40 "rule" is a myth

You'll see the advice to pay yourself 60% salary and 40% distributions (or the reverse) everywhere. There is no such rule in the tax code, IRS guidance, or case law. It's a rough heuristic at best. A 60/40 split might be too high for a $400,000 consulting practice and too low for a $70,000 one. Use the role-based method above instead, and use any ratio only as a sanity check.

## Hidden Trade-Offs of a Low S Corp Owner Salary

Most content stops at "lower salary = lower tax." But a salary that's too low has costs beyond audit risk, and this is where I've seen too many business owners trip themselves up.

**1. Smaller retirement contributions.** If you run a Solo 401(k) through your S corp, the employer profit-sharing contribution is capped at 25% of your W-2 wages. Paying yourself $40,000 limits that piece to $10,000; at $90,000 it's $22,500. Your employee deferral limit for 2026 is $24,500 (plus catch-up if you're 50+), but you can't defer more than you're paid. For owners serious about retirement saving, a higher salary can be the better overall deal.

**2. Lower Social Security benefits.** Your future Social Security benefit is based on your earnings record. Years of artificially low wages mean a smaller check later. For some owners that's an acceptable trade; it should be a conscious one.

**3. The QBI interaction.** The 20% qualified business income deduction — made permanent by the 2025 tax law — is calculated on profit *after* your salary. A lower salary increases QBI, but for higher earners in certain service businesses, the deduction phases out and may depend on W-2 wages paid. This gets technical fast and is exactly where a CPA earns their fee.

**4. Loan and mortgage applications.** Lenders often look at W-2 income first. A $35,000 salary paired with $150,000 in distributions can complicate a mortgage application unless the lender is comfortable reading your K-1s and business returns.

**5. Audit exposure.** If the IRS recharacterizes distributions as wages, you owe both halves of payroll tax, plus interest and potential penalties (including the trust fund recovery penalty concerns that come with underpaid payroll). The savings from an aggressive salary rarely justify that exposure.

## Common Mistakes When Setting a Reasonable Salary for S Corp LLC Owners

After reviewing a lot of small-business setups, these are the errors that show up again and again.

**Taking distributions with no salary at all.** If you work in the business and the business has profit, a $0 salary is the most likely thing to draw IRS attention. The exception is a genuine startup year with little or no profit — but distributions should be similarly minimal.

**Not actually running payroll.** Reasonable salary means real W-2 wages with withholding, quarterly Form 941 filings, and a year-end W-2 — not a "salary" you transfer to yourself whenever you feel like it. If you're unsure how draws, distributions, and salary interact, our guide on [how to pay yourself from an LLC](/blog/how-to-pay-yourself-from-an-llc/) covers the mechanics.

**Electing S corp too late or too early.** Form 2553 is generally due within 2 months and 15 days of the start of the tax year you want it to apply to. Elect too early, when profit is low, and you pay for payroll and an 1120-S return without meaningful savings.

**Forgetting health insurance treatment.** For owners holding more than 2% of the S corp, health insurance premiums paid by the company are included in W-2 Box 1 wages (but not subject to Social Security and Medicare). Done right, this sets up the self-employed health insurance deduction on your personal return. Done wrong, you lose the deduction.

**Never revisiting the number.** Your role, revenue, and the market all change. A salary set in 2023 for a one-person shop may not be reasonable in 2026 after you've hired two people and doubled revenue.

**Mixing personal and business money.** Paying personal expenses from the business account blurs what's a distribution and what's compensation. Keep separate accounts and clean books — this protects your liability shield as well as your tax position.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a reasonable salary for S corp LLC owners?

There's no fixed number. A reasonable salary for S corp LLC owners is what the business would have to pay an unrelated person to perform the same services, based on your duties, experience, hours, and local market wages. For many full-time owner-operators, that lands somewhere between the 25th percentile and median wage for their occupation in their metro area, adjusted for the business's ability to pay.

### Can an S corp owner pay themselves no salary?

Only in limited situations — for example, if you don't perform services for the business, or the business has little or no profit to distribute. If you work in the business and take distributions, the IRS expects reasonable W-2 compensation first. A $0 salary paired with meaningful distributions is one of the most common audit triggers for S corps.

### Is the 60/40 salary-to-distribution rule legitimate?

No. The 60/40 split is a rule of thumb, not an IRS requirement or court standard. Reasonable compensation is based on the value of the services you provide, not a percentage of profit. Some owners end up near 60/40 using a proper analysis; many don't.

### How much can an S corp save in payroll taxes?

It depends on your profit and salary. In 2026, a single-owner business netting $120,000 that pays a $60,000 reasonable salary could save roughly $7,000–$8,000 in payroll taxes compared with default LLC taxation, before subtracting payroll service, tax preparation, and state costs of roughly $1,500–$3,500 a year. Savings generally become meaningful once net profit clears $70,000–$100,000.

### What happens if the IRS says my S corp salary is too low?

The IRS can recharacterize some or all of your distributions as wages. You'd then owe the employer and employee shares of Social Security and Medicare tax on the reclassified amount, plus interest and potentially penalties for failure to deposit and failure to file accurate payroll returns. Good documentation of how you set your salary is your best defense.

### How often should I adjust my S corp owner salary?

Review it at least once a year, ideally before year-end when you have a clear view of profit. Adjust it when your role, hours, revenue, or staff change significantly. Keep a dated memo each year showing your reasoning and data sources.

### Do I need a CPA to set my reasonable salary?

It isn't legally required, but it's strongly recommended. A CPA or enrolled agent can pull market compensation data, model the QBI and retirement contribution trade-offs, and help you document the decision. Given what's at stake, the fee is usually well worth it.

### Does a reasonable salary have to be paid through payroll?

Yes. Shareholder-employee compensation must be paid as W-2 wages with federal (and usually state) income tax withholding, Social Security and Medicare withholding, quarterly Form 941 filings, and annual W-2 and W-3 filings. Most owners use a payroll service to handle this automatically.

## The Bottom Line

Setting a reasonable salary for S corp LLC owners is a balancing act: pay yourself enough to match what your work is worth on the open market, and you keep the payroll tax savings that make the S corp election worthwhile in the first place. Pay too little and you invite an IRS adjustment; pay too much and you erode the benefit.

The owners who handle this well in 2026 share three habits: they base the number on real market data rather than a ratio, they write down how they got there, and they revisit it every year with a tax professional. Do those three things and the S corp structure can be one of the most valuable tools available to a profitable LLC.
