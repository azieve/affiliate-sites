Running an assisted living facility means taking responsibility for people's safety, medications, meals, and daily dignity, all while carrying a mortgage or lease, a payroll, and a regulator who can walk in unannounced. That's a lot of exposure for one person to hold personally. A well-built LLC for an assisted living facility won't remove that risk, but it can keep a lawsuit against the business from automatically reaching your house, savings, and other assets.

This guide covers how to structure the entity, what the LLC does and doesn't protect, how licensing interacts with formation, and what it costs in 2026. The most common formation mistake I see is putting the building and the operations into the same entity. We'll get to that.

## Why an LLC Makes Sense for an Assisted Living Facility

An LLC (limited liability company) is a legal entity separate from its owners. Contracts, debts, and most lawsuit judgments attach to the company, not to you personally, provided you keep the entity properly maintained. If you're new to the concept, our guide on [what an LLC is](/learn/what-is-an-llc/) covers the basics.

For an assisted living LLC, the practical benefits are:

- **Contract protection.** Vendor agreements, staffing contracts, equipment leases, and resident agreements are signed by the company.
- **Debt separation.** A business loan or line of credit in the LLC's name generally doesn't make you personally liable unless you sign a personal guarantee. Lenders often require one for new facilities, so read that paperwork carefully.
- **Credibility with licensors and payers.** State licensing agencies, Medicaid waiver programs, long-term care insurers, and referral partners expect to deal with a formal entity.
- **Flexible taxation.** An LLC can be taxed as a sole proprietorship, partnership, or S corporation. See the [IRS overview of LLCs](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc) for the federal classification rules.

If you're still weighing the alternatives, our comparison of [LLC vs sole proprietorship](/learn/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/) shows why running a care business as a sole proprietor is a risky choice.

## What an LLC Will NOT Protect You From

I want to be blunt here, because the marketing around LLCs tends to oversell them. In senior care, the biggest claims typically involve falls, medication errors, elopement (residents wandering off), neglect allegations, and wrongful death. An LLC does not shield you from:

- **Your own negligence.** If you personally provide care, or personally direct a bad decision, you can be named directly in a suit.
- **Regulatory penalties and license actions.** Fines, admission bans, and revocations hit the licensed entity, and in some states regulators can look at the individuals behind it.
- **Personal guarantees.** Anything you sign personally stays personal.
- **Piercing the corporate veil.** Courts can disregard the LLC if you commingle funds, undercapitalize it, or ignore formalities.
- **Unpaid payroll taxes.** Responsible persons can be held personally liable for trust-fund taxes.

That's why insurance is part of the structure, not an add-on. Plan for professional liability (sometimes called errors and omissions), general liability, abuse and molestation coverage, workers' compensation, property coverage, and often umbrella coverage. Many states and lenders set minimums. Your broker should be one who actually insures senior living, because generic small-business policies often exclude care-related claims.

## The Structure Most Operators Should Consider: OpCo and PropCo

If you own the real estate, the standard approach is to use two LLCs:

1. **The operating company (OpCo)** holds the license, employs staff, signs resident agreements, and carries the operational liability.
2. **The property company (PropCo)** owns the building and leases it to the OpCo at a market-rate rent.

The reasoning is simple. If a resident sues the OpCo and wins a large judgment, the PropCo's building isn't automatically on the table. The reverse also holds, since a property-related claim, such as a structural problem or a contractor dispute, is less likely to drag the operating business under.

A few cautions from experience:

- **Use a real written lease** with real rent, paid on time, from the OpCo's bank account to the PropCo's. A "paper" arrangement that's ignored in practice won't hold up.
- **Check lender and licensing rules first.** Some mortgage lenders, especially those using HUD or SBA-backed programs, have requirements about who must own what. Some states require that the licensee have control or a leasehold interest in the premises.
- **Capitalize the OpCo adequately.** A thinly funded operating company invites veil-piercing arguments.
- **Keep records separate.** Each entity needs its own EIN, bank account, books, and insurance.

I've seen too many first-time operators buy a building, put it in the same LLC that holds the license, and then discover the structure can't be cleanly unwound without triggering a transfer tax or a licensing change of ownership. Fixing it later is far more expensive than doing it right once. If you lease rather than own, a single OpCo is usually sufficient to start.

Whichever way you go, this assisted living business structure should be reviewed by a healthcare attorney in your state before you file anything.

## How Licensing Fits Into Forming Your LLC

Here's the sequence that saves the most time and money: **choose the entity and state first, but don't assume the LLC alone lets you operate.** In the U.S., assisted living is licensed and regulated at the state level, and the rules vary enormously, including the name of the license (assisted living residence, residential care facility, personal care home, and so on), required administrator credentials, staffing ratios, building and fire codes, and resident-level-of-care limits.

For the federal side, Medicare generally doesn't cover assisted living room and board. Medicaid coverage, where available, usually runs through state waiver programs and has its own enrollment steps. The [Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://www.cms.gov/) is a good starting point for understanding the federal framework, but your state's licensing agency is the authority on whether you can open the doors.

A practical sequence for how to start an assisted living facility:

1. **Contact your state licensing agency** early and get the current application packet, fee schedule, and administrator requirements.
2. **Form your LLC in the state where the facility will operate.** Because the licensee must be registered where it does business, forming elsewhere (such as Delaware) usually just adds a second state's fees and filings.
3. **Get an EIN** from the IRS, which is free through the IRS website.
4. **Draft an operating agreement** (more below).
5. **Open a dedicated business bank account** and fund it adequately.
6. **Secure insurance** and have certificates ready for the licensing application.
7. **Apply for the facility license** in the LLC's exact legal name, matching the state filing, and obtain local zoning approval, fire inspection, and occupancy permits.

One more gotcha: licensing applications typically ask for disclosure of owners, managers, and sometimes background checks on anyone with a controlling interest. Choose your LLC members carefully, because adding a silent partner later may trigger a change-of-ownership filing.

## Operating Agreement: Don't Skip It

Even a single-member LLC should have a written operating agreement, and a multi-member LLC absolutely must. In a care business it does real work: it defines who can sign leases and loans, who can act as the licensed administrator's supervisor, what happens if a member dies or becomes disabled, and how members can be bought out. Our [LLC operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/) walks through the key clauses.

For facilities with investors or family partners, add provisions on capital calls, distributions, and decision thresholds. Vague agreements between relatives are where a lot of avoidable disputes begin.

## Taxes for an Assisted Living LLC

By default, a single-member LLC is taxed as a disregarded entity and a multi-member LLC as a partnership. Profits flow through to your personal return, and self-employment tax generally applies to your share of active business income.

Once the facility is profitable, many owners look at an **S corporation election** (Form 2553). In broad terms, an S corp lets you pay yourself a "reasonable" salary subject to payroll tax and take the remaining profit as distributions that aren't subject to self-employment tax. The catch is that the IRS expects the salary to be reasonable for your role, and for an owner who actively runs a facility, that bar can be meaningful. Compare the trade-offs in our [LLC vs S-Corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) guide, and run the numbers with a CPA before electing.

Other tax points specific to facilities:

- **Payroll is your largest cost.** Caregiver classification matters. Misclassifying aides as independent contractors is a common and expensive audit trigger.
- **Property taxes and depreciation.** If a separate PropCo owns the building, depreciation and cost-segregation strategies may apply. A real estate-savvy CPA is worth the fee.
- **Sales and local taxes.** Some jurisdictions tax certain services or require local business licenses.
- **Resident payments.** Whether any portion of resident fees is medical-expense-deductible to the resident is the resident's tax question, not yours, but be ready to provide itemized statements.

Estimated quarterly tax payments apply, so plan cash flow around them. Tax rules and thresholds change, particularly in 2026 as various federal provisions are phased or revised, so confirm current rules with a professional.

## What It Costs to Set Up in 2026

Formation is the cheap part. A realistic first-year budget looks like this:

- **State LLC filing fee:** about $35 to $500 depending on state
- **Registered agent:** $0 to $150 per year (often bundled)
- **Formation service (optional):** $0 to $300
- **Annual report or franchise tax:** $0 to $800+ depending on state
- **Facility license application and inspections:** often several hundred to several thousand dollars
- **Insurance:** frequently the largest recurring item, and highly dependent on bed count, location, and claims history

Opening a facility itself takes far more capital than the LLC, including property, renovations, working capital to cover payroll before census builds, and reserves. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need an LLC to open an assisted living facility?

No law requires one in every state, but most operators use an entity because operating as a sole proprietor leaves your personal assets exposed. Your state licensing agency will specify which entity types it accepts. An LLC is the most common choice because of its simplicity and flexibility.

### How much does it cost to set up an LLC for an assisted living facility?

The LLC itself typically costs $35 to $500 in state filing fees, plus optional service fees of roughly $0 to $300. The much larger costs are licensing, insurance, property, and working capital. Check current fees with your state and your chosen provider.

### Should I use one LLC or two (OpCo and PropCo)?

If you own the real estate, two entities are the usual approach, so a judgment against the operating business doesn't automatically reach the property. If you lease the building, one operating LLC is generally enough to start. A healthcare attorney can confirm what your state and lender allow.

### Does an LLC protect me from resident lawsuits?

Only partly. The LLC separates business liabilities from your personal assets in many situations, but it doesn't protect you from your own negligence, personal guarantees, or veil-piercing if you ignore formalities. Adequate insurance is essential.

### Which state should I form my LLC in?

Generally the state where the facility operates, since it must be licensed and registered there. Forming in another state like Delaware or Wyoming usually adds cost without benefit for a physical, state-licensed operation. See our [best state to form an LLC](/blog/best-state-to-form-an-llc/) guide for the broader trade-offs.

### Can an LLC apply for the assisted living license?

Yes, in most states the LLC is the applicant and licensee. Use the LLC's exact legal name on every application, and expect disclosures about owners, managers, and administrators, sometimes including background checks.

### Do I need a PLLC instead of a regular LLC?

Typically no. Professional LLCs (PLLCs) are for licensed professions such as physicians and attorneys. A facility is usually licensed as a business, although any licensed clinicians you employ may have their own rules. Confirm with your state.

### Should I elect S corp status?

It can reduce self-employment taxes once profits are steady, but it adds payroll compliance and a reasonable-salary requirement. It isn't automatically better. Model it with a CPA first.
