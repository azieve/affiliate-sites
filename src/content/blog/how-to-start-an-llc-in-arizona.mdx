## Why Arizona Is a Strong State for Your LLC

Arizona's appeal in 2026 comes down to cost and simplicity. Three features stand out:

- **A flat 2.5% income tax.** Arizona finished phasing in its flat individual income tax rate of 2.5% — one of the lowest in the nation among states that tax income at all. Because an LLC's profits pass through to your personal return by default, this rate applies directly to your business income. Compare that to California's top marginal rate of 13.3% or Oregon's 9.9%, and the savings for a profitable single-member LLC add up fast.
- **No annual report and no annual fee.** This is the one most people don't believe. Unlike California's $800 minimum annual franchise tax or the annual reports required in states like Florida and Texas, Arizona LLCs file no annual report and pay no recurring state fee just to stay in good standing. You file once, and your formation is effectively permanent unless you dissolve it or fall out of compliance with your statutory agent.
- **A low, flat $50 filing fee.** Arizona's Articles of Organization cost $50 to file with regular processing — far below states like Massachusetts ($500) or Tennessee (which charges per member).

The trade-off is the publication requirement, which I'll cover in detail below. It's the single most common reason Arizona LLCs accidentally fall out of compliance in their first 60 days.

## Step 1: Choose and Check Your LLC Name

Your Arizona LLC name must be distinguishable from every other business entity already on file with the Arizona Corporation Commission (ACC), and it must include one of the following designators:

- "Limited Liability Company," or
- the abbreviation "LLC," "L.L.C.," "LC," or "L.C."

A few naming rules worth knowing in 2026:

- You **cannot** use words that imply you're a different kind of entity (e.g., "Corporation," "Incorporated," or "Inc.").
- Restricted words like "bank," "trust," "insurance," or "deposit" require additional approval.
- The name can't be misleading about your business's purpose.

Check availability using the ACC's free [eCorp business entity search](https://ecorp.azcc.gov/). I always recommend searching three or four variations before committing, because Arizona's "distinguishable" standard is stricter than most founders assume — adding "Arizona" or "LLC" to an existing name usually isn't enough to clear it.

If you've found a name but aren't ready to file, you can reserve it for 120 days by filing an Application to Reserve Entity Name for **$10** ($45 for expedited service). For most people this is unnecessary — if your name is available, just file your Articles and lock it in.

It's also worth checking that the matching `.com` domain and your preferred social handles are available before you commit. A formation service like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) bundles a name-availability check into its filing flow, which catches conflicts before you pay the state fee.

## Step 2: Appoint a Statutory Agent

Arizona calls what most states label a "registered agent" a **statutory agent**. Every Arizona LLC must continuously maintain one. The statutory agent is the person or company designated to receive legal documents — service of process, lawsuits, and official state notices — on the LLC's behalf.

Your statutory agent must:

- Have a physical street address in Arizona (a P.O. box alone won't satisfy the requirement), and
- Be available during normal business hours to accept documents.

You can act as your own statutory agent if you're an Arizona resident, but I generally advise against it for two reasons. First, your name and street address become part of the permanent public record — not ideal if you run your business from home. Second, you have to be physically present during business hours; if you're served with a lawsuit while you're traveling and miss it, you can lose by default. (For a fuller treatment of the role, see our guide on [what a registered agent is](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/).)

## Step 3: File Your Articles of Organization

This is the step that legally creates your LLC. You'll file the **Articles of Organization** with the Arizona Corporation Commission, either online through [eCorp](https://ecorp.azcc.gov/) or by mail.

Your Articles will require:

- The LLC's name
- The statutory agent's name, address, and signed acceptance (Form M002)
- The LLC's known place of business address in Arizona
- The management structure — member-managed or manager-managed
- The names and addresses of members or managers (depending on structure)

The fees as of 2026:

- **Regular filing:** $50
- **Expedited processing:** an additional $35 (drops turnaround from a few weeks to a few business days)

Online filings through eCorp are processed faster than mailed paper filings, and I almost always recommend filing online unless you have an unusual situation. Once approved, the ACC issues confirmation that your LLC officially exists.

## Step 4: Handle the Arizona Publication Requirement

Here's the step unique to Arizona — and the one that catches the most founders off guard. Within **60 days** of your LLC's approval, you must publish a **Notice of Publication** in an ACC-approved newspaper in the county of your statutory agent's address. The notice must run for **three consecutive publications**.

The critical exception: if your statutory agent's address is in **Maricopa County** (Phoenix metro) or **Pima County** (Tucson), you do **not** need to publish. The ACC automatically posts your notice to its public database, satisfying the requirement at no extra cost. Because these two counties cover the large majority of Arizona's population and business activity, most new LLCs are exempt — but you have to confirm your county to know for sure.

If you're outside those two counties, publication typically costs $30–$300 depending on the newspaper, and you'll want to use a paper from the ACC's approved list. Miss the 60-day window and your LLC can be administratively dissolved, so set a calendar reminder the day your Articles are approved.

In my experience, this is exactly where DIY filers stumble — they nail the Articles, then forget the publication step entirely because no other state has it. A formation service that explicitly handles Arizona publication is worth the small surcharge here purely as insurance against an administrative dissolution.

## Step 5: Create an Operating Agreement

Arizona does not legally require an operating agreement, but skipping one is a mistake I see cost owners dearly. An operating agreement is the internal contract that governs how your LLC is run: ownership percentages, how profits and losses are allocated, voting rights, what happens when a member leaves, and how the company is dissolved.

Even for a single-member LLC, having an operating agreement on file strengthens your liability shield. Courts look for evidence that you're treating the LLC as a genuinely separate entity — and "we never bothered with paperwork" is precisely the kind of fact a plaintiff's attorney uses to argue for piercing the corporate veil. Banks frequently ask for one when you open a business account, too.

## Step 6: Get an EIN and Set Up Taxes

An **Employer Identification Number (EIN)** is your business's federal tax ID. You'll need it to open a business bank account, hire employees, and file taxes. You can get one for free directly from the [IRS EIN application](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/apply-for-an-employer-identification-number-ein-online) in about 15 minutes — never pay a third party for the EIN itself, though many formation packages bundle it as a convenience.

On the state side, a few Arizona-specific tax items to address in 2026:

- **Transaction Privilege Tax (TPT):** Arizona's version of a sales tax is technically a tax on the privilege of doing business. If you sell taxable goods or certain services, you'll need a TPT license through the Arizona Department of Revenue at [AZTaxes.gov](https://azdor.gov/). The state license fee is modest, with additional per-jurisdiction fees depending on the cities you operate in.
- **State income tax:** Arizona's flat 2.5% rate applies to pass-through LLC income on your personal return.
- **Federal BOI reporting:** Beneficial Ownership Information reporting rules have shifted considerably over the past two years, and the requirements for domestic LLCs have changed since the original 2024 rollout. Before assuming you must (or must not) file, check the current FinCEN guidance and our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) — and when in doubt, confirm with a tax professional, because the penalties for guessing wrong have historically been steep.

If your LLC becomes consistently profitable, ask your accountant whether electing **S-Corp taxation** makes sense. For owners netting roughly $60,000+ in profit, the self-employment tax savings can be substantial — our [LLC vs S-Corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) breakdown covers the math.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to start an LLC in Arizona?

The state filing fee for Articles of Organization is $50 in 2026, with optional expedited processing for an additional $35. If you're outside Maricopa and Pima counties, you'll also pay $30–$300 for the newspaper publication requirement. A statutory agent service, if you don't act as your own, typically runs $0–$125 in the first year.

### Do I need to publish my LLC in a newspaper in Arizona?

Only if your statutory agent's address is outside Maricopa County and Pima County. In those two counties, the Arizona Corporation Commission posts your notice to its public database automatically, so no newspaper publication is required. Everywhere else, you must publish for three consecutive runs within 60 days of approval.

### How long does it take to form an LLC in Arizona?

Regular online filings through the eCorp portal are typically processed within a few weeks, while expedited processing (an extra $35) brings that down to a few business days. Mailed paper filings take longer. Don't forget the separate 60-day publication window if it applies to you.

### Does Arizona require an annual report for LLCs?

No. Arizona is one of the few states that does not require LLCs to file an annual report or pay an annual fee. Once your LLC is formed and your statutory agent is maintained, it stays in good standing without recurring state filings.

### What is a statutory agent in Arizona?

A statutory agent is Arizona's term for what other states call a registered agent — the individual or company designated to receive legal documents and official notices on behalf of your LLC. They must have a physical Arizona street address and be available during business hours.

### Can I be my own statutory agent in Arizona?

Yes, if you're an Arizona resident with a physical street address in the state and you're available during business hours. However, doing so puts your name and address on the public record and risks missed legal documents if you're unavailable, which is why many owners use a commercial service.

### Do I need an operating agreement for an Arizona LLC?

Arizona law does not require one, but it's strongly recommended. An operating agreement defines ownership, management, and profit allocation, and helps preserve your personal liability protection — even for single-member LLCs.
