A home inspector sells one thing: an opinion about a property, delivered in a few hours, that a buyer relies on while making the largest purchase of their life. If you miss a failing roof, a cracked heat exchanger, or active knob-and-tube wiring, the buyer's repair bill can run into five figures, and they will look for someone to pay it. That is the reason an LLC for home inspectors is one of the most common structures in the trade, and also one of the most misunderstood.

An LLC can separate your personal assets from your business debts and some claims. It can also make you look like a professional operation to agents, lenders, and clients. It does not make you immune from claims about your own work. This guide explains what an LLC does and doesn't do for a home inspection business, what it costs in 2026, how it's taxed, and how to set one up without wasting time. ## Why Home Inspectors Form LLCs

Most new inspectors start as sole proprietors by default. You get a license (in the roughly three dozen states that regulate inspectors), buy software and tools, and start taking jobs. That works, but legally you and the business are the same person. A judgment against the business can reach your savings, your car, and in some states your home equity.

Forming a home inspection business LLC changes the default in several ways:

- **Separation of business debts.** Vendor bills, equipment leases, office rent, and contractor invoices belong to the LLC, not to you.
- **Protection from other people's mistakes.** If you hire a second inspector or an assistant and they cause a claim, your personal assets are generally better shielded than they would be as a sole proprietor.
- **Credibility.** Real estate agents refer inspectors they perceive as established. "Caldwell Property Inspections, LLC" reads differently than a personal name on an invoice.
- **Cleaner finances.** A separate bank account and books make tax time easier and audits less painful.
- **Flexibility.** You can later add partners, hire inspectors, or elect S-corp taxation without rebuilding the business.

For a broader look at the entity itself, see our guide on [what an LLC is](/learn/what-is-an-llc/) and the [LLC vs sole proprietorship](/learn/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/) comparison.

## What an LLC Does Not Protect You From

This is the section I wish every new inspector read before buying a formation package. In my experience, many inspectors assume the LLC makes them bulletproof. It doesn't.

**You remain personally liable for your own negligence.** If you personally inspect a home and carelessly miss something a reasonable inspector would have caught, a plaintiff can sue you individually as well as your company. Courts generally don't let professionals hide behind an entity for their own professional errors. The LLC protects you from business debts and from other people's actions, not from your own work product.

That makes **home inspector liability** a layered problem, and the layers are:

1. **Pre-inspection agreement.** A well-drafted contract that defines the scope (visible and accessible areas only), sets limits on damages, and includes a notice or arbitration clause. Several states restrict or ban limitation-of-liability clauses, so have a local attorney review yours.
2. **Standards of practice.** Follow your state's rules or the standards of a body like [ASHI](https://www.homeinspector.org/) or InterNACHI, and document what you did.
3. **Insurance.** This is your real shield against a claim about your own work. See the next section.
4. **The LLC.** The final layer, protecting what's left if a claim exceeds your policy or arises from business operations.

I've seen too many small service business owners spend $500 on an LLC and $0 on a quality contract, then discover the contract was the thing that mattered when a dispute arrived. Budget for both.

## Home Inspector Insurance: What You Need With the LLC

**Home inspector insurance** is what pays when something goes wrong. An LLC without coverage is a thin shield. The core policies are:

| Coverage | What it covers | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Errors & Omissions (E&O) / professional liability | Claims that you missed or misreported a defect | Required or strongly expected in many states and by many agents |
| General liability | Bodily injury or property damage during an inspection (a fall through a ceiling, a damaged floor) | Often required by clients and for some association memberships |
| Commercial auto | Vehicle accidents while traveling to jobs | Personal auto policies often exclude business use |
| Workers' compensation | Employee injuries | Required once you have employees in most states |
| Cyber liability | Client data and report-portal breaches | Increasingly sensible as reports are delivered online |

Premiums vary widely by state, claims history, annual inspection volume, and whether you inspect residential only or also commercial and add-ons like mold, radon, or sewer scopes. Many solo inspectors budget somewhere in the low-to-mid four figures per year for a combined package, but you should get quotes from carriers that specialize in inspectors, such as those endorsed by your professional association.

Two practical gotchas:

- **Name the LLC on the policy.** Make sure the named insured is the business entity and you individually. A policy issued to "John Smith" may not respond properly to a claim against "Smith Home Inspections, LLC."
- **Check claims-made terms.** E&O is typically written on a claims-made basis. If you cancel the policy when you retire or close the business, you may need tail (extended reporting) coverage to be protected for past inspections. Because statutes of limitations can run for years after an inspection, this matters.

## State Licensing and Naming Rules

About 38 states license or regulate home inspectors, and the details differ. Some require E&O insurance as a condition of licensure, some require specific education and exam hours, and some have rules about advertising and business names. Others, like a handful of states with no licensing, leave you to association credentials alone.

Before you file, check three things with your state's inspector licensing board:

- **Does the license attach to you, to the company, or both?** In many states the individual holds the license and the LLC simply employs or contracts the inspector. Some boards want the business entity registered or its name matching what's on your license.
- **Name restrictions.** Names that imply a government affiliation or credentials you don't hold can be rejected or can trigger complaints.
- **Required disclosures.** Some states require your license number on contracts, reports, and advertising, including under the LLC's name.

You should also confirm whether your state requires a particular entity type. Most don't restrict inspectors to LLCs, but a few jurisdictions regulate professional entities for licensed trades. When in doubt, a short call to the board or a local business attorney is cheap insurance.

- Annual or biennial report fees in most states
- Registered agent fees (often $0 for the first year with a service, then $100 to $300 per year)
- Business license or local permits

Keep in mind that where you form matters less than inspectors often think. Because your inspections are performed in your home state, you'll generally form there rather than in Delaware or Wyoming. Forming elsewhere and then registering as a foreign LLC at home just adds fees.

## Step-by-Step: Setting Up Your Home Inspection Business LLC

Here is the efficient order of operations, the way a CFO would sequence it to avoid rework:

1. **Confirm licensing rules.** Check your state board's requirements for business entities, names, and insurance before choosing a name.
2. **Pick and check the name.** Search your Secretary of State database and your state's trademark records. Include "LLC" or the state's required designator.
3. **Choose a registered agent.** You can serve as your own, but your address becomes public and you must be reliably available during business hours. Inspectors who are in crawlspaces all day often shouldn't. See [what a registered agent is](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/).
4. **File the Articles of Organization.** Directly with the state or through a service.
5. **Write an operating agreement.** Even for a single-member LLC. It supports your liability protection and is often requested by banks. Our [operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/) covers the essentials.
6. **Get an EIN.** It's free directly from the IRS; no one should charge you for the number itself. See our guide to [getting an EIN free](/blog/how-to-get-an-ein-for-your-llc-free-irs-method/).
7. **Open a business bank account.** Keep inspection revenue and expenses completely separate.
8. **Update your license and insurance.** Add the LLC to your state license records if required, and get the policy endorsed or reissued in the LLC's name.
9. **Update contracts and marketing.** Your pre-inspection agreement, invoices, reports, website, and vehicle signage should all show the LLC's legal name and your license number.
10. **Check federal reporting.** As of 2025, FinCEN removed beneficial ownership reporting requirements for U.S.-formed domestic companies, but rules have shifted repeatedly, so confirm current status in our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/).

Most inspectors can complete steps 1 through 7 in a week, often in a few days when the state processes online filings quickly.

## How an LLC for Home Inspectors Is Taxed

By default, the IRS treats a single-member LLC as a "disregarded entity." You report income and expenses on Schedule C of your personal return, just as you did as a sole proprietor. A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default. The [IRS explains these classifications](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc) on its LLC page.

The practical consequences:

- **Self-employment tax.** You owe roughly 15.3% on net earnings (Social Security and Medicare components, subject to the annual Social Security wage base). Forming an LLC doesn't reduce this by itself. See our guide to [self-employment tax for LLC owners](/blog/self-employment-tax-for-llc-owners/).
- **Estimated payments.** You'll generally pay quarterly estimates since no one withholds taxes from your inspection fees.
- **Deductions.** Common ones include E&O and general liability premiums, tools (moisture meters, thermal cameras, ladders, drones), report software subscriptions, continuing education, licensing and association dues, vehicle expenses, and marketing.

**Illustrative example:** Suppose an inspector nets $90,000 after expenses. As a default LLC, the entire $90,000 is exposed to self-employment tax. If that inspector elects S-corp status and pays themself a reasonable salary of, say, $55,000, only the salary is subject to payroll taxes, and the remaining distribution is not. The potential savings can be real, but they're offset by payroll costs, additional tax filings, and the IRS requirement that salary be "reasonable." This is an illustration, not a prediction, and your results depend on your facts.

My opinion: most inspectors in their first year or two should stay with the default taxation and revisit an S-corp election once net profit consistently reaches a level where the savings clearly exceed the added accounting cost. Many advisors use a rough threshold in the neighborhood of $60,000 to $80,000 of net profit, but that is a rule of thumb, not a rule. Our [LLC vs S-corp guide](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) and [LLC taxes overview](/blog/llc-taxes-how-are-llcs-taxed/) explain how to evaluate the election. Talk to a CPA before filing Form 2553.

## Common Mistakes Inspectors Make With Their LLC

- **Commingling funds.** Paying personal bills from the business account (or vice versa) invites a court to "pierce the veil" and ignore your liability protection.
- **Skipping the annual report.** Missing it can lead to administrative dissolution and loss of good standing, which can also jeopardize your license.
- **Signing contracts personally.** Always sign as "Jane Doe, Member, Doe Inspections, LLC."
- **Under-insuring.** Choosing the lowest E&O limit to save a few hundred dollars is a classic false economy.
- **Ignoring add-on services.** Mold, radon, WDO (wood-destroying organism), pool, and sewer-scope services can carry different insurance and licensing requirements. Confirm each is covered.
- **Using an outdated pre-inspection agreement.** Laws and court decisions on liability limits change. Have the contract reviewed periodically.

Inspectors who also run handyman-type side work or advise on repairs should read our [LLC for handyman and home services](/blog/llc-for-handyman-and-home-services/) guide, because repair work creates conflicts of interest and some states prohibit inspecting and repairing the same property.

## Is an LLC Right for You?

An LLC makes sense for most full-time inspectors, especially if you have employees, own assets worth protecting, or work in a litigious market. Part-timers with minimal assets might reasonably delay, though the cost of formation is small relative to the cost of one claim.

The simplest summary I can offer after years of watching small service businesses: insure first, contract carefully, then form the LLC. An LLC for home inspectors works best as one layer of a system, not a substitute for the others. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do home inspectors need an LLC?

No law requires it, but most professionals form one. An LLC separates business debts and some claims from your personal assets. It doesn't protect you from claims arising from your own negligence, so insurance remains essential.

### How much does it cost to start an LLC for a home inspection business?

State filing fees typically range from about $35 to $500, and a formation service may add $0 to a few hundred dollars. Plan separately for insurance, registered agent fees, and annual reports. Our [LLC cost guide](/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-form-an-llc/) has state-by-state context.

### Does an LLC protect me from a lawsuit over a missed defect?

Not entirely. You can still be sued personally for your own professional errors. The LLC can help protect against other people's mistakes and business debts, while E&O insurance and a solid contract address your own work.

### Do I need E&O insurance if I have an LLC?

Yes, in practice. Some states require it for licensure, and many agents and clients expect it. An LLC does not replace professional liability coverage.

### Should a home inspector choose an LLC or an S-corp?

They aren't mutually exclusive. An LLC is a legal structure, and an S-corp is a tax election the LLC can make. Many inspectors start with default taxation and consider the S-corp election as profits grow. See our [LLC vs S-corp comparison](/blog/llc-vs-s-corp-which-is-better-for-taxes/).

### What is the best LLC formation service for home inspectors?

For most, ZenBusiness offers the best balance of price, speed, and compliance reminders. LegalZoom is a solid alternative, and Northwest Registered Agent is worth a look if privacy matters. Compare them in our [best LLC services](/best-llc-services/) hub.

### Can I form my LLC in a different state than where I inspect?

You can, but you'd generally need to register as a foreign LLC in your home state and pay both states' fees. For a local service business, forming in your home state is usually simpler. See our guide to [foreign LLC registration](/blog/foreign-llc-registration-doing-business-in-another-state/).

### Do I need to update my license after forming an LLC?

Often yes. Many state boards require you to report business entity information, and your insurance policy should name the LLC. Check with your licensing board before you file.
