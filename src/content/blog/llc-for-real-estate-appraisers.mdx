## What an LLC Does (and Doesn't) Do for an Appraiser

A limited liability company separates your business from your personal assets for most business obligations. If you want the fundamentals first, see [What Is an LLC?](/learn/what-is-an-llc/). For appraisers, the practical benefits fall into three buckets.

**What an LLC can protect:**
- Contract disputes with AMCs, clients, or vendors (office lease, software subscriptions, equipment financing).
- Debts of the business, such as a line of credit or unpaid vendors.
- Liability arising from the acts of *other people* in the business, such as a co-owner or an employee, to the extent the law allows.
- Claims connected to a trainee's work, in some circumstances, where you haven't personally been negligent.

**What an LLC generally cannot protect:**
- **Your own professional negligence.** Appraiser licenses and certifications are issued to individuals, not companies. If you personally sign an appraisal report, you are generally personally answerable for it. Courts and plaintiffs' attorneys routinely name the appraiser individually regardless of the entity.
- **Licensing or disciplinary consequences.** A state appraiser board complaint attaches to your license, not your LLC.
- **Your supervisory responsibilities.** If you supervise a trainee, the supervising appraiser carries regulatory responsibility for their work.
- **Anything you personally guarantee.** Lenders and landlords often ask for personal guarantees from small business owners.

In my experience, this is the most common misunderstanding among professionals who form an LLC. They assume the entity makes them bulletproof. It doesn't. What it does is wall off the *business* risks from your personal assets, while your insurance handles the *professional* risk. I'd always form the LLC and buy the right coverage, not one or the other.

## Why Appraisers Form an LLC in 2026

If an LLC doesn't shield you from your own errors, why bother? Here are the reasons I see most often.

### 1. Separation from non-appraisal risk

Many appraisers also own rental property, run a side business, or have a spouse with significant assets. An appraiser LLC keeps the business's contracts, equipment, and operating accounts apart from everything else you own.

### 2. Professionalism and AMC/lender relationships

Operating as "Smith Valuation Group, LLC" instead of under your personal name looks established to clients. Some lenders and AMCs contract with firms and ask for entity details, W-9s, and certificates of insurance in the entity's name. An LLC makes this cleaner.

### 3. A platform for growth

If you plan to add a trainee, hire an admin, or bring on another appraiser, having an entity in place makes the transition easier. See our guide to [member vs. manager-managed LLCs](/blog/llc-member-vs-manager-managed/) if you're considering partners.

### 4. Tax flexibility

An LLC lets you elect S-corporation taxation later, which may reduce self-employment tax once your income is high enough. More on that below.

## Check Your State's Rules First

Appraiser regulation is a patchwork. Federal law (Title XI of FIRREA) establishes the framework, and the [Appraisal Subcommittee (ASC)](https://www.asc.gov) oversees state appraiser regulatory programs. Day-to-day licensing, however, happens at the state level, and you must follow [USPAP](https://www.appraisalfoundation.org), the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice, set by the Appraisal Foundation.

Before forming an appraiser LLC, confirm these points with your state appraiser board:

- **Can your license be used by an entity?** In most states you can operate through an LLC while remaining individually licensed. A few states have specific rules on business names, advertising, or professional entity types.
- **Do you need a PLLC or professional corporation instead?** Some states require licensed professionals to use a professional entity type. Appraisal is not typically on those lists the way law, medicine, and accounting are, but rules vary and change. Verify before filing.
- **Business name rules.** Boards often have rules on how appraisers advertise and name their firms. Avoid names that imply a government affiliation or a certification you don't hold.
- **Record-keeping.** USPAP requires you to keep your workfile and records for a set period (generally at least five years after the report date, or two years after final disposition of any related judicial proceeding, whichever is longer). Your entity does not change that obligation.

Choosing the right state of formation matters too. Most single-owner appraisers are best off forming in the state where they work, because forming elsewhere and registering as a foreign LLC adds fees without much benefit. See [How to Start an LLC in Texas](/start-llc/texas/), [Florida](/start-llc/florida/), or [California](/start-llc/california/) for the three largest appraisal markets, or our [state selection guide](/blog/best-state-to-form-an-llc/).

## Appraiser E&O Insurance: The Piece an LLC Can't Replace

If you remember one section from this article, make it this one. **Appraiser E&O insurance** (errors and omissions, also called professional liability) is the coverage that responds when a client alleges your appraisal was negligent. The LLC and the policy do different jobs.

Key points:

- **Many AMCs and lenders require it.** Panels commonly ask for E&O coverage with limits such as $1 million per claim and $2 million aggregate, though requirements vary by client.
- **Claims-made policies.** Most E&O policies are "claims-made," meaning they cover claims reported while the policy is active. Dropping coverage when you retire or leave the business can leave you exposed. Ask about "tail" or extended reporting coverage.
- **Name the entity and the individual.** Make sure the policy covers both your LLC and you as the signing appraiser, plus any trainees.
- **Prior acts coverage.** When switching carriers, ask for retroactive date protection so older reports remain covered.
- **General liability is separate.** If clients visit your office or you carry equipment, a general liability policy is a different product from E&O.

Premiums vary widely by state, volume, property types, and claims history, so get several quotes. Appraiser associations and specialist brokers often offer group programs.

## How an Appraiser LLC Is Taxed

By default, a single-member LLC is a "disregarded entity" for federal income tax. You still report profit on Schedule C and pay self-employment tax (15.3% on net earnings, up to the Social Security wage base, with Medicare continuing above it). A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default. Our guide to [how LLCs are taxed](/blog/llc-taxes-how-are-llcs-taxed/) covers each option in detail.

### When the S-corp election makes sense

Once your net income is consistently strong, you can elect S-corporation tax treatment for the LLC. Under that structure, you pay yourself a reasonable salary (subject to payroll taxes), and remaining profit passes through as a distribution that isn't subject to self-employment tax. The IRS explains the rules on its [S corporations page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/s-corporations).

A simplified illustration, for discussion only:

| | Sole proprietor / default LLC | LLC taxed as S-corp |
|---|---|---|
| Net business profit | $120,000 | $120,000 |
| Salary paid to owner | n/a | $70,000 (example) |
| Subject to SE/payroll tax | ~$120,000 (subject to wage-base limits) | ~$70,000 |
| Additional costs | None | Payroll service, separate S-corp tax return (Form 1120-S), possible state fees |

The potential savings are real, but they're offset by payroll costs, bookkeeping, and the requirement to pay yourself a "reasonable" salary. The IRS looks closely at underpaid owner salaries. This is why LLC vs S-corp for appraisers is a question for your CPA, not a one-size-fits-all rule. Many advisers suggest the election starts to pencil out at roughly $60,000 to $80,000 or more in net profit, but your numbers and state taxes matter. Read our breakdown of [LLC vs S-Corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) and our guide to [self-employment tax for LLC owners](/blog/self-employment-tax-for-llc-owners/) before deciding.

### Other tax points

- **Quarterly estimated taxes.** Whether you're a default LLC or an S-corp owner, you generally need to make estimated payments. See our [LLC quarterly tax payments guide](/blog/llc-quarterly-tax-payments-guide/).
- **Deductions.** Common appraiser deductions include mileage or vehicle expenses, USPAP continuing education, state license and association fees, software (such as form and comparable-data subscriptions), camera and measuring equipment, E&O premiums, and home office costs where eligible. Keep documentation.
- **Qualified business income (QBI) deduction.** The Section 199A deduction may apply to pass-through income, but whether appraisal work is treated as a "specified service trade or business" with income limits is a question to raise with your tax professional.
- **State-level taxes.** Some states charge an annual LLC fee or franchise tax. California's $800 minimum is the best-known example; see our [California LLC franchise tax guide](/blog/california-llc-800-franchise-tax/).

## Sole Proprietor vs. LLC: Is It Worth It for You?

Here is a simple way to think about the real estate appraiser business structure decision.

**An LLC is usually worth it if you:**
- Have personal assets (home equity, savings, investment property) you'd like separated from business obligations.
- Plan to hire a trainee, assistant, or second appraiser.
- Contract with AMCs or lenders who prefer to deal with entities.
- Expect to revisit an S-corp election as income grows.

**You might wait if you:**
- Appraise part-time with minimal income and no employees.
- Work in a state where annual LLC costs are high relative to your profit.
- Are still deciding whether you'll stay in the profession.

Even then, an LLC doesn't replace E&O coverage, and the cost is modest compared with what a single claim can cost you. For a broader comparison, see [LLC vs Sole Proprietorship](/learn/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/) and our [LLC pros and cons](/blog/llc-pros-and-cons-complete-list/).

## How to Set Up an LLC for Real Estate Appraisers: Step by Step

1. **Confirm your state board's rules** on business entities and advertising.
2. **Choose a compliant name** and check availability with your Secretary of State.
3. **Appoint a registered agent.** You can serve as your own, but appraisers who work in the field all day often prefer a professional service. See [What Is a Registered Agent?](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/)
4. **File Articles of Organization** with your state and pay the filing fee.
5. **Write an operating agreement.** Even single-member LLCs benefit from one; it supports your liability protection. See our [operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/).
6. **Get an EIN** from the IRS, which is free. Our [step-by-step EIN guide](/blog/how-to-get-an-ein-for-your-llc-free-irs-method/) walks through it.
7. **Open a business bank account** and keep appraisal income and expenses separate. See [how to open an LLC bank account](/blog/llc-bank-account-how-to-open-one/).
8. **Update your license records, E&O policy, AMC profiles, and W-9s** to reflect the entity as your board and clients require.
9. **Stay current on annual reports** and any state fees.

Skipping step 7 is the mistake I see most. Mixing personal and business funds is one of the fastest ways to give a plaintiff an argument to "pierce the veil," so keep the accounts clean.

## Ongoing Compliance for Appraiser LLCs in 2026

Forming the LLC is the easy part. To keep the protections:

- **File annual or biennial reports** where your state requires them, and pay any franchise tax or annual fee.
- **Renew your license and CE** on your state's schedule. A lapsed license is a bigger problem than a late LLC report.
- **Keep your workfiles** per USPAP record-keeping rules.
- **Handle BOI reporting correctly.** In March 2025, FinCEN issued an interim final rule that removed the beneficial ownership information reporting requirement for U.S.-formed companies and U.S. owners. Rules in this area have shifted repeatedly, so check [FinCEN's BOI page](https://www.fincen.gov/boi) for the current status, and see our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/).
- **Update your entity if things change**, such as adding a member or changing your address. See [how to add a member to an LLC](/blog/how-to-add-a-member-to-an-llc/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do real estate appraisers need an LLC?

No law requires an appraiser to operate through an LLC. Many appraisers work as sole proprietors. An LLC adds a layer of separation for business debts and contracts, and it can open tax planning options, but it does not replace licensing, USPAP compliance, or appraiser E&O insurance.

### Does an LLC protect me from appraisal malpractice claims?

Generally no, not for your own negligence. Because you personally sign the report under your own license, you can be named in a claim regardless of the entity. An LLC may help limit exposure for the acts of others, such as a co-owner, but E&O coverage is your primary protection for professional errors.

### How much does it cost to form an LLC for real estate appraisers?

The main cost is your state's filing fee, which ranges from roughly $35 to $500 depending on the state, plus any annual fees. Formation services may add registered agent or package fees. See our breakdown of [how much an LLC costs](/learn/how-much-does-an-llc-cost/).

### Do I need E&O insurance if I have an LLC?

In practice, yes. Many AMCs and lenders require appraiser E&O insurance as a condition of working with them, and an LLC does not cover professional errors. Ask your carrier about limits, claims-made terms, and tail coverage.

### Should my appraiser LLC elect S-corp status?

It depends on your net profit, state taxes, and willingness to run payroll. S-corp status can reduce self-employment tax at higher income levels, but it adds costs and requires a reasonable salary. A CPA can run the numbers with your actual figures.

### Can a trainee appraiser work under my LLC?

Often yes, but state rules on supervision, trainee records, and who may employ or contract with trainees vary. Confirm requirements with your appraiser board, and make sure your E&O policy covers trainees.

### Can I form my LLC in a different state than where I appraise?

You can, but you'll typically need to register as a foreign LLC in the state where you actually work, paying fees in both. For most appraisers, forming in your home state is simpler and cheaper. See our guide to [foreign LLC registration](/blog/foreign-llc-registration-doing-business-in-another-state/).

### Do I need to file a BOI report for my appraiser LLC?

As of the March 2025 FinCEN rule, domestic companies and U.S. persons are exempt from BOI reporting, but this area has changed repeatedly. Check FinCEN's website or ask your attorney for current requirements before relying on this.
