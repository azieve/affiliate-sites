## What Is a BOI Report?

A **Beneficial Ownership Information (BOI) report** is a federal filing submitted to the **Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN)**, a bureau of the U.S. Department of the Treasury. It was established by the **Corporate Transparency Act (CTA)**, signed into law in 2021 and codified at 31 U.S.C. § 5336—you can review the full statutory text on [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov). The reporting requirement originally took effect on January 1, 2024.

The purpose of the BOI report is to combat money laundering, tax evasion, and financial crimes by requiring most small businesses to disclose who actually owns and controls them. The federal government wants to identify the real human beings behind LLCs and corporations—not just a company name in a state registry.

### What Information Does a BOI Report Include?

For each **beneficial owner** (anyone who owns 25% or more of the company, or who exercises substantial control over it), you must report:

- Full legal name
- Date of birth
- Current residential or business address
- A unique identifying number from a government-issued ID (driver's license, passport, etc.) plus an image of that document

If the company was formed on or after January 1, 2024, you must also report the same information for the **company applicant**—the person who filed the formation documents with the state.

### Key Characteristics of the BOI Report

- **Filed with:** FinCEN (U.S. Treasury bureau)
- **Who it discloses:** Beneficial owners and, for newer entities, company applicants
- **Frequency:** One-time initial filing, plus updates within 30 days of any changes to reported information
- **Cost:** Free to file directly through FinCEN's BOIR online system
- **Governing law:** Corporate Transparency Act (31 U.S.C. § 5336)

For a comprehensive walkthrough of the filing process, see our [BOI Report Guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/).

### The Evolving Legal Landscape in 2026

It's important to acknowledge that BOI reporting has been one of the most legally turbulent compliance areas in recent memory. Since the CTA took effect, it has faced multiple court challenges, injunctions, and shifting enforcement postures from the Treasury Department. In early 2025, FinCEN announced it would not enforce penalties against domestic reporting companies while rulemaking was ongoing—a significant development for millions of small business owners.

In 2026, the landscape continues to evolve. **We strongly recommend checking [FinCEN.gov](https://www.fincen.gov) directly for current enforcement status and deadlines** before assuming you're either required or exempt. This is precisely the kind of rapidly changing regulatory area where a brief consultation with a business attorney is worth more than any filing service can provide.

## What Is an Annual Report for an LLC?

An **annual report** (also called a biennial statement, periodic report, or statement of information, depending on the state) is a **state-level filing** submitted to your state's Secretary of State or equivalent agency. It has nothing to do with FinCEN or the federal government.

Every state that requires an annual report uses it to confirm that your LLC is still active and that basic information on file remains accurate. This typically includes:

- Your LLC's legal name and principal office address
- The name and address of your registered agent
- Names of members or managers (in some states)
- A brief description of business activity

That's it. Annual reports are administrative in nature—they're how the state keeps its business registry current and verifies that registered companies are still operating. There's nothing about ownership structure, personal identification, or financial reporting.

### Key Characteristics of an Annual Report

- **Filed with:** State Secretary of State (or equivalent state agency)
- **Who it covers:** The LLC entity itself (not individual owners)
- **Frequency:** Annual (or biennial in some states; a few states require no annual report)
- **Cost:** Varies by state — from $0 (New Mexico, Ohio, Arizona) to $800+ (California Franchise Tax Board)
- **Governing law:** State LLC statutes (varies by jurisdiction)

### Not Every State Requires One

This catches a lot of LLC owners by surprise. Ohio, New Mexico, and Arizona—among others—do not have a traditional annual report requirement. But that doesn't mean you have no recurring state obligations; these states often substitute franchise taxes, publication requirements, or other periodic filings. Always verify your specific state's rules rather than assuming no annual report means no annual obligations.

## The Key Differences That Actually Matter in Practice

In my experience working with small business owners on compliance, the BOI report vs annual report distinction trips people up for three consistent reasons:

**1. They're administered by completely different levels of government.**

Annual reports go to your state. BOI reports go to the federal government. These are entirely separate bureaucracies with separate systems, separate deadlines, and separate enforcement mechanisms. Filing one has zero effect on your obligations under the other—they don't communicate, they don't cross-reference, and compliance with one provides no shelter for non-compliance with the other.

**2. Annual reports are recurring; BOI reports are largely a one-time obligation with event-triggered updates.**

Most business owners think of compliance as something that happens once a year. The BOI report doesn't fit that mental model. It's primarily a one-time initial filing, with update obligations triggered by specific events—like an ownership change, an address update, or a new ID document—rather than by the calendar.

**3. The content is fundamentally different in kind.**

Annual reports are about the **business**—its location, its registered agent, its operational status. BOI reports are about the **people**—specifically, who owns and controls the business and where to find them. One is a business profile update; the other is a personal identity disclosure requirement with federal enforcement authority behind it.

## Deadlines and Penalties: What Happens If You Miss Either

### BOI Report Deadlines and Penalties

The original BOI filing framework established these timelines (always verify current status at FinCEN.gov):

- **Companies formed before January 1, 2024:** Initial deadline was January 1, 2025
- **Companies formed January 1, 2024 – January 1, 2025:** 90 days from date of formation to file
- **Companies formed after January 1, 2025:** 30 days from date of formation to file
- **Updates to existing information:** 30 days from the date of the change

For current deadlines and enforcement status, see our [BOI Report Deadline 2026](/blog/boi-report-deadline-2026/) guide, and always confirm at FinCEN.gov given the ongoing regulatory flux.

**BOI Penalties:** Under the CTA, willful non-filers face civil penalties of up to **$591 per day** (inflation-adjusted from the original $500 statutory figure) and criminal penalties of up to $10,000 plus up to two years in prison. For the full breakdown of how these penalties are calculated and assessed, see our [BOI Late Filing Penalty](/blog/boi-report-penalties-for-late-filing/) guide.

### Annual Report Deadlines and Penalties

Annual report deadlines vary significantly by state. The main structures are:

- **Anniversary-based:** Due on the anniversary of your LLC's formation (common in Delaware and New York)
- **Calendar-based:** Fixed date each year regardless of formation date (Florida: April 1; California: March 1; Georgia: April 15)
- **Fiscal year-based:** Tied to your LLC's fiscal year end

**Annual Report Penalties** are generally less severe than BOI penalties but still consequential, and they escalate:

1. **Late fee:** Typically $25–$100 tacked on top of the standard filing fee
2. **Loss of good standing:** Your LLC can no longer certify it is in good standing—which creates problems for bank accounts, contracts, loans, and professional licenses
3. **Administrative dissolution:** If ignored long enough, the state will dissolve your LLC involuntarily, stripping you of liability protection

## Who Has to File Each Report?

### BOI Report: Who Files

Most LLCs and corporations formed in the U.S.—as well as foreign entities registered to do business here—are **reporting companies** under the CTA. However, there are **23 defined exempt categories**, including:

- **Large operating companies:** 20+ full-time U.S. employees, $5M+ in U.S.-source gross receipts, physical U.S. office
- **SEC-reporting companies:** Publicly traded entities already subject to securities disclosure requirements
- **Regulated financial entities:** Banks, credit unions, broker-dealers, insurance companies, and similar heavily regulated businesses
- **Inactive entities:** Companies meeting specific criteria for dormancy (formed before 2020, holding no assets, no foreign ownership, etc.)

The overwhelming majority of small LLCs do **not** qualify for these exemptions. If you're uncertain, review our [Who Is Exempt from BOI Reporting](/blog/who-is-exempt-from-boi-reporting/) guide or consult a business attorney before assuming you're off the hook.

### Annual Report: Who Files

If your state requires an annual report, your LLC files it—full stop. Exemptions are rare and typically limited to officially dissolved entities, nonprofits with specific state designations, or companies that have converted to another legal form. If you're still an active LLC in a state with an annual report requirement, you file.

## Do You Need to File Both? (Almost Certainly Yes)

Here's the direct answer most people searching this question are looking for: **yes, for the vast majority of LLCs, both filings are required—and they're completely independent of each other.**

Filing your annual report with the state does not satisfy your federal BOI obligation. Filing your BOI report with FinCEN does not replace your state's annual report. These are parallel compliance tracks that run simultaneously with no overlap. Missing one while complying with the other still means you are non-compliant on the missed filing—with all the associated consequences.

I've seen too many business owners assume that because they filed "everything" with their formation service, their compliance was handled. Formation is not the same as ongoing compliance, and neither a BOI report nor an annual report is filed at the time of formation (BOI reports are due within 30–90 days of formation; annual reports usually start in the year following formation).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the main difference between a BOI report and an annual report?

The BOI report is a **federal filing** submitted to FinCEN (U.S. Treasury) that discloses who owns and controls your business. An annual report is a **state filing** submitted to your Secretary of State that confirms your LLC is active and keeps basic business information current. They serve entirely different purposes, go to entirely different agencies, and missing one has no effect on your obligations under the other.

### Do I have to file both a BOI report and an annual report?

In most cases, yes. These are separate, independent obligations that run on parallel tracks. Filing one does not satisfy or replace the other. Most LLCs must comply with both unless they qualify for specific exemptions (23 federal exemptions exist for the BOI report; state exemptions for annual reports are minimal).

### Is the BOI report free to file?

Yes. Filing directly through FinCEN's BOIR system costs nothing. If you use a third-party filing service, they may charge a convenience or service fee—typically $49–$200 depending on the provider—but the underlying government fee is $0.

### What happens if I miss my BOI report filing?

Under the Corporate Transparency Act, willful non-compliance can result in civil penalties of up to $591 per day and criminal penalties of up to $10,000 plus two years in prison. However, enforcement status has been subject to change in 2026—verify current requirements at FinCEN.gov before drawing conclusions. See our full [BOI Late Filing Penalty](/blog/boi-report-penalties-for-late-filing/) breakdown for details.

### What happens if I miss my annual report?

Consequences escalate in stages: a late fee (typically $25–$100) is assessed first, then your LLC loses good standing status, and eventually the state will involuntarily dissolve your LLC if the lapse continues long enough. Dissolution strips you of liability protection and can disrupt banking relationships, contracts, and licensing.

### How often do I need to file a BOI report compared to an annual report?

The BOI report is a one-time initial filing, plus updates required within 30 days of changes to beneficial owner information (address, name, ID document, ownership percentage). Annual reports are recurring—usually once per year, though some states require biennial filings and a handful require no annual report at all.

### Does every state require an annual report for LLCs?

No. States like Ohio, New Mexico, Arizona, and Missouri have eliminated the traditional annual report requirement. However, these states often substitute other periodic obligations such as franchise taxes or publication requirements. Confirm your specific state's requirements before assuming you have no annual filing obligations.
