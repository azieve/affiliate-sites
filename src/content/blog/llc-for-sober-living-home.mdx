Running a sober living home is a mission-driven business, but it is still a business. You sign leases, collect rent, hire house managers, and take on real liability for the people living under your roof. An LLC for a sober living home is the most common way operators separate that risk from their personal finances. It also gives the operation a clean legal and tax identity.

The formation itself is cheap and quick. A service like [ZenBusiness](/go/zenbusiness/) will file your Articles of Organization for $0 plus your state's filing fee, with paid tiers that add an operating agreement template, EIN assistance, and compliance reminders. The harder part is structuring the LLC correctly for a residential recovery business. This guide covers that, along with the licensing, insurance, and tax points that matter in 2026.

## Why an LLC for a Sober Living Home Makes Sense

A sober living home faces a different risk profile from an ordinary rental. You are housing people in early recovery, often several to a house, and you are responsible for house rules, safety, and (in some states) compliance with certification standards.

Typical exposure includes:

- **Premises liability.** Slip-and-fall injuries, fire, carbon monoxide, and habitability claims.
- **Resident disputes.** Wrongful eviction or discharge claims, property loss, and conflicts between residents.
- **Employment claims.** Wage disputes from house managers, or misclassification of peer staff.
- **Neighbor and zoning conflicts.** Complaints, code enforcement, and occasionally litigation over local rules.
- **Contract liability.** Leases, vendor agreements, and loans you sign for the business.

An LLC limits your personal liability for the business's debts and most business-related claims. If a creditor sues the company, they generally reach company assets, not your house or personal savings.

In my experience, new operators underestimate how fast a "side project" house becomes a real company. Once you have two or three homes, a payroll, and a few dozen residents cycling through a year, operating as a sole proprietor leaves you personally on the hook for everything. See our guide to [LLC vs sole proprietorship](/learn/llc-vs-sole-proprietorship/) for the baseline comparison.

An LLC is not a force field. Courts can "pierce the veil" if you commingle funds, skip formalities, or undercapitalize the business. It also does not replace insurance. Treat the LLC as one layer of protection, not the only one.

## Structuring Your Sober Living Home Business: One LLC or Two?

This is where the advice differs from a generic LLC article. If you own the real estate, consider a **two-entity structure**:

1. **A property-holding LLC** that owns the building and leases it to the operator.
2. **An operating LLC** that runs the sober living home business: collects resident fees, employs staff, holds insurance, and pays rent to the property LLC.

Why bother? If a resident sues the operating company, the building sits in a separate entity and is harder to reach. The reverse also holds: a mortgage or lien problem on one property doesn't automatically drag down your operations.

**A worked example.** Say you buy a 6-bed house and want to open it as a recovery residence. You form "Harbor Path Properties LLC" to hold the title and "Harbor Path Recovery LLC" to operate. The operating LLC signs a written lease with the property LLC at market rent, charges residents $150 to $250 per week, and carries its own liability policy.

Two cautions from the legal side:

- **Don't move financed property into an LLC without checking your loan.** Most mortgages contain due-on-sale clauses. Federal law (the Garn-St Germain Act) has exceptions, but they don't clearly cover transfers to an LLC. Talk to your lender and an attorney first.
- **Keep the entities genuinely separate.** Separate bank accounts, written leases between them, and no paying property expenses from the operating account without documentation.

If you rent the house rather than own it, a single operating LLC is usually enough. Our [LLC for rental property investors](/blog/llc-for-rental-property-investors/) guide covers the property-holding side in more depth.

## Licensing and Regulation: Know Your State Before You Form

Forming the LLC does not authorize you to operate a sober living home. Regulation varies widely, which is why we can't give one answer for every state.

**Housing vs. treatment.** In many states the key distinction is whether you provide only housing and peer support, or also clinical services such as counseling, medication management, or treatment. Once you offer treatment, state licensing for behavioral health or substance use treatment facilities often applies. Many operators deliberately stay on the housing side, but you must actually stay there. Regulators look at what you do, not what your LLC name says.

**Voluntary and mandatory certification.** Some states have certification programs for recovery residences, and many follow standards from the [National Alliance for Recovery Residences (NARR)](https://narronline.org/). Florida, for example, runs a certification framework through state-recognized credentialing entities, and referrals from licensed treatment providers can be tied to certification. Other states have no specific sober living home license at all. Always check your state's current rules for a recovery residence, because they change.

**Federal fair housing law.** People in recovery from substance use disorders are generally protected under the Fair Housing Act and the Americans with Disabilities Act, which can limit how local governments apply zoning to group homes. The [U.S. Department of Justice and HUD joint statement on group homes](https://www.justice.gov/crt/fair-housing-act-1) is a good starting point. The protections have limits, and active illegal drug use is not protected, so get local legal advice before you rely on them in a zoning dispute.

**Referral and marketing rules.** Some states restrict or prohibit payments for resident referrals (often called patient brokering). Treat any referral arrangement with a treatment center as something to run past an attorney first.

**Local requirements.** Expect to deal with business licenses, occupancy limits, fire and safety inspections, and possibly a zoning review or special-use permit.

Before filing, make a short checklist:

- Does my state license, certify, or define sober living homes or recovery residences?
- Will I provide any clinical or treatment services?
- What do my city and county say about occupancy and group homes?
- Do any referral sources I plan to use have rules about certified residences?

## How to Form Your LLC Step by Step

The mechanics are the same as for any business. Here is the efficient order.

**1. Choose your state.** For a home-based business tied to a physical property, form the LLC in the state where the home operates. Forming in Delaware or Wyoming and then registering as a foreign LLC in your home state adds fees without much benefit for a local operation. Our [Texas](/start-llc/texas/), [Florida](/start-llc/florida/), and [California](/start-llc/california/) guides give state-specific fees and rules.

**2. Pick a name.** Check availability with your Secretary of State. Many operators choose a name that reflects the mission, but avoid anything implying clinical treatment or state licensure you don't hold.

**3. Appoint a registered agent.** This is the person or company that receives legal notices. Many operators use a commercial service rather than listing a home address, and our [registered agent guide](/learn/what-is-a-registered-agent/) explains the tradeoffs.

**4. File Articles of Organization.** State filing fees range from roughly $35 to $500 depending on where you form. Your filing service fee is on top of that.

**5. Write an operating agreement.** Even a single-member LLC should have one. It documents ownership, management, and how decisions are made, and it supports your liability protection. See our [LLC operating agreement guide](/learn/llc-operating-agreement/).

**6. Get an EIN.** Free through the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/apply-for-an-employer-identification-number-ein-online). You need it to open a business bank account and to hire staff.

**7. Open a dedicated business bank account.** Never mix resident fees with personal funds. This is the single most common way operators weaken their liability protection.

**8. Handle licenses and insurance** (covered below) before you accept your first resident.

Regarding beneficial ownership reporting: under FinCEN's rules as revised in 2025, domestic LLCs are currently exempt from BOI reporting. Check our [BOI report guide](/learn/boi-report-guide/) for the latest status, since this area has changed repeatedly.

## Insurance: The Part You Cannot Skip

An LLC protects your personal assets from business liabilities, but insurance pays the claims. Many standard landlord policies exclude or limit coverage for group living and recovery housing, so tell the insurer exactly what you operate.

Coverage to discuss with a broker who knows residential recovery:

- **General liability** for injuries on the premises.
- **Property insurance** appropriate to the use of the building.
- **Professional liability**, if you provide any peer-support or case-management services.
- **Workers' compensation** once you have employees (required in most states).
- **Abuse and molestation or resident-related coverage**, where available.
- **Umbrella liability** for extra limits above your base policies.

Cost varies enormously by state, location, and bed count, so get multiple quotes. A policy that is cheap because it quietly excludes your actual operation is worse than no policy, because it creates false confidence. Ask directly whether your use is covered.

## Taxes for a Sober Living Home LLC

A single-member LLC is a "disregarded entity" by default, so profits flow to your personal return on Schedule C (or Schedule E for pure rental income). A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership. For the IRS framework, see [IRS guidance on LLCs](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc).

Points that matter for this business:

- **Rental vs. services income.** Income from simply renting rooms is treated differently from income that includes substantial services such as meals, supervision, or programming. That can affect whether the income is reported as rental income or business income, and whether self-employment tax applies. This is a place where a CPA earns their fee.
- **Self-employment tax.** If your income is treated as business income, expect roughly 15.3% self-employment tax on net earnings (up to the Social Security wage base), plus income tax.
- **S-corp election.** Once net profit is steady and meaningful, an S-corp election can reduce self-employment tax, but it requires running payroll and paying yourself a reasonable salary. Our [LLC vs S-Corp](/learn/llc-vs-s-corp/) guide explains when it starts to pay off.
- **Depreciation.** Owners of the building can depreciate the property, which can offset income. Rules are technical, so work with a tax professional.
- **Payroll.** House managers are usually employees, not independent contractors. Misclassification is a frequent audit trigger.
- **Sales and lodging taxes.** Some localities treat long-term room rentals differently from short-term ones. Check locally.
- **Nonprofit alternative.** Some recovery residences operate as nonprofits. That is a different path with its own rules, and an LLC cannot simply claim tax-exempt status.

I've seen operators lose a large part of their first-year profit to avoidable tax surprises because they never separated their rent income from service income. Meet with a CPA before your first resident moves in, not after your first tax bill.

## Running the Business Safely: Operations That Protect the LLC

The LLC only protects you if you run it like a real company.

- **Written resident agreements.** Spell out house rules, fees, discharge procedures, and your drug-testing policy. Have an attorney review them for your state's landlord-tenant and eviction rules, since removing a resident can carry legal process requirements that surprise new operators.
- **Privacy.** If you handle any health information, be careful about confidentiality. Federal rules protecting substance use disorder records (42 CFR Part 2) can apply in some settings. Ask counsel what applies to you.
- **Separate finances.** Pay yourself through documented owner draws or payroll, never by casually dipping into the business account.
- **Annual filings.** File your state's annual report on time and keep the registered agent current.
- **Safety documentation.** Keep records of fire inspections, smoke detector checks, and incident reports.
- **Sign contracts in the LLC's name.** Sign as "Harbor Path Recovery LLC, by [Your Name], Manager," not as yourself. Lenders may still ask for a personal guarantee, which bypasses the LLC shield for that debt.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need an LLC to open a sober living home?

No law requires one, but it is strongly recommended. Without an entity, your personal assets are exposed to business debts and claims. An LLC for a sober living home is the most common starting structure because it is inexpensive, flexible, and simple to maintain.

### How much does it cost to start an LLC for a sober living home?

The state filing fee is typically $35 to $500, depending on the state. Adding a formation service costs $0 to roughly $350 per year for the plans compared above. Budget separately for insurance, a registered agent if you don't use the service's, and any local licensing or certification fees. Our [LLC cost guide](/learn/how-much-does-an-llc-cost/) breaks the numbers down.

### Do I need a sober living home license?

It depends on your state and on what you offer. Some states have specific certification or licensing for recovery residences. Others regulate only operations that provide treatment. Check with your state health or behavioral health agency and a local attorney before accepting residents.

### Should I put the property and the operation in the same LLC?

Usually separate entities are safer: one LLC owns the real estate and a second one operates the sober living home business. If you only lease the house, one operating LLC is generally enough. Confirm any mortgage restrictions before transferring a financed property.

### Can an LLC for a sober living home be a nonprofit?

An LLC is a for-profit legal form by default. A nonprofit recovery residence is typically organized as a nonprofit corporation and may seek 501(c)(3) status. In some cases an LLC can be owned by a nonprofit. See [LLC vs 501(c)(3)](/blog/llc-vs-501-c-3/) for the differences.

### What happens if I don't keep my LLC in good standing?

Missing annual reports or fees can lead to administrative dissolution, and your liability protection and ability to operate may be affected. See what happens if [you don't renew your LLC](/blog/what-happens-if-you-don-t-renew-your-llc/).

### Will my LLC protect me from every lawsuit?

No. It generally shields personal assets from business liabilities, but not from your own negligence or personal guarantees, and courts can disregard it if you mix personal and business funds. That is why insurance and clean record-keeping matter.
